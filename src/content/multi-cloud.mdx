<TLDR>
  One cluster in one cloud eventually isn’t enough. Big deployments spread thousands of GPUs across many regions and
  cloud providers, and the hard part is making all of it act like **one pool**: get GPUs wherever you can, send each
  user to a nearby region, expect and survive hardware failures and outages, and keep data where the law says it must
  stay.
</TLDR>

## Why go beyond one cluster?

[Autoscaling](/learn/autoscaling) within a single cluster works up to a certain point. But high-volume deployments
serving a global user base need **thousands of GPUs distributed around the world**, and no single cluster, region, or
provider can supply that reliably.

The easy way to use several clouds is to build multi-cloud inference as a **collection of siloed compute** across
different providers: a separate deployment in each cloud, each managed on its own. The book’s criticism: in that setup
there’s no way to use capacity across clouds **fluidly**, and moving workloads between clouds is a tedious, error-prone,
manual process. Spare GPUs in one silo can’t help an overloaded silo next door.

**True multi-cloud inference** requires building a **multi-region, multi-provider bin-packing tool** that treats
distinct pools of compute as **fungible** (interchangeable) with each other.

<Analogy title="Think of it like Tetris across data centers">
  Bin packing is fitting pieces of different sizes into the available space with as little waste as possible. Here, the
  pieces are model replicas (each needing a certain number and type of GPU), and the space is every GPU you have access
  to, in every region and every cloud. A siloed setup plays a separate game of Tetris on each board; true multi-cloud
  plays one game across all of them.
</Analogy>

<Worked title="What fungible capacity buys you">

1. You run three clusters. Free GPUs right now: **cluster A (cloud 1): 6**, **cluster B (cloud 2): 10**,
   **cluster C (cloud 3): 3**. That’s 19 free GPUs in total.
2. Traffic for a model surges in cluster A’s region, and it needs **one more replica of 8 GPUs**.
3. **Siloed:** cluster A only has 6 free GPUs, so the replica can’t be placed there. The other clusters belong to
   separate, independently managed deployments, so the 19 free GPUs don’t help. Users queue.
4. **Fungible:** the global scheduler sees that cluster B has 10 free GPUs, places the replica there, and routes the
   overflow traffic to it (at the cost of a little extra network distance). The spike is absorbed.

</Worked>

Just as Kubernetes takes a global view within a single cluster, multi-cloud capacity management must take a **global
view across all clusters**, enabling **self-healing** (automatically replacing what fails) and **global scheduling**
(placing work wherever capacity exists).

Doing it properly unlocks four things:

| Benefit | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Capacity** | Pool GPUs from multiple providers for greater and more flexible access |
| **Redundancy** | Split inference across providers, so one provider’s outage doesn’t take you down |
| **Latency** | Run inference close to your end users to cut network latency |
| **Compliance** | Run inference in line with data sovereignty and other regulatory requirements |

## Control plane and workload planes

Scaling from one cluster in one cloud to many clusters in many clouds requires a **new coordination layer**. It uses the
same idea as inside a Kubernetes cluster, one level up (the book’s Figure 7.11 draws the control plane above many
workload planes spread across regions):

- **Control plane**: handles **model deployment** and **global scaling decisions**, and receives **real-time event
  streams** from everywhere.
- **Workload planes**: each one handles **direct inference traffic** and **in-cluster scaling decisions**, and reports
  its utilization and demand back up to the control plane.

This separation of responsibilities means **individual workload planes can serve traffic independently.** If something
happens to the control plane, or to any given workload plane, the other workloads should be unaffected.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **What keeps working when the control plane is down?** Everything on the request path: each workload plane keeps
  serving inference, and its in-cluster autoscaling keeps adding and removing replicas within its own cluster. What
  pauses are the *global* actions: deploying new models or versions, and moving capacity between clusters. The design
  goal is that a control plane outage delays changes but never drops live traffic.
</Note>

## Getting GPUs

A good number of companies are in the business of providing access to GPUs. The book groups them into three major
types:

- **Hyperscalers**: large, general-purpose cloud providers like **AWS** or **GCP**.
- **Neoclouds**: GPU-focused clouds like **CoreWeave** or **Nebius**.
- **Resellers**: secondary markets like **SF Compute Company**.

Players vary in **capacity, availability, and reliability**. You generally pay a **premium for hyperscalers**, and across
all providers there’s a trade-off between cost and factors like **uptime SLAs, support, regional availability, instance
configuration, and cluster sizes**. (Contract terms can include things like how quickly nodes start, which feeds directly
into cold start times.)

**The first challenge is securing capacity at all.** It’s often difficult to get the GPUs you need, especially the
latest hardware. **Large clusters** are also hard to get, with relatively few players offering blocks of hundreds of
nodes. Many providers allocate most of their in-demand GPUs to their **largest customers on long-term reservations**.
So you may need to work across **multiple providers** just to get enough GPUs in the right regions: multi-cloud is often
a necessity before it’s a strategy.

Cloud GPUs can be procured three ways:

| Type | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Reserved** | Blocks of hundreds or thousands of GPUs reserved for months or years at discounted rates | Cheapest per hour, but you pay whether or not you use them |
| **On-demand** | Individual instances as needed, up to a quota | Flexible, but a relatively high per-hour cost |
| **Spot** | Discounted on-demand instances that can be **pre-empted** at an agreed notice period, often minutes | Cheap, but can be taken back at short notice |

Large-scale inference generally uses a **blend**: a **baseline of low-cost reserved instances**, plus a mix of
**on-demand and spot** for handling peaks. These GPUs are spread across multiple clusters worldwide for proximity to end
users.

<Worked title="Why a blend beats any single option">

Illustrative prices only: reserved **$2.00**, on-demand **$4.00**, spot **$2.50** per GPU-hour. Your traffic needs
**60 GPUs** around the clock and **100 GPUs** for about **8 peak hours** a day.

1. **All reserved at peak size:** 100 GPUs × 24 h × $2.00 = **$4,800/day**, but 40 of those GPUs sit idle for 16 hours
   a day.
2. **All on-demand, scaled to traffic:** (60 × 24 + 40 × 8) GPU-hours × $4.00 = (1,440 + 320) × $4.00 =
   **$7,040/day**. Flexible, but you pay the high rate on your steady baseline.
3. **Blend:** 60 reserved around the clock + 40 on-demand for the peak: 1,440 × $2.00 + 320 × $4.00 =
   $2,880 + $1,280 = **$4,160/day**. Using spot for part of the peak lowers it further.
4. The lesson: put the steady **baseline** on the cheapest committed capacity, and cover the **variable** part with
   flexible capacity.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Handling spot pre-emption.** Because spot instances can be reclaimed with minutes of notice, treat them as
  disposable: when a pre-emption notice arrives, stop sending the replica new requests, let in-flight requests finish or
  retry elsewhere, and let the autoscaler replace the capacity. That’s also why spot belongs in the peak layer, not the
  baseline: losing it should mean slightly less headroom, not an outage.
</Note>

## Sending each user somewhere close

Successful AI applications have users all over the world. Just as a single cluster has a load balancer to make sure every
GPU receives the right amount of traffic, a multi-cluster system needs a **global load balancer**. It balances two goals:

- You don’t want a user’s request **sitting in a queue** when there’s spare capacity elsewhere.
- But you also don’t want to make a habit of sending a request **from Singapore to a server in San Francisco**.

The book’s rule of thumb: it takes about **5 milliseconds for a request to pass through a time zone**. New York to San
Francisco spans three time zones, so about **15 ms one way**. Given how small latency budgets are, it’s important to run
workloads as close to end users as possible.

<Worked title="Distance hurts more than once">

1. New York to San Francisco: about **15 ms one way**, so **30 ms** for a round trip.
2. A request isn’t one trip. Opening a fresh secure connection first takes round trips for the connection setup and
   encryption handshake before the request itself is sent (the [shipping lesson](/learn/shipping) covers this).
   Assume two round trips of setup: 2 × 30 = **60 ms**.
3. Then the request and the first streamed token: another round trip, **30 ms**.
4. Roughly **90 ms** of pure distance before counting any inference, on a fresh connection. Serving the same user from a
   nearby region, a few milliseconds away, cuts that to a handful of milliseconds.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Where the rule of thumb comes from.** Light in optical fiber travels at roughly two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum,
  about 200 km per millisecond, and real cable routes are longer than straight lines. That sets a hard physical floor:
  no amount of engineering makes a transpacific round trip fast. Long ocean routes typically come in slower than the
  time-zone rule would suggest, which is one more reason to serve users from a region near them.
</Note>

## Building for reliability

GPUs are **infamous for their high failure rate in production.** Every engineer who has done a large training run knows
they need to plan for the eventuality that hardware will fail.

The book’s example comes from the **Llama 3** paper (Grattafiori and colleagues): while running **16,000 GPUs for 54
days**, the Llama team experienced **419 unexpected interruptions**, primarily due to hardware failure. That works out to
approximately **one failure per 50,000 GPU-hours**.

<Worked title="From the Llama 3 numbers to your fleet">

1. Total GPU-hours in the Llama 3 run: 16,000 GPUs × 54 days × 24 hours = **20,736,000 GPU-hours**.
2. Divide by the interruptions: 20,736,000 ÷ 419 ≈ **49,500 GPU-hours per failure**, which rounds to the book’s one per
   50,000.
3. One inference node of 8 GPUs, running for a year: 8 × 8,760 hours = **70,080 GPU-hours**, already more than 50,000.
   Expect roughly **1.4 failures per node per year**.
4. A fleet of **1,000 GPUs** for a year: 8,760,000 GPU-hours ÷ 50,000 ≈ **175 failures a year**, about **one every two
   days**.

</Worked>

The book also includes a chart from the Llama 3 paper (Figure 7.12) breaking these interruptions down by **root cause**.
The chart itself isn’t reproduced here. *Reconstruction from the paper:* the large majority of unexpected
interruptions, roughly three-quarters, were attributed to confirmed or suspected **hardware** problems, with **faulty
GPUs** the single largest category and **GPU memory (HBM)** failures next. Software bugs, network switches and cables,
and host maintenance made up much smaller slices. See the original paper for the exact breakdown.

Some practical lessons:

- **GPU health is a node-level concern.** When a single GPU fails, other GPUs on the node often fail next or need to be
  taken offline for maintenance anyway. So watch for failures **proactively**, **cordon** the affected nodes, and
  **cycle pods** onto healthy machines to keep each cluster healthy.
- **GPUs aren’t the only thing that breaks.** Cloud providers have **scheduled maintenance** and their own
  **unscheduled downtime**. Every layer of infrastructure must be reinforced to provide high reliability.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Cordoning and draining, in Kubernetes terms.** *Cordoning* a node marks it unschedulable, so no new pods land on
  it. *Draining* then moves the pods already running there onto other nodes, after which the node can be repaired or
  replaced. For GPUs, health signals include the driver’s error reports (NVIDIA calls these XID errors) and memory
  error counters; a node that starts reporting them is a candidate for cordoning before it fails outright mid-request.
</Note>

Multi-cloud inference brings two approaches to high reliability:

- **Active-active**: multiple regions or clusters **actively serve live traffic at the same time**. If any plane fails,
  traffic seamlessly continues on the others.
- **Active-passive**: a **hot standby** cluster or region is kept ready but idle. If the active plane fails, traffic is
  **cut over** to the passive plane.

| | Active-active | Active-passive |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal operation** | All regions serve users | One region serves; the standby waits |
| **When a region fails** | Remaining regions absorb its traffic immediately | Traffic is cut over to the standby |
| **Cost** | Every region is doing useful work, but each needs headroom to absorb a failed neighbor | You pay for a standby that isn’t serving anyone |
| **Bonus** | Users are always served from a nearby region | Simpler to reason about |

When individual clusters, regions, or cloud providers go down, **seamlessly failing over** to another workload plane
keeps reliability high and latency low.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Counting nines.** Availability is usually quoted in “nines”: 99% allows about **3.65 days** of downtime a year,
  99.9% about **8.8 hours**, and 99.99% about **53 minutes**. (The book’s preface contrasts popular closed-model APIs at
  roughly two nines with dedicated deployments that can reach four nines or better.) Redundancy is how you add nines. If
  two regions each fail independently 0.1% of the time, both are down at once only 0.1% × 0.1% = 0.0001% of the time in
  theory. In practice failures are often correlated (a shared provider, a bad deployment pushed everywhere), and
  failover isn’t instant, so the real gain is smaller, but still large.
</Note>

## Security and compliance

Cloud infrastructure has been a hot topic for security and compliance departments for more than twenty years, and for AI
models to power mission-critical applications, inference must be both secure and compliant. The conversations generally
center on three areas:

- **User data**: security and compliance teams want to ensure all data, including user inputs and model outputs, is
  protected.
- **Model weights**: for companies with fine-tuned or proprietary models, the weights are an invaluable **trade secret**.
- **Infrastructure**: GPUs themselves, and access to intelligence, are both **targets for abuse**.

<Note kind="tip">
  One of the easiest decisions you can make to improve security is to **simply not store user inputs or model
  outputs.** That may not be possible (you might have logging requirements, or user agreements to retain usage data for
  future model training), but if you don’t need to retain user data, not keeping it **reduces your attack surface**:
  data you never stored can’t be stolen.
</Note>

Otherwise, the book notes that securing AI inference workloads is similar to securing any other containerized workload.
The gold standard remains **data encryption, container security, network and access controls, and workload isolation**,
all validated by extensive **third-party penetration testing** (independent experts attempting to break in).

<Note kind="beyond">
  **What those controls look like for inference.** *Encryption* means TLS for data in transit and encrypted disks for
  anything at rest, including cached weights. *Access controls* decide who and what can pull proprietary weights or
  change a deployment, which also means never publishing images that contain private weights to a public registry.
  *Workload isolation* keeps one customer’s or model’s containers from reaching another’s. For infrastructure abuse,
  authentication and per-key rate limits stop stolen credentials from turning into a free GPU farm.
</Note>

Increasingly, inference engineers support applications in **regulated industries** and **compliance-heavy regions**,
and multi-cloud helps in two ways:

- **Compliance flows through your providers.** For your application to comply with a certification like **SOC 2
  Type II** or a regulation like **HIPAA**, your providers generally must be compliant too. Being able to move workloads
  onto compliant providers is useful.
- **Data residency.** Certain industries and countries require that user data from their country **cannot be processed
  on servers in a different country**. Running one model across multiple regions solves this. The book’s example: one
  cluster in a provider near **Toronto** and another near **New York** lets you keep Canadian data in Canada and American
  data in the United States, with minimal latency overhead for users across the region.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **The two names in brief.** *SOC 2 Type II* is an independent audit report showing that a company’s security controls
  actually operated effectively over a period of time (typically several months), not just that they exist on paper.
  *HIPAA* is the U.S. law governing health information; companies handling that data for a healthcare customer
  generally sign an agreement (a business associate agreement) committing to protect it, and they need the same from
  the providers underneath them.
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- Siloed clouds can’t share capacity. True multi-cloud uses a **bin-packing** scheduler that treats every GPU pool as
  **fungible**, with self-healing and global scheduling.
- It unlocks **capacity, redundancy, latency, and compliance**.
- A global **control plane** handles deployments and global scaling from real-time event streams. **Workload planes**
  serve traffic, scale within their cluster, report back, and keep running if anything else fails.
- GPUs come from **hyperscalers** (premium), **neoclouds**, and **resellers**. Buy a **reserved** baseline and cover
  peaks with **on-demand** and **spot**. Securing capacity at all is the first challenge.
- A **global load balancer** balances spare capacity against distance: about **5 ms per time zone** one way, paid again
  on every round trip.
- Expect failures: about **1 per 50,000 GPU-hours** (Llama 3), so ~1.4 per 8-GPU node per year. Cordon bad nodes and
  cycle pods; use **active-active** or **active-passive** failover.
- Protect **user data, model weights, and infrastructure**. Don’t store data you don’t need. Use compliant providers and
  per-country regions for **SOC 2, HIPAA, and data residency**.
</KeyPoints>
