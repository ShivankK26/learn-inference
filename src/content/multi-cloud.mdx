<TLDR>
  One cluster in one cloud eventually isn't enough. Big deployments spread thousands of GPUs across many regions and
  cloud providers, and the hard part is making all of it act like **one pool**: get GPUs wherever you can, send each
  user to a nearby region, survive hardware failures and outages, and keep data where the law says it must stay.
</TLDR>

## Why go beyond one cluster?

Autoscaling inside one cluster works up to a point. But a high-volume product with users around the world needs
**thousands of GPUs, spread across the globe**.

The easy way to use several clouds is to run separate, **siloed** deployments in each. The problem is that silos can't
help each other: you can't smoothly use spare GPUs in one cloud when another is overloaded, and moving workloads
between clouds is slow and error-prone.

**True multi-cloud** needs a **bin-packing** system that spans regions and providers and treats all those separate pools
of GPUs as **interchangeable**.

<Analogy title="Think of it like Tetris across data centers">
  Bin packing is fitting pieces of different sizes into the available space with as little waste as possible. Here,
  the pieces are model replicas and the space is every GPU you have access to, in every region and every cloud.
</Analogy>

Just as Kubernetes has a global view of *one* cluster, multi-cloud capacity management needs a global view of *all of
them*, so it can **heal itself** (replace what fails) and **schedule globally**.

Doing it properly unlocks four things:

| Benefit | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| **Capacity** | Pool GPUs from several providers, for more and more flexible access |
| **Redundancy** | Spread inference across providers, so one outage doesn't take you down |
| **Latency** | Run models close to users, cutting network delay |
| **Compliance** | Keep processing in the right places for data-sovereignty and other regulations |

## Control plane and workload planes

Going from one cluster to many needs a new coordination layer, built from the same idea you saw inside a cluster, but
one level up:

- **Control plane**: handles deploying models and making **global** scaling decisions. It takes in a live stream of
  events from everywhere.
- **Workload planes**: each one serves inference traffic directly, makes its own **in-cluster** scaling decisions, and
  reports its utilization and demand back up.

The key property of this split: **every workload plane can keep serving on its own.** If the control plane has a
problem, or any single workload plane fails, the rest keep running unaffected.

## Getting GPUs

Plenty of companies rent out GPUs. They fall into three groups:

- **Hyperscalers**: the giant clouds, like AWS or GCP.
- **Neoclouds**: newer clouds focused specifically on GPUs, like CoreWeave or Nebius.
- **Resellers**: secondary markets, like SF Compute Company.

They differ in capacity, availability, and reliability. Hyperscalers usually cost more, and everywhere you trade price
against things like **uptime guarantees, support, which regions are available, instance configurations, and cluster
size**.

The first challenge is simply **getting the GPUs at all**, especially the newest hardware. Large clusters are hard to
find too: few providers can offer blocks of hundreds of nodes. Many providers give most of their in-demand GPUs to their
biggest customers on long-term reservations, so you may need **several providers** just to get enough GPUs in the
regions you need.

There are three ways to pay:

| Type | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Reserved** | Blocks of hundreds or thousands of GPUs, committed for months or years | Cheapest per hour, but you pay whether you use them or not |
| **On-demand** | Individual machines whenever you need them, up to a quota | Flexible, but a relatively high hourly price |
| **Spot** | Discounted on-demand machines the provider can **take back** with short notice, often minutes | Cheap, but can disappear |

Large deployments **blend all three**: a baseline of cheap **reserved** GPUs, topped up with **on-demand** and **spot**
machines for traffic peaks, spread across clusters worldwide to stay close to users.

## Sending each user somewhere close

A single cluster has a load balancer. A multi-cluster system needs a **global load balancer**, and it balances two
goals:

- Don't leave a request sitting in a queue when there's spare capacity somewhere else.
- But don't make a habit of sending a user in Singapore to a server in San Francisco.

The book's rule of thumb: a request takes about **5 milliseconds to cross one time zone**. New York to San Francisco is
three time zones, so about **15 ms one way**. Latency budgets are tight, so run workloads as close to users as you can.

## Building for reliability

GPUs are **infamous for failing** in production. Anyone who has done a large training run plans for hardware to break.

The book cites the Llama 3 paper: running **16,000 GPUs for 54 days**, Meta's team hit **419 unexpected
interruptions**, mostly from hardware failures. That's roughly **one failure per 50,000 GPU-hours**.

That sounds rare until you do the math: a single 8-GPU node running for a year is **more than 70,000 GPU-hours**. So
even a small deployment should **expect** failures. (The book includes a chart from the paper breaking these
interruptions down by root cause; hardware problems with the GPUs themselves and their memory dominate.)

Some practical lessons:

- **GPU health is a node-level concern.** When one GPU on a node fails, the others on that node often fail next, or have
  to be taken offline for repairs anyway. Watch for failures proactively, **cordon** the bad node (stop sending it new
  work), and **cycle** the workloads onto healthy machines.
- **GPUs aren't the only thing that breaks.** Cloud providers have scheduled maintenance and their own unplanned
  outages. Every layer of the stack needs to be reinforced.

Multi-cloud adds two classic reliability setups:

- **Active-active**: several regions or clusters **serve live traffic at the same time**. If one fails, traffic simply
  continues on the others.
- **Active-passive**: a **hot standby** region or cluster is kept ready but idle. If the active one fails, traffic is
  switched over to it.

Either way, being able to **fail over** to another workload plane when a cluster, a region, or an entire cloud provider
goes down keeps you reliable *and* keeps latency low.

## Security and compliance

Security and compliance teams have worried about cloud infrastructure for over twenty years, and inference that powers
important products has to satisfy them. Their concerns fall into three areas:

- **User data**: everything users send and everything the model sends back must be protected.
- **Model weights**: if you've fine-tuned or built your own model, the weights are a valuable trade secret.
- **Infrastructure**: the GPUs themselves, and access to the model's intelligence, are targets for abuse.

<Note kind="tip">
  One of the simplest security wins is to **not store user inputs or model outputs at all**. That's not always
  possible (you may have logging requirements, or agreements to keep data for future training), but if you don't need
  the data, not keeping it shrinks what an attacker could steal.
</Note>

Otherwise, securing inference looks like securing any containerized workload: **encryption, container security, network
and access controls, and workload isolation**, all checked by thorough third-party **penetration testing** (hiring
experts to try to break in).

More and more inference serves **regulated industries** and **compliance-heavy regions**, and multi-cloud helps here too:

- **Certifications and regulations flow through your providers.** To meet a standard like **SOC 2 Type II** or a law
  like **HIPAA**, the providers you run on generally have to be compliant too. Being able to move workloads onto
  compliant providers is valuable.
- **Data residency.** Some countries and industries require that their users' data is processed only inside their
  borders. With one cluster near **Toronto** and another near **New York**, you can keep Canadian data in Canada and
  American data in the U.S., while users anywhere in the region still get low latency.

<KeyPoints>
- Siloed clouds can't share capacity. True multi-cloud treats every GPU pool as **one interchangeable pool**.
- It unlocks **capacity, redundancy, latency, and compliance**.
- A global **control plane** makes deployment and scaling decisions. **Workload planes** serve traffic and keep running
  on their own if anything else fails.
- GPUs come from **hyperscalers, neoclouds, and resellers**, bought as **reserved, on-demand, or spot**, usually blended.
- A **global load balancer** keeps users near their servers: about **5 ms per time zone**, one way.
- Expect hardware failures (about **1 per 50,000 GPU-hours**). Cordon bad nodes, and use **active-active** or
  **active-passive** failover.
- Protect **user data, model weights, and infrastructure**. Don't store data you don't need. Use regions for
  **compliance** and **data residency**.
</KeyPoints>
