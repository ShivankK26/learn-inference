<TLDR>
  Image and video models are **compute-bound pipelines**, not LLMs, so the playbook changes: pick the **right kernels**,
  **fuse** the small ones, use **FP8** for twice the FLOPS, and use clever shortcuts like **skipping guidance** in later
  steps. Video is the hardest modality of all: **attention is 70–80% of the work**, eight GPUs team up on **one video at
  a time**, and the only way to make it cheaper is to make the model itself faster.
</TLDR>

<Note kind="tip">
  This lesson builds on [How image & video models work](/learn/image-and-video-models). Review it first if denoising
  steps, guidance, or latent space feel fuzzy.
</Note>

## Why images aren’t like LLMs

Image generation models can take a text prompt and sometimes **reference images**, and they produce new images. The
book’s figure shows both kinds of input feeding one model. The book says working with image and video models is
**entirely different** from working with LLMs on several axes:

1. **Architecture.** A few recent models, like **HunyuanImage-3.0**, more closely resemble LLMs. But most image and
   video models are **iterative denoisers**, not autoregressive token generators. They’re **pipelines of multiple
   small models** (text encoder, denoiser, VAE) working together in latent space, rather than the single uniform
   decoder of an LLM.
2. **Tooling.** Because the architecture differs, so do the tools. When the book was written, **SGLang Diffusion** and
   **vLLM Omni** were brand new. Most image and video inference is implemented **lower in the stack**, working
   directly with **PyTorch or TensorRT**.
3. **Constraints.** Image generation models are **ten to twenty times smaller** than frontier language models, and
   inference is constrained by <Compute>compute</Compute>, **not** memory bandwidth (see
   [Finding the bottleneck](/learn/bottlenecks)).
4. **Trade-offs.** Perhaps the most significant difference: image and video models offer **more direct
   quality-to-speed trade-offs**, such as the number of steps, the resolution, or the guidance tricks later in this
   lesson.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why compute-bound? An LLM decode step does a tiny bit of math per weight it loads (one token’s worth), so it waits on
  memory. A denoising step pushes **thousands of latent tokens** through every weight at once, like a giant prefill,
  so it does a lot of math per byte loaded. On the roofline chart it sits far to the right, under the flat compute
  ceiling.
</Note>

### Measuring quality is hard

That last point, direct quality-to-speed trade-offs, comes with a catch: you need to know when you’ve traded away too
much quality, and **image quality is difficult to evaluate programmatically**. The book is blunt:

- **Automatic pipelines using vision language models** (asking a VLM to grade the images) give **directional signal at
  best**, and may **diverge from human preferences**.
- The human eye is mysterious, so most image quality evaluations work by **asking humans to pick among thousands of
  images**, then aggregating those preferences into quality benchmarks.

<Note kind="beyond">
  This is the same head-to-head idea as **Elo ratings** in [Choosing a model](/learn/choosing-a-model): show people two
  images from the same prompt, ask which is better, and rank models by their win rates. For your own optimizations, a
  practical version is to show reviewers the original model’s image and the optimized model’s image side by side and
  check they can’t reliably tell them apart.
</Note>

## Kernel optimization for image models

When you read the model card for an image generation model, the inference examples generally use the **Diffusers**
library with **very few optimizations**. That code is written to be correct and readable, not fast.

The book makes a subtle point here: while image generation is *theoretically* compute-bound, you often need to
**select memory-efficient kernels and use kernel fusion just to reach that bottleneck**. In an unoptimized pipeline,
the GPU spends much of its time shuttling data between small operations, so it isn’t compute-bound yet. It’s wasting
time on memory traffic.

<Worked title="Why small kernels waste a compute-bound GPU">
  Take a normalization step like **RMSNorm** applied to the activations between big matrix multiplies.

  1. It reads the whole activation tensor from GPU memory, does a few operations per value (square, average, divide,
     scale), and writes the whole tensor back.
  2. That’s only a handful of FLOPs per value, while each 16-bit value costs 2 bytes to read and 2 bytes to write.
     Its **arithmetic intensity is around 1–2 FLOPs per byte**.
  3. An H100’s ops:byte ratio is about **295** ([Finding the bottleneck](/learn/bottlenecks)). At 1–2 FLOPs per byte,
     RMSNorm is **deeply memory-bound**: the Tensor Cores sit idle while it runs.
  4. **Fusing** RMSNorm into the neighboring kernel means the data is normalized while it’s already on-chip, so the
     extra read and write disappear, and the GPU gets back to doing math.

  Multiply that by the dozens of small kernels in every layer and every step, and fusion is the difference between
  being compute-bound on paper and in practice.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The worked example’s FLOP counts are approximate; the point is the ratio. Anything that does only a few operations
  per value it loads will be memory-bound on a modern GPU.
</Note>

### Three routes to fast image inference

High-performance image model inference uses one of **three libraries**:

| Option | What you get |
|---|---|
| **SGLang Diffusion** | Performant inference engines built for popular image and video generation architectures. |
| **TensorRT** | High-quality **black-box** implementations of popular models, using NVIDIA’s in-house kernels. |
| **PyTorch** | Careful **kernel selection and fusion**: control, flexibility, and improved high-end performance. |

The book’s advice: **if you want something that works well and you want it now**, use the SGLang Diffusion or TensorRT
implementation of a model. But with PyTorch, there’s room for advanced inference engineers to do **deep
customization**. Here’s where that customization goes, in order of importance.

### 1. The attention kernel

The **most essential kernel** is attention. Many image models use **FlashAttention 2** out of the box, but
**FlashAttention 3** performs better on **Hopper** GPUs and **FlashAttention 4** on **Blackwell** GPUs (see
[Making attention faster](/learn/faster-attention)). Each version is hand-built for its GPU generation, so matching the
kernel to the hardware is free performance.

### 2. Fuse the small kernels

There’s a whole barrage of smaller kernels, especially **normalization functions like RMSNorm**, that are good
candidates for **fusion** to ensure efficient memory usage (see [CUDA and kernels](/learn/cuda-and-kernels)). As the
worked example above shows, these are where a compute-bound model quietly loses time to memory.

### 3. GEMM kernels, in FP8

Then come **GEMM kernels** (general matrix multiplies), which matter for compute-bound inference. GEMM kernels apply to
**linear layers**, and those are generally **safe to quantize to 8-bit floating point**, which accesses **two times
higher FLOPS** on Tensor Cores. The book suggests kernels from **CuTe, CUTLASS, or DeepGEMM**, and notes the best choice
varies **model by model**, so benchmark them.

<Worked title="What FP8 buys a compute-bound model">
  1. From the [GPU generations](/learn/gpu-generations) spec tables: an H100 does about **989 TFLOPS** of dense FP16
     math and about **1,979 TFLOPS** in FP8.
  2. For a memory-bound LLM, FP8’s benefit is **half as many bytes to move**.
  3. For a compute-bound image model, the benefit is **twice as many operations per second**: 1,979 ÷ 989 ≈ **2×** for
     the layers you quantize.
  4. Same technique, different reason. Which reason applies tells you which layers are worth quantizing.
</Worked>

### Torch compilation

**Torch compilation** (`torch.compile`) ties it together:

- It performs **automatic kernel fusion**.
- It has a **plugin system** for inserting manually selected kernels (your FlashAttention 3 or DeepGEMM choice).
- The resulting engine can be **cached** for faster load times on node startup. That matters because **compilation
  takes several minutes**: without a cache, every new machine spends those minutes before it can serve a request (see
  cold starts in [Autoscaling](/learn/autoscaling)).

<Note kind="warn">
  Like most high-performance engines, Torch compilation **targets the specific GPU model and architecture that
  performs the compilation**. If you want to run the model on a B200, **do the compilation on a B200**. A cached engine
  compiled on an H100 isn’t tuned for, and may not run on, different hardware.
</Note>

## One weird trick: skip guidance late

Kernel selection and Torch compilation are **bona fide** inference optimization techniques. But the book notes the
world of inference optimization has **fun hacks** too, and here’s one.

First, the facts it builds on:

- **Image generation time tracks linearly with step count.** That’s why **few-step models** and **latent consistency
  models** are so much faster than full 50-step models. But reducing the step count may drop image quality below an
  acceptable threshold.
- **Each step runs at batch size two**, because each step includes a pass **with** prompt guidance and a pass
  **without** it.
- The **guidance** parameter controls how much the prompt-guided result is weighted when the two are combined. **If
  guidance is zero, the prompt-guided pass doesn’t need to be generated at all.**

Now the insight, illustrated by the book’s figure of diffusion as a step-by-step process: **the general outline of the
image is established in early steps**. After the first few steps, the basic outline is in place, and the rest of the
steps fill in details. So **prompt adherence matters more in early steps**, which set the broad strokes. As the book
puts it, the model isn’t going to change its mind on a later step and generate a dog when it’s in the middle of
generating a cat.

The trick: **turn off guidance partway through**. You save passes through the denoiser **without reducing the step
count**, and quality generally remains high.

<Worked title="Counting passes with late guidance skipping">
  Let **S** be the total number of steps and **G** the number of guided steps.

  1. Each guided step costs 2 passes (with and without the prompt). Each unguided step costs 1.
  2. Total passes = 2 × G + 1 × (S − G) = **S + G**.
  3. The book’s example: S = 50, and guidance is skipped for the last 20 steps, so G = 30.
  4. Total passes = 50 + 30 = **80**, instead of 2 × 50 = **100**.
  5. Generation time tracks passes, so that’s about **20% less work**, with the same 50 steps of refinement.

  Compare with cutting steps instead: a 40-step fully guided run is also 80 passes, but it has 10 fewer steps of
  refinement, which is exactly what hurts quality.
</Worked>

## Video generation: the hardest modality

Video generation models take text prompts and **may take keyframes or other image, audio, and video input**, as the
book’s figure shows. The book calls video generation **the most demanding modality**, and gives a hardware
recommendation: whenever possible, run these models on **Blackwell GPUs** (or **Rubin**, once available), because they
offer:

- **high memory capacity** for context parallelism (below),
- **fast Tensor Cores** for attention computation,
- **microscaling data formats** for more precise quantization ([Quantization](/learn/quantization)).

Architecturally, video generation is similar to image generation, just rendering a **full video** from latent space
instead of a single frame. Following the principle from [Chapter 5](/learn/quantization) that **greater scale unlocks
more techniques**, video generation uses **all the same techniques as image generation, plus additional
optimizations**.

Like image generation, video generation is **compute-bound** and works by **iterative denoising over latent space**.
Video models take about the **same number of denoising steps (~50)** as image models, but **each step processes much
more data**.

### Batch size one, on eight GPUs

Because video generation is compute-bound, **batching isn’t useful** like it is for text generation. Batching helps LLM
decode because it adds math to a GPU that was waiting on memory; a video model has no idle compute to fill. So video
generation models usually run on **full nodes of eight GPUs with a batch size of one**: all eight GPUs work together
to create **one video at a time**.

This removes a lever you’ve relied on all course. With batched workloads, you trade latency for throughput by
adjusting the batch size. With video, the book says, **the only way to improve throughput and cost is to make the model
itself faster**.

<Worked title="Why speed is cost for video (illustrative)">
  1. Suppose an 8-GPU node costs **$40 per hour** and generates one video in **2 minutes**.
  2. Videos per hour: 60 ÷ 2 = **30**. Cost per video: $40 ÷ 30 ≈ **$1.33**.
  3. Make the model twice as fast (1 minute per video): 60 videos per hour, **$0.67 per video**.
  4. There’s no batch-size dial to turn instead, so every optimization in the rest of this lesson translates directly
     into cost per video.

  The dollar and time figures are illustrative; the proportionality is the point.
</Worked>

### Why attention takes over

Early video generation models were **framewise**: they generated frames one at a time, which reduced quality and
coherence. Today, video models run denoising steps on **the video as a whole** in latent space. Where the latent space
for images has **two dimensions** (width, height), for video it has **three**: width, height, and **time**.

That means passing **huge amounts of data** through each attention calculation. The book states that for video
models, **attention is 70 to 80 percent of the compute time**, which makes attention **the most important thing to
optimize**.

Why so lopsided? Because the two kinds of work grow at different rates as the latent gets bigger:

- The **linear layers** process each token independently, so their work grows **in proportion to the number of
  tokens**.
- **Attention** compares every token with every other token, so its work grows with **the number of tokens squared**
  ([Attention and the KV cache](/learn/attention-and-kv-cache)).

<Worked title="From a picture to a clip: tokens and attention">
  These latent sizes are typical of current open models (illustrative, not from the book): the VAE shrinks width and
  height 8× and time 4×, then the transformer takes 2×2 patches.

  1. **Image, 1024×1024**: 1024 ÷ 16 = 64 per side → 64 × 64 = **4,096 tokens**.
  2. **Video, 720p (1280×720), 5 seconds at 24 fps**: 120 frames → about **30 latent frames** after 4× time
     compression. Each latent frame is (1280 ÷ 16) × (720 ÷ 16) = 80 × 45 = 3,600 tokens. Total: 30 × 3,600 =
     **108,000 tokens**.
  3. **Token ratio**: 108,000 ÷ 4,096 ≈ **26×** more tokens than the image.
  4. **Linear-layer work** grows 26×. **Attention work** grows 26² ≈ **700×**.
  5. In the widget below’s idealized model, attention goes from about **15%** of the compute for the image to about
     **80%** for the clip, in line with the book’s 70–80%.
</Worked>

### Faster attention: kernels and caching

Attention optimization **starts with kernel selection**. The book says to test **FlashAttention, DeepGEMM, CuTe, and
CUTLASS** kernels to see which perform best for your model.

Next is **caching**. Where language models use the KV cache to accelerate attention, video models use **other caching
patterns** that try to reuse model outputs. Reusing parts of the attention computation can make video generation
**30–40% faster** in practice. The precise methods change constantly with new research, but there are **two fundamental
approaches**:

- **Timestep-based caching**: cache and reuse the outputs of certain **timesteps** (denoising steps) to **skip entire
  steps**.
- **Transformer-based caching**: cache and reuse **hidden states** to **skip layers** within the transformer itself.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why can you reuse anything at all? In later denoising steps the latent changes only a little from one step to the
  next, so a step’s intermediate results often look very similar to the previous step’s. Caching methods measure that
  similarity and reuse the old result when the difference is small enough. The book’s reading list includes examples
  such as **TeaCache** and **Cache-DiT** (see [Further reading](/reading)).
</Note>

<Note kind="warn">
  The book warns that caching algorithms and implementations range from **negligible quality degradation to unusable
  output**. Test these strategies carefully, with human review, before using them in production.
</Note>

### Faster attention: quantization

Beyond kernels and caching, the **main tool for speeding up attention is quantization**, but it helps video models for
a **different reason** than language models:

- For **bandwidth-constrained** language model inference, the benefit of quantization is **less data to load**
  through memory.
- For **compute-bound** video models, it means **double the FLOPS** by switching to lower-precision Tensor Cores.

And the **target** is different. Language model quantization focuses on **weights**: the large linear layers, where
quantization’s quality impact is negligible. For video models, quantizing weights still helps, but those linear layers,
which take most of the *memory bandwidth* and so constrain language models, are only **a small fraction of the compute
time** for video models. Instead, **quantization on video models focuses on attention**.

<Worked title="Why quantize attention? A speedup budget">
  Suppose FP8 makes whatever it touches **2× faster**, and in your video model attention is **75%** of compute time and
  linear layers are the remaining **25%**.

  1. **Quantize only the linear layers**: time = 75% + 25% ÷ 2 = 87.5% of the original → **1.14× faster**.
  2. **Quantize only attention**: time = 75% ÷ 2 + 25% = 62.5% → **1.6× faster**.
  3. **Quantize both**: time = 50% → **2× faster**.

  The part of the work that takes the most time is the part worth speeding up.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The rule in that worked example is often called **Amdahl’s law**: the overall speedup from improving one part of a
  job is limited by how much of the total time that part takes.
</Note>

But attention is **the riskiest part of any model to quantize**, because errors **accumulate over the course of
inference**. For video models, with **~50 steps** instead of the **thousands of autoregressive iterations** in token
generation, the risk is **slightly lower but still important**.

The book gives two methods for reducing the quality impact:

**1. Blockwise quantization with a microscaling format.** Use **blockwise quantization** and a **microscaling data
format (MXFP8)**, both available on Hopper and Blackwell. Microscaling formats do a better job of preserving **outlier
values**, which have a **major impact on attention accuracy** (see block scaling in
[Quantization](/learn/quantization)). Each small block gets its own scale factor, so one huge value only distorts its
own block, not the whole tensor.

**2. Selective quantization.** The most sophisticated approach is to quantize only the **less important parts** of the
model:

- **By step: keep early steps in FP16 and quantize later steps.** This follows the same insight as the guidance trick:
  early steps establish the outline, later steps refine the details. Early steps matter more for **prompt adherence
  and accuracy**.
- **By layer: keep the first and last layers and quantize the hidden layers.** The first and last layers are more
  important because they **take the input and produce the final output**. The hidden layers perform intermediate
  calculations, which don’t suffer as much from approximation.

<SelectiveQuant />

By quantizing only the less important parts of the process, **quality is preserved**. These tactics are found in
kernels like **SageAttention**, an **8-bit attention kernel** you can use for good-quality low-precision attention on
video generation models.

### Context parallelism

Video generation models generally run on a **full node of eight GPUs**, but they use **context parallelism**, not the
[tensor parallelism](/learn/parallelism) common for LLMs. The book’s figure shows the difference: the model weights are
**replicated** on every GPU, while the **latent space is shared** out to compute attention.

- **Copy the weights onto every GPU.** Video models are small enough that replicating the weights eight times takes a
  **meaningful amount of memory but is feasible on B200**.
- **Split the attention calculation, not the model.** Each GPU holds **a piece of the context** (a slice of the video’s
  latent tokens). This is coordinated by a mechanism like <Term def="Each GPU holds part of the sequence and passes its keys and values to the next GPU in a loop, until every piece has been compared with every other piece.">ring attention</Term>:
  each GPU holds a piece of the context and **passes intermediate results to the next GPU in the ring**.

<Worked title="Can eight copies of the weights fit? (illustrative)">
  1. Take a video model with **14B parameters** stored in BF16 (2 bytes each): 14 × 10⁹ × 2 = **28 GB** of weights.
  2. Context parallelism puts a full copy on each of 8 GPUs: 8 × 28 GB = **224 GB** across the node. That’s the
     “meaningful amount of memory”.
  3. But per GPU it’s only 28 GB of a B200’s **192 GB**, leaving about **164 GB** on each GPU for activations and
     attention work. Feasible, as the book says.
  4. With tensor parallelism instead, each GPU would hold 1/8 of the weights, but every layer would need GPUs to
     exchange partial results, and for video the heavy part is attention over the huge latent, not the weights.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  How ring attention works, step by step: split the 108,000 latent tokens across 8 GPUs, about 13,500 each. Every GPU
  keeps its own queries and starts with its own slice of keys and values.

  1. Each GPU computes attention between its queries and the key/value slice it currently holds.
  2. It passes that key/value slice to the next GPU in the ring and receives one from the previous GPU.
  3. Repeat. After 8 rounds, every GPU’s queries have met every slice of keys and values.

  Partial results are combined as they arrive (the same running-softmax trick FlashAttention uses), and passing the
  next slice can overlap with computing on the current one, which hides much of the communication cost.
</Note>

<ContextParallel />

The book notes two more ways to parallelize:

- **Attention heads.** Attention in transformers is **multi-head**, usually with **eight or more heads**. Heads are
  **independent**, so they can be computed separately on different GPUs and the results combined afterward.
- **VAE decoding.** Attention isn’t the only thing that parallelizes. The **latent decoding step**, where the VAE turns
  the finished latent into pixels, takes **3–5% of total inference time** and can also be run across GPUs.

The book closes the chapter by stating that these parallelism techniques are what **make AI video feasible**. As video
sequences get longer and video models get larger, parallelism will remain **the most critical technique** for video
generation inference.

<KeyPoints>
- Image and video models are **denoising pipelines**, 10–20× smaller than frontier LLMs, **compute-bound**, served lower in the stack (PyTorch/TensorRT), with **direct quality-to-speed trade-offs**, and quality is best judged by **human preference**.
- Diffusers examples are unoptimized. You often need **memory-efficient kernels and fusion just to reach** the compute bound, because small ops like RMSNorm are memory-bound.
- Routes: **SGLang Diffusion** or **TensorRT** for quick wins, **PyTorch** for deep control. Priorities: the **attention kernel** (FA3 on Hopper, FA4 on Blackwell), **fuse** small kernels, **FP8 GEMMs** for 2× FLOPS (CuTe, CUTLASS, DeepGEMM, per model).
- `torch.compile` fuses and plugs in kernels, can be **cached** (compiles take minutes), and must be **compiled on the GPU you’ll run on**.
- **Skip guidance late**: passes = steps + guided steps; 50 steps with the last 20 unguided = **80 passes instead of 100**.
- Video: Blackwell (or Rubin), **~50 steps** like images but far more data per step, **batch size 1 on 8 GPUs**, so only a faster model improves cost.
- Attention is **70–80%** of video compute because it grows with **tokens²** while linear layers grow with tokens.
- Speed up attention with **kernel selection**, **timestep/transformer caching** (30–40%, test carefully), and **attention quantization**: MXFP8 blockwise, plus **selective** quantization by step (early steps FP16) and layer (first/last layers kept), as in **SageAttention**.
- **Context parallelism**: replicate weights (feasible on B200), split the latent, combine with **ring attention**. Heads and the VAE decode (3–5% of time) parallelize too.
</KeyPoints>
