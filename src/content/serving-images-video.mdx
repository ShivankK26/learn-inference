## Why images aren’t like LLMs

Image generation models can take a text prompt and sometimes reference images, and they produce new images (the
book’s figure shows both inputs). Working with them differs from LLMs in several ways:

- **Architecture.** A few newer models (like HunyuanImage-3.0) look like LLMs, but most are **iterative denoisers**:
  pipelines of several small models working in latent space, not one uniform decoder.
- **Tooling.** LLM-style engines for images are brand new (**SGLang Diffusion** and **vLLM Omni** had only just
  appeared when the book was written). Most image and video inference is built **lower in the stack**, directly on
  PyTorch or TensorRT.
- **Constraints.** Image models are **10–20× smaller** than frontier LLMs, and they’re limited by <Compute>compute</Compute>,
  not memory bandwidth (see [Finding the bottleneck](/learn/bottlenecks)).
- **Trade-offs.** Most importantly, image and video models offer **more direct quality-versus-speed dials**.

That last point comes with a catch: **image quality is hard to measure automatically.** Using a VLM to judge images
gives a rough signal at best, and it can disagree with what people like. The human eye is mysterious, so most
image-quality benchmarks come from asking **people to pick between thousands of images** and aggregating their
preferences.

## Kernel optimization for image models

The example code on an image model’s page usually uses the **Diffusers** library with **very few optimizations**. In
fact, even though image generation is *theoretically* compute-bound, you often need memory-efficient kernels and
[kernel fusion](/learn/cuda-and-kernels) just to *reach* that compute limit. Before that, the GPU is wasting time on
memory traffic.

High-performance image inference uses one of three routes:

| Option | What you get |
|---|---|
| **SGLang Diffusion** | Fast, ready-made engines for popular image and video architectures. |
| **TensorRT** | High-quality black-box implementations of popular models with NVIDIA’s own kernels. |
| **PyTorch** | Careful hand-picked kernels and fusion. The most control, flexibility, and top-end performance. |

If you want something good *now*, use the SGLang Diffusion or TensorRT version. PyTorch is where advanced engineers can
customize deeply. Where to focus:

1. **The attention kernel matters most.** Many models ship with **FlashAttention 2**, but **FlashAttention 3** is
   faster on Hopper GPUs and **FlashAttention 4** on Blackwell (see [Making attention faster](/learn/faster-attention)).
2. **Fuse the small stuff.** There’s a crowd of little kernels, especially normalization functions like **RMSNorm**,
   that are great candidates for fusion to cut memory traffic.
3. **GEMM kernels** (the matrix multiplies in linear layers) matter a lot when you’re compute-bound. They’re usually
   **safe to quantize to FP8**, which unlocks **2× the FLOPS** on Tensor Cores. Kernels from **CuTe, CUTLASS, or
   DeepGEMM** may win depending on the model, so test them.

**Torch compilation** (`torch.compile`) does automatic kernel fusion, lets you plug in hand-picked kernels, and produces
an engine you can **cache** so new machines start faster. That matters because compiling takes **several minutes**.

<Note kind="warn">
  Compiled engines are tuned for the **exact GPU that compiled them**. If you’ll run on a B200, compile on a B200.
</Note>

## One weird trick: skip guidance late

Kernel choice and compilation are serious engineering. This one is more of a clever hack.

Image generation time grows **in a straight line with the number of steps**. That’s why few-step models are so fast,
but cutting steps can drop quality below what’s acceptable. Is there a way to do less work *without* fewer steps?

Recall that every step actually runs the model **twice**, at a batch size of two: once *with* your prompt and once
*without*, blended according to the **guidance** setting. If guidance were zero, the prompt-guided pass wouldn’t be
needed at all.

Now the key insight (the book’s figure illustrates it): **the rough outline of the image is decided in the first few
steps**. The later steps just fill in details. Prompt adherence matters most early on. The model isn’t going to change
its mind halfway through and paint a dog when it’s already painting a cat.

So **turn guidance off partway through**. If you skip guidance for the **last 20 steps of a 50-step run**, you do
**80 passes instead of 100**, and quality usually stays high.

## Video generation: the hardest modality

Video models take text prompts, and sometimes keyframes or other image, audio, and video inputs (the book’s figure).
**Video is the most demanding modality**, so run it on **Blackwell GPUs** (or **Rubin** once available) whenever
possible. They offer:

- lots of **memory** for context parallelism (below),
- fast **Tensor Cores** for attention,
- **microscaling** number formats for more precise quantization.

Architecturally, video models are image models that render a **whole clip** from latent space, and since bigger scale
unlocks more techniques, they use **every image trick plus some extra ones**. Like image generation, video is
**compute-bound** and uses about the **same ~50 denoising steps**, but each step processes vastly more data.

Because it’s compute-bound, **batching doesn’t help** the way it does for text. Video models usually run on a **full
node of 8 GPUs at batch size 1**: all eight GPUs work together on **one video at a time**. That means there’s no
latency-versus-throughput dial to turn. **The only way to improve throughput and cost is to make the model itself
faster.**

Early video models generated one frame at a time, which hurt quality and coherence. Today’s models denoise the **entire
video at once** in a latent space with three dimensions (width, height, **time**) instead of two. That means enormous
amounts of data go through every attention calculation. **Attention is 70–80% of the compute time** in video models,
so it’s the thing to optimize.

### Faster attention: kernels and caching

Start with **kernel selection**: benchmark **FlashAttention, DeepGEMM, CuTe, and CUTLASS** kernels on your model.

LLMs speed up attention with the KV cache. Video models use **other caching patterns** to reuse earlier outputs, and
reusing parts of the attention work can make generation **30–40% faster** in practice. The research changes quickly,
but there are two basic approaches:

- **Timestep-based caching**: reuse the outputs of certain denoising steps to **skip whole steps**.
- **Transformer-based caching**: reuse hidden states to **skip layers** inside the transformer.

<Note kind="warn">
  Results range from **no visible quality loss** to **unusable output**, depending on the algorithm. Test caching
  strategies carefully before production.
</Note>

### Faster attention: quantization

Beyond kernels and caching, the main tool is [quantization](/learn/quantization), but it helps video for a
**different reason** than LLMs:

- For memory-bound LLMs, quantization means **less data to move**.
- For compute-bound video models, it means **2× the FLOPS** by switching to lower-precision Tensor Cores.

And the target is different. LLM quantization focuses on **weights**: the big linear layers, where quantization barely
hurts quality. Those layers dominate memory traffic in LLMs, but in video models they’re only a **small slice of compute
time**. So video quantization focuses on **attention**, which is the **riskiest part of any model to quantize**,
because errors pile up. Video has only ~50 steps instead of thousands of token-by-token iterations, so the risk is a
little lower, but it’s still real.

Two ways to limit the damage:

1. **Blockwise quantization with a microscaling format** (**MXFP8**, available on Hopper and Blackwell). Microscaling
   keeps **outlier values** more accurate, and outliers have a big effect on attention accuracy.
2. **Selective quantization**: only quantize the less important parts.
   - **By step:** keep the **early steps in FP16** and quantize the later ones. It’s the same insight as the guidance
     trick: early steps set the composition, later steps refine details.
   - **By layer:** keep the **first and last layers** in higher precision, since they take in the input and produce
     the final output. The hidden layers in between handle approximation better.

These tactics are packaged in kernels like **SageAttention**, an **8-bit attention kernel** designed for good-quality,
low-precision attention in video models.

### Context parallelism

Video models run across a full 8-GPU node, but they use **context parallelism**, not the
[tensor parallelism](/learn/parallelism) common for LLMs.

- **Copy the weights onto every GPU.** Video models are small enough that eight copies use a meaningful chunk of memory
  but still fit on B200s.
- **Split the attention work instead of the model.** Each GPU holds a **slice of the video’s latent** (its context).
  Using a scheme like <Term def="Each GPU holds part of the sequence and passes its keys and values to the next GPU in a loop, until every piece has been compared with every other piece.">ring attention</Term>,
  each GPU passes intermediate results to the next GPU in a ring until all slices have been compared with each other.
  (The book’s figure shows the weights replicated while the latent is shared.)

<ContextParallel />

Two more things can run in parallel:

- **Attention heads**: attention has several heads (usually eight or more) that are **independent**, so they can be
  computed separately and combined afterward.
- **The VAE decode step**, which turns the finished latent into pixels and takes **3–5% of total time**, can also be
  spread across GPUs.

These parallelism techniques are what make AI video feasible. As videos get longer and models grow, parallelism will
stay **the most critical technique** for video inference.

<KeyPoints>
- Image models: **denoising pipelines**, 10–20× smaller than frontier LLMs, **compute-bound**, with hard-to-automate quality evaluation.
- Use **SGLang Diffusion** or **TensorRT** for quick wins, or **PyTorch** for deep control. Prioritize the **attention kernel** (FA3 on Hopper, FA4 on Blackwell), **fuse** small kernels like RMSNorm, and use **FP8 GEMMs** for 2× FLOPS.
- `torch.compile` fuses kernels and can be cached, but **compile on the same GPU you’ll run on**.
- **Skip guidance** in later steps: 50 steps with the last 20 unguided = 80 passes instead of 100.
- Video: Blackwell GPUs, **batch size 1 on 8 GPUs**, and **attention is 70–80% of compute**.
- Speed it up with kernel choice, **timestep/transformer caching** (30–40%), and **attention quantization** (MXFP8, selective by step and layer, e.g. SageAttention).
- **Context parallelism**: replicate the weights, split the latent, and combine with **ring attention**. Heads and VAE decoding parallelize too.
</KeyPoints>
