<TLDR>
  The biggest performance decision isn’t a clever GPU trick, it’s **which model you run**. A smaller model is faster and
  cheaper on the same hardware. So once you’re scaling, find (or make) the **smallest model that’s smart enough**, and
  use your own evals to prove it.
</TLDR>

## Smaller is faster, so choose carefully

If everything else is equal (same hardware, same software, same optimizations, same architecture), a model with
**fewer parameters** will always be faster and cheaper to run than a bigger one. That makes model choice the single
most important performance decision, more important than which inference engine or speculation algorithm you pick.

The advice flips depending on your stage:

- **Before product-market fit**: just use pay-per-token APIs for powerful frontier models (open ones like Kimi and
  DeepSeek, or even closed ones like GPT and Gemini). It’s not worth spending time or money on your own inference yet.
- **When it’s time to scale**: find, or create, the **smallest, easiest-to-run model that’s smart enough for the task**.
  Sometimes that will still be a trillion-parameter frontier model, but it’s always worth checking whether something
  smaller, cheaper, and faster will do.

<Note kind="tip">
  Your model choice also decides which optimizations you can use. Inference engines support some architectures much
  better than others, so **stick with popular architectures** to get strong support from the whole performance tooling
  ecosystem.
</Note>

## Evals: measuring whether a model is good enough

**Model evaluation**, or **evals**, means systematically measuring how smart a model is *at your task*. Solid evals are
a prerequisite for inference engineering, for two reasons:

1. **They tell you where to spend your time.** Before you invest in making a model fast, make sure it’s actually
   useful.
2. **They give you a baseline.** Some speed-up techniques (like quantization, in Chapter 5) can slightly reduce
   quality. You need a “before” score to check the “after” against.

### Benchmarks vs. evals

Public **intelligence benchmarks** like MMLU (general knowledge) or SWE-bench (coding) measure models on common tasks.
They’re handy for **shortlisting** candidates, but many are **saturated** (every top model aces them) or even
**gamed**.

That’s an example of **Goodhart’s Law**, roughly *“when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.”*
Labs have a huge incentive to post record benchmark scores with every release, which makes those scores less
trustworthy.

There are better ways to gauge general intelligence, like **Elo ratings** based on head-to-head win rates between
models (the same idea as chess rankings). But nothing replaces **measuring directly how the model performs in your
application.**

Tips for evals that are actually useful:

- **Look at your data.** Check eval results against your own intuition about the product and the problem.
- **Be precise.** Know the *hardest* problems the model has to solve, and focus the evals there.
- **Use tools.** Evals are a core AI engineering problem, so don’t reinvent the wheel. (The book’s recommended-reading
  appendix lists tools.)

## Fine-tuning: make a small model an expert

**Fine-tuning** means taking a pre-trained foundation model and training it further on new data for your specific use.
As the book’s figure shows, fine-tuning **changes the values of the weights but keeps the architecture exactly the
same**. So a fine-tuned model runs just as fast as the original and gets all the same optimizations.

If you can fine-tune a **small** model until it passes your evals, you’ve made hitting your latency and cost targets
much easier.

<Analogy>
  A general-purpose model is like a brilliant generalist consultant: expensive and able to do anything. Fine-tuning is
  like training a sharp junior employee to do *one* job really well. For that one job, they can match the consultant at a
  fraction of the cost.
</Analogy>

The book’s favorite example is **text-to-SQL**: turning a plain-English question into a database query. General coding
models write SQL well, but they have **hundreds of billions** of parameters. SQL is a fairly constrained language, so
for an app that *only* needs to write SQL, a fine-tuned model of just **a few billion** parameters can perform as well
on that task.

That’s an extreme case, and many domains won’t allow such a dramatic shrink. It works when you have:

- a cleanly scoped domain,
- strong evaluation criteria, and
- high-quality labeled data to fine-tune on.

## Distillation: shrink a big model’s behavior

What if you could keep most of a big model’s intelligence at a fraction of the size? That’s **distillation**. A large
**teacher** model trains a smaller **student** model to imitate it.

The key difference from ordinary fine-tuning is *what the student learns from*. Fine-tuning on examples shows only the
input and the final answer. Distillation shows the student the teacher’s **full probability distribution**: not just
“the answer was X”, but how likely the teacher thought every option was.

<DistillCompare />

| | Fine-tuning | Distillation |
|---|---|---|
| **Learns from** | Input → correct output pairs | The teacher model’s full probabilities |
| **Goal** | Get better at a specific domain | Imitate a bigger model’s behavior (good *and* bad habits) |
| **How common** | Very common | Much less common in practice |

A surprising detail: when a lab releases a family of models in several sizes, the small ones usually **aren’t**
distilled from the big one. They’re trained independently, so the big model’s biases don’t artificially limit the
smaller ones. Distillation is most useful when a lab only trains one huge model and wants to make it more accessible.

**Example:** in January 2025, DeepSeek released **DeepSeek-R1**, a huge reasoning model with **671B** parameters. They
also released distilled versions built on the most popular open architectures of the time, **Llama 3** and **Qwen
2.5**. The distills reasoned in a similar style to R1, with lower benchmark scores, but they were small and could reuse
all the existing performance work for Llama and Qwen. At the time the book was written, these R1 distills were still
among the most popular distilled models on Hugging Face, alongside distills of Whisper (audio transcription) and some
image generation models.

<KeyPoints>
- On the same setup, **fewer parameters = faster and cheaper**, which makes model choice the biggest performance
  decision.
- Early on, use frontier APIs. At scale, find the **smallest model that’s smart enough**, on a **popular architecture**.
- **Evals** tailored to your product beat public benchmarks, which are often saturated or gamed (Goodhart’s Law).
- **Fine-tuning** changes the weights, not the architecture, and can make a small model an expert (e.g. text-to-SQL).
- **Distillation** trains a small student on a big teacher’s full probabilities. It’s rarer than fine-tuning.
</KeyPoints>
