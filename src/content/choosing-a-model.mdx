<TLDR>
  The biggest performance decision isn’t a clever GPU trick, it’s **which model you run**. On the same setup, a smaller
  model is always faster and cheaper. So once you’re scaling, find (or create, through fine-tuning or distillation) the
  **smallest model that’s smart enough**, on a **popular architecture**, and prove it with **your own evals**.
</TLDR>

## Why model choice beats every other optimization

The book’s starting point: **all else being equal** (same hardware, same runtime, same optimizations, same
architecture), **inference on a smaller model with fewer parameters will be faster and cheaper** than on a larger
model.

That leads to a striking conclusion: **the most important decision in model performance optimization isn’t the runtime
engine or the speculation algorithm. It’s which model you choose to work with in the first place.**

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why size matters so directly: as Chapter 2 explains, generating each token requires reading essentially all of a
  model’s weights from GPU memory. A 70-billion-parameter model stored in 16-bit numbers is about 140 GB of weights; an
  8-billion-parameter model is about 16 GB. On the same GPU, the smaller model can produce tokens several times faster,
  and it may fit on one GPU instead of several. No engine setting comes close to a difference that large.
</Note>

### The advice flips with your stage

- **Early stage (before product-market fit)**: AI engineers iterating on a new product should **just use prebuilt
  pay-per-token APIs for powerful frontier models**, open ones like **Kimi** and **DeepSeek**, or even closed ones like
  **GPT** and **Gemini**. It’s not worth spending time or money on your own inference yet.
- **When it’s time to scale**: the **opposite** advice applies. **Find, or create, the smallest, easiest-to-run model
  that’s smart enough for the task.** In many cases that will *still* need to be a trillion-parameter frontier model,
  but it’s **always worth checking** whether a smaller, cheaper, faster model can do the job.

### Stick with popular architectures

Which model you pick also decides **which inference optimizations are available to you**. Inference engines vary in
how deeply they support different model architectures. The book’s advice: **stick with popular model architectures**
so you’ll find robust support across the whole performance tooling landscape.

<Note kind="beyond">
  An *architecture* is the model’s structural design (how many layers, what kind of attention, whether it’s a Mixture
  of Experts, and so on). Many different models share one architecture: every fine-tune of Llama 3 is still a Llama 3
  architecture. Engines like vLLM, SGLang, and TensorRT-LLM ship optimized code for popular architectures first, so a
  model with a rare or brand-new architecture may run slowly, or not at all, until support catches up. Chapter 2 shows
  how to read a model’s architecture from its config file.
</Note>

## Model evaluation (evals)

**Model evaluation**, or **evals**, is the practice of **systematically measuring model intelligence**. The book calls
**high-conviction model evaluation a prerequisite for inference engineering**, for two reasons:

1. **Spend time wisely.** Before investing effort in making a model fast, evals make sure the model is actually
   **useful**. Optimizing the wrong model is wasted work.
2. **Establish a baseline.** Some performance techniques **risk reducing model quality** (quantization in Chapter 5 is
   the classic example). You need a **baseline** score to compare the optimized model against, or you can’t tell whether
   your speedup quietly made the product worse.

### Benchmarks vs. evals

| | Intelligence benchmarks | Evals |
|---|---|---|
| **What they measure** | Model capabilities on **common tasks** | Performance on **your** product, domain, and tasks |
| **Examples** | **MMLU** (broad knowledge), **SWE-bench** (fixing real code issues) | A test set built from your own users’ hardest requests |
| **Good for** | **Shortlisting** candidate models | Deciding whether a model is good enough to ship |
| **Weakness** | Often **saturated** or even **gamed** | Takes work to build |

<Note kind="beyond">
  “Saturated” means top models all score near the maximum, so the benchmark can no longer tell them apart. “Gamed”
  means models were tuned, deliberately or not, to do well on that specific test (for instance because test questions
  leaked into training data), so the score overstates real ability.
</Note>

Why do benchmarks get gamed? The book invokes **Goodhart’s Law**: *“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a
good measure.”* Frontier labs have a **heavy incentive to show new world-record benchmark scores** with each model
release, and that pressure erodes what the scores mean.

There are **better ways to gauge overall intelligence**, like **Elo ratings** based on head-to-head win rates against
other models (the same system used to rank chess players). But the book is clear: **there is no substitute for directly
measuring how a model performs for your application.**

### Tips for useful evals

The book’s three tips:

- **Look at your data.** Check eval results against your intuition for the product and problem space. If a score
  looks great but the outputs look wrong to you, trust your eyes and investigate.
- **Be precise.** Have a clear idea of the **hardest problems** the model needs to solve, and focus evaluation there.
  Easy cases don’t separate good models from great ones.
- **Use tools.** Evals are one of the fundamental problems in AI engineering; **don’t reinvent the wheel**. (The book’s
  recommended reading, on this site’s <a href="/reading">Further reading</a> page, lists tools and books on evals.)

## Fine-tuning for domain-specific quality

**Fine-tuning** is taking a **pre-trained foundation model** and adapting it to a specific use case by **introducing new
data**. As the chapter’s figure shows, fine-tuning **changes the values of the model weights while keeping the same
overall architecture**.

That last part is what makes fine-tuning so valuable for inference: a fine-tuned model is exactly as fast as the
original and can use **every optimization built for that architecture**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  A popular lightweight form of fine-tuning is **LoRA** (low-rank adaptation), which trains a small set of extra weights
  instead of changing all of them. LoRAs come back later in the course, because serving many LoRAs on one base model is
  a common production pattern.
</Note>

The payoff: **if you can fine-tune a small model to pass your evals, you set yourself up for an easier time hitting
your latency and cost targets.**

<Analogy>
  A general-purpose model is like a brilliant generalist consultant: expensive and able to do anything. Fine-tuning is
  like training a sharp junior employee to do *one* job really well. For that one job, they can match the consultant at a
  fraction of the cost.
</Analogy>

### The text-to-SQL example

The book’s example of a domain where fine-tuning shines is **translating English into SQL**, the language used to
query databases:

1. **General-purpose coding models are good at writing SQL**, but these models have **hundreds of billions of
   parameters**.
2. **SQL is a relatively constrained language**: a limited vocabulary of keywords and a strict structure.
3. So for an application that **only** needs to generate SQL from natural-language prompts, **a tiny fine-tuned model
   of just a few billion parameters can reach equivalent performance on this specific task.**

The book calls text-to-SQL an **extreme example**: many domains won’t support such a vast reduction in model size. But
it shows what’s possible when you have three ingredients:

- a **cleanly scoped domain**,
- a **strong set of evaluation criteria**, and
- **high-quality labeled data** to fine-tune on.

<Worked title="How much a fine-tune can save">
  Suppose a general coding model with **300B** parameters handles your text-to-SQL feature, and a fine-tuned **3B**
  model passes the same evals.

  1. In 16-bit precision (2 bytes per parameter), 300B parameters is about **600 GB** of weights; 3B is about **6 GB**.
  2. The big model needs a multi-GPU machine just to hold its weights. The small one fits comfortably on a single,
     cheaper GPU, with plenty of room left for serving many users at once.
  3. Since generating each token means reading the weights, the small model moves **100× less data per token**, on
     far cheaper hardware.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The sizes in this worked example are hypothetical, chosen to illustrate the book’s “hundreds of billions” versus “a
  few billion.” Real speedups are smaller than the raw ratio, because other costs (network, overheads, attention over
  long inputs) don’t shrink with the model.
</Note>

## Distillation

What if you could keep **most of a large model’s intelligence at a fraction of the size**? That’s the idea behind
**distillation**. As the chapter’s figure shows, distillation **preserves the behavior of a large model in a smaller,
more efficient one**.

In distillation, a large **teacher** model trains a smaller **student** model to **emulate the teacher’s behavior**. The
key difference from fine-tuning on synthetic data:

- **Fine-tuning on synthetic data** trains the student on **input-output pairs**: here’s a prompt, here’s the teacher’s
  final answer.
- **Distillation** shows the student the teacher’s **actual probability distributions**, not just its final answers.

<DistillCompare />

Why does the probability distribution matter? When a model picks its next token, it first assigns a probability to
*every* token in its vocabulary (Chapter 2 shows how). The final answer throws most of that away. The full distribution
carries much richer information: which alternatives the teacher considered nearly as good, which it ruled out
entirely, how confident it was. Learning from that lets a student absorb the teacher’s *way of thinking*, not just its
outputs.

| | Fine-tuning | Distillation |
|---|---|---|
| **Learns from** | Input → output pairs | The teacher model’s full probability distributions |
| **Teaches the model to** | Perform better **in a specific domain** | **Emulate the behavior** of a larger model, **good and bad** |
| **Real-world use** | Very common | **Substantially less common** |

### How labs use (and don’t use) distillation

A detail that surprises people: when a frontier lab releases a **family of models in different sizes**, the smaller
models are **generally not distilled** from the larger ones. They’re **trained independently**, to **prevent the biases
of the large model from artificially limiting the smaller models**. Distillation makes the most sense when a lab
**only trains one large model** and wants to make it more accessible.

### The DeepSeek-R1 distills

The book’s example: in **January 2025**, open model lab DeepSeek released its then-flagship reasoning model,
**DeepSeek-R1**. It was huge (**671B parameters**), so DeepSeek **also released distilled versions** built on the most
popular open architectures of the time: **Llama 3** and **Qwen 2.5**.

- The distills showed **similar reasoning behavior** to the full R1, albeit with **worse benchmark scores**.
- But they were **relatively small**, and because they used Llama and Qwen architectures, they could **take advantage of
  all the existing performance work** for those architectures. (This is the “stick with popular architectures” advice
  paying off.)
- At the book’s publication, these R1 distills were **still among the most popular distilled models on Hugging Face**,
  alongside distills of **Whisper** (audio transcription) and some image generation models.

<KeyPoints>
- On the same setup, **fewer parameters = faster and cheaper**, which makes model choice the single most important
  performance decision.
- Early on, use frontier APIs. At scale, find or create the **smallest model that’s smart enough**, on a **popular
  architecture** for the best tooling support.
- **Evals** let you spend effort wisely and set a **baseline** before quality-risking optimizations. Benchmarks are for
  shortlisting; they’re often saturated or gamed (**Goodhart’s Law**). Elo is better, but nothing beats your own evals.
- Eval tips: **look at your data, be precise about the hardest problems, use tools.**
- **Fine-tuning** changes weights, not architecture; with a scoped domain, strong evals, and good labeled data, a
  few-billion-parameter model can match a giant one (text-to-SQL).
- **Distillation** trains a student on a teacher’s **probability distributions**. It’s rarer than fine-tuning; model
  families are usually trained independently. The DeepSeek-R1 distills on Llama 3 and Qwen 2.5 are the classic example.
</KeyPoints>
