<TLDR>
  An **inference engine** is a ready-made, highly configurable model server. There are three serious options:
  **vLLM** (the most popular and the easiest), **SGLang** (flexible, and great for giant MoE models) and
  **TensorRT-LLM** (usually the fastest, but the hardest). **NVIDIA Dynamo** sits on top of any of them to run huge
  deployments across many GPUs.
</TLDR>

## Why inference engines exist

You *could* hand-write optimized PyTorch for every model. Inference engines save you from that. They ship with
pre-optimized components for common architectures, so instead of re-implementing the same things over and over, you
spend your time **testing combinations of techniques** through configuration. Their power comes from being
**configurable**.

They give great out-of-the-box performance for LLMs and for other kinds of model built on similar architectures
(Chapter 6). As of late 2025, **vLLM** (via *vLLM Omni*) and **SGLang** (via *SGLang Diffusion*) also started
supporting some **image and video generation** models. **TensorRT-LLM does not**; for those models you'd use TensorRT
or PyTorch directly (Chapter 6).

All three include the essentials out of the box, like
<Term def="Mixing new requests into the running batch token by token, instead of waiting for a whole batch to finish. Also called in-flight batching.">continuous batching</Term>,
and they support all the main speed-up techniques you'll meet in Chapter 5: quantization, speculative decoding, prefix
caching, parallelism and disaggregation.

## The three engines at a glance

| | vLLM | SGLang | TensorRT-LLM |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Performance** | Good | Good | Best |
| **Ease of use** | Easy | Easy | Hard |
| **Model support** | Most | Most | Some |
| **Hardware** | GPU, TPU | NVIDIA, AMD | NVIDIA only |
| **License** | Apache 2.0 | Apache 2.0 | Apache 2.0 |

In one sentence: **vLLM and SGLang are general, easy to adopt, and support new models from day zero; TensorRT-LLM has a
steeper learning curve but usually wins on performance.** At Baseten (where the author works) all three are used, with
TensorRT-LLM the most common. A good inference engineer knows all three and **chooses per deployment**.

## vLLM

**vLLM has the largest market share.** At the time the book was written, it had twice as many GitHub stars as SGLang
and TensorRT-LLM combined. (Stars are only a rough measure of popularity.)

- **History**: released in summer 2023, a few months before the others. Created at UC Berkeley, now hosted by the
  PyTorch project within the Linux Foundation.
- **Biggest strength: breadth.** It runs on the most hardware (NVIDIA, AMD and Intel GPUs, plus Google TPUs) and
  supports the most models. Nearly every open LLM works with vLLM from the day it's released.
- **Multimodal**: *vLLM Omni* extends it to image, audio and video inputs and outputs.
- **Setup**: `pip install`, or official Docker images with dependencies pre-bundled for different hardware.

You run it with the `vllm serve` command, passing settings as flags. For example, serving a model across 8 GPUs looks
something like this:

```bash
vllm serve <model-name> --tensor-parallel-size 8
```

<Note kind="deeper">
  A core principle of inference engineering: **the more constraints you accept, the more performance you can get.** A
  tool that runs everything everywhere can't specialize as deeply. Well configured, vLLM performs impressively, but in
  the author's experience it falls short of the very top end that a narrower tool like TensorRT-LLM reaches.
</Note>

**Use vLLM when:**
- You want a model server up quickly, with solid performance, for almost any open model.
- You're running an "omni" model with multiple input and output types.
- You're on a smaller GPU or an older architecture, where TensorRT-LLM offers little extra performance.

## SGLang

SGLang is the other big community-driven engine. It was released in **December 2023**, rose to prominence alongside
Chinese open models like **DeepSeek** and **Qwen**, and is the engine of choice at **xAI**.

- **Its unique angle**: a fast backend runtime paired with a flexible frontend language. In practice, you can swap
  out or customize **individual components** of the engine without rewriting everything else.
- **Hardware**: NVIDIA and AMD GPUs.
- **New models**: strong day-zero support. SGLang works directly with labs like DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and Z AI to ship
  optimized versions of new architectural features, such as DeepSeek's Multi-Latent Attention.
- **Big MoE at scale**: heavy investment in serving huge Mixture of Experts models across multiple machines, on systems
  like **GB200 NVL72**. These setups are extremely cost-efficient for large models with a lot of traffic.
- **Images and video**: *SGLang Diffusion* adds a **pipeline abstraction** that orchestrates the stages of an image or
  video model (a close match to how those models are built, as you saw in Chapter 2). It adds diffusion-specific
  parallelism and reuses SGLang's scheduler and optimized kernels.

You launch it with `sglang.launch_server`, again with flags:

```bash
python -m sglang.launch_server --model-path <model-name> --tp 8
```

**Use SGLang when:**
- You want excellent out-of-the-box throughput with decent latency on large MoE models like DeepSeek and Kimi.
- You want an inference-engine experience for image and video generation models.
- You want control and customization, and you're keen to take part in the SGLang community.

## TensorRT-LLM

TensorRT-LLM is **NVIDIA's open-source inference engine**, and of the three it offers the **highest performance** and
the **most flexibility for experts**.

<Note kind="warn">
  **The name is confusing.** There are two major versions, and only the old one is actually related to TensorRT:
  - **V0 (versions 0.x.y)** was a plugin that built a TensorRT engine for serving language models.
  - **V1 (versions 1.x.y)**, released in summer 2025, is a standalone package built on **PyTorch**, with no TensorRT
    dependency at all.

  Deployments of the old version are still common, so always check which one you're using.
</Note>

**Why it's the fastest**: it has access to kernels written by NVIDIA's own engineers, including some that are closed
source. These handwritten, hand-fused kernels have excellent support for the newest GPUs (Hopper, Blackwell) and for
NVIDIA-specific number formats like **NVFP4** (Chapter 5).

It has a robust implementation of **in-flight batching** (NVIDIA's name for continuous batching at the token level),
which helps throughput. It also supports just about every optimization setting you could ask for: quantization,
speculation algorithms, prefix caching,
<Term def="Splitting a long prompt’s prefill into smaller chunks so it can be interleaved with other requests’ decode steps.">chunked prefill</Term>,
flexible parallelism and disaggregation.

V1 feels a lot like vLLM and SGLang: a `trtllm-serve` command with flags, plus a `config.yaml` file for deeper
customization. The recommended way to install it is through one of NVIDIA's official Docker containers.

```bash
trtllm-serve <model-name> --tp_size 8 --extra_llm_api_options config.yaml
```

**Use TensorRT-LLM when:**
- You're running a well-supported model architecture on a **Hopper or newer** GPU.
- You're willing to do extra engineering work for the best possible performance.
- (Optionally) you plan to use NVIDIA Dynamo and want the most deeply integrated engine.

*The commands above are simplified sketches; the book shows full 8-GPU examples for each engine, and exact flags
change between versions, so check each engine's docs.*

## NVIDIA Dynamo: the layer on top

**NVIDIA Dynamo** is a **distributed system for model serving**, announced at NVIDIA's GTC conference in March 2025. It
doesn't replace inference engines. It **uses any of them (vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM) as backends** and adds an
orchestration layer for large-scale deployments.

<Analogy>
  If an inference engine is one very well-run restaurant kitchen, Dynamo is the **head office running a chain of
  kitchens**. It decides which kitchen gets each order, splits prep and cooking between specialized teams, and opens
  or closes stations as the dinner rush comes and goes.
</Analogy>

It supports three big techniques (each explained fully in Chapter 5):

- **KV cache re-use**: keeping KV cache data between requests and **routing requests to the machine that already has
  a matching prefix** cached.
- **Disaggregation**: running prefill and decode on **separate, individually optimized engines** that scale
  independently.
- **Multi-node parallelism**: optionally spreading one copy of a model across **two or more machines** of GPUs,
  usually with expert parallelism.

Because Dynamo sees what's happening across the whole deployment in real time, it can **adjust as traffic changes**.
For example, an **SLA-based planner** can automatically add or remove prefill and decode workers to meet the time to
first token and tokens-per-second targets you set.
(<Term def="Service Level Agreement: a promised performance or reliability target, such as ‘first token in under 500 ms’.">SLA</Term>
means the performance targets you've promised.)

### When Dynamo is (and isn't) worth it

A general principle: **the more scale you have, the more optimization tools become available.** Dynamo is built for
scale: big models, big traffic. It shines when serving foundation models like the **trillion-parameter Kimi** family
to huge numbers of concurrent users. For smaller models on large deployments, it can still give moderate gains.

It's a great choice if you're building an API for your own foundation model, or serving an open model inside a
high-traffic product. **But many deployments don't need it.** Unless you have enough volume for disaggregation and
KV-aware routing to matter, Dynamo is extra work and overhead, and you're better off using an inference engine
directly.

Dynamo is the newest project in this chapter and still gaining features. It's open source (Apache 2.0), with an active
community, public CI and support from NVIDIA engineers.

<KeyPoints>
- Inference engines are **configurable, pre-optimized model servers**. All three support the main Chapter 5 techniques.
- **vLLM**: most popular, easiest, broadest hardware and model support (including TPUs), plus vLLM Omni.
- **SGLang**: flexible and customizable, excellent for giant MoE models at scale, plus SGLang Diffusion for images and video.
- **TensorRT-LLM**: usually the fastest (NVIDIA's own kernels), NVIDIA-only, harder to use. V1 is PyTorch-based; V0 was a TensorRT plugin.
- **Dynamo** orchestrates engines at large scale (KV-aware routing, disaggregation, multi-node), but it's overkill for
  small deployments.
</KeyPoints>
