<TLDR>
  “Optimizing” only means something once you know what *best* means for **your** product. Before touching a GPU, pin
  down your model, your latency budget, your costs, and your traffic. Then decide whether to rent intelligence from an
  API or run your own deployment.
</TLDR>

## Optimization means trade-offs

To optimize is to pick the best option from a range of options. In inference that almost always means **trading off**
three things against each other: **latency** (how fast), **throughput** (how much, and so how cheap), and **quality**
(how smart). You rarely get to max out all three. The work is finding the right balance.

<Analogy title="Think of it like athletes">
  American football players are big, fast, and strong, but not as big as sumo wrestlers, as fast as Olympic sprinters,
  or as strong as champion powerlifters. Their bodies are tuned for the specific demands of their position over a
  whole season. The book’s first figure makes the same point: an inference system should be specialized for the demands
  of *its* workload, not be the best at everything.
</Analogy>

The more **constraints** you can pin down, the better you can do. Before optimizing, you should know your:

- **Model requirements**: which model (or models) you need to run.
- **Application interface**: how inputs reach the model, and what format the output needs to be in.
- **Latency budget**: end to end, how fast your product has to respond to a user action.
- **Unit economics**: what it makes sense to spend per request, per user, or per month.
- **Usage patterns**: how many people use it at the same time, and whether there are patterns (e.g. busier during
  business hours).

<Note kind="tip">
  Early on, you often won’t know these answers yet, and that’s fine. At that stage, just use **off-the-shelf APIs**.
  Inference engineering becomes worth it once the product is growing and the requirements become clear.
</Note>

## Shared vs. dedicated inference

There are two ways to put an AI model into your product:

- **Shared inference**: send requests to a public API that many customers use, and pay per million tokens (or some
  other usage-based price).
- **Dedicated deployment**: rent GPUs (or buy them and install them yourself, called *on-premises*) and run an
  inference service only for your app, paying per hour of GPU time.

This isn’t quite the same question as closed vs. open models. Plenty of shared APIs serve open models, and big
customers can often get some kind of dedicated setup for closed models. But one big reason people adopt open models is
that they make **unrestricted dedicated inference** possible.

Most AI products start on pay-per-token APIs, because the trade-offs suit a product that’s still finding its market:

| Shared inference: pros | Shared inference: cons |
|---|---|
| Nothing to manage; you only pay for what you use | Cost grows in a straight line with usage |
| No <Term def="The delay while a new copy of a model starts up and loads its weights before it can serve requests.">cold starts</Term>: the model is always ready | Your uptime can’t beat the provider’s, and other customers’ traffic (“noisy neighbors”) can slow you down |
| Almost no engineering; you just need an API key | No control over latency, model quality, or rate limits |

Over time, moving to dedicated deployments becomes essential for three reasons:

1. **Scale**: you have enough traffic that paying per GPU-hour is cheaper than paying per token.
2. **Specialization**: you run a custom or fine-tuned model, or you have strict latency or uptime needs.
3. **Orchestration**: your product chains several models into multi-step pipelines, and you need to cut the network
   delays and complexity of calling them all separately.

<CostBreakeven />

<Note kind="warn">
  Going dedicated makes you responsible for your own inference engineering. You get flexibility and control, but you
  also get more systems to run and a **higher minimum monthly bill**, since you pay for GPUs even when they’re idle.
  Only switch when there’s a clear, immediate business need.
</Note>

## Everything starts from your use case

Every inference decision comes downstream of what your app actually does.

<Analogy title="Think of it like a coach recruiting">
  Which students should a coach talk to? It depends entirely on the sport. The basketball coach wants the tallest kids
  in the class, and the gymnastics coach picks among the shortest. There’s no single “best” recruit, and there’s no
  single best inference setup.
</Analogy>

There are only two situations where you have to build for **every** possible use:

- **Foundation models**: you trained your own model and sell access through a public API, so you must handle all kinds
  of usage.
- **Inference platforms**: you’re building a platform (for your company or as a product) that has to run any model for
  any use case.

Most AI products aren’t like that. They’re **vertical** apps (a code editor, a customer-support agent) that use AI to
create a new experience. For those, you want to add **as many constraints as possible** by being specific about your
use case.

### Different apps, different priorities

Generative AI has created whole new categories of apps, and each one needs inference tuned differently:

| Category | Example | What matters most |
|---|---|---|
| **Agents** | A prospecting agent for sales teams | One user action triggers *many* model calls |
| **Chat** | Customer-support chat that looks up documents (<Term def="Retrieval-augmented generation: fetching relevant documents and adding them to the prompt so the model can answer from them.">RAG</Term>) | Fast time to first token makes chat *feel* fast |
| **Voice** | Real-time translation between languages | End-to-end latency, so conversation feels natural |
| **Media** | Virtual try-on for clothes, shoes, jewelry | Balancing output quality against speed |
| **Search** | Legal document discovery | Bulk indexing of documents (offline) vs. live user searches (online) |
| **RecSys** | E-commerce product recommendations | Consistent latency at very high request volume |
| **Completion** | Tab completion while coding | A full chunk of suggestion, delivered at typing speed |
| **Moderation** | Scanning user posts for safety | High throughput, so every check is cheap |

That’s a tiny sample, and as models get faster, cheaper, and smarter, entirely new use cases will appear.

## Online vs. offline

One of the most basic trade-offs is **latency vs. throughput**. Lower latency makes your app faster. Higher throughput
makes it cheaper at scale, because the same GPUs can serve more requests.

- **Online** apps run in real time with an impatient person waiting on every request: code completion, chat, voice
  agents. Optimize for **latency**.
- **Offline** (batch) jobs have nobody waiting on any single request. Optimize for **throughput**: each request may be
  too slow for a live user, but the system chews through far more work per hour. Examples:
  - **Catalog transcription**: transcribing a back catalog of podcasts or interviews so it’s searchable.
  - **Document processing**: embedding, converting, or analyzing batches of documents on a schedule.
  - **Corpus preparation**: cleaning or embedding huge datasets for training models.

The same model can do both jobs. Whisper (speech-to-text) could power a live dictation app *and* a bulk transcription
job. If both get enough traffic, it’s cheaper to run **two separate deployments** of the same model: one tuned for
latency, one tuned for throughput.

## Consumer vs. business (B2B)

| | Consumer apps | Business apps (B2B) |
|---|---|---|
| **Money** | Very cost-sensitive | Better margins |
| **Traffic** | Unpredictable; one viral launch can spike usage overnight | More stable |
| **Top priorities** | Low marginal cost and flexibility, with “decent” latency and uptime | High uptime and consistently low latency (it’s often in the customer’s revenue path) |
| **Secondary** | Latency and availability | Cost and scale |

### Compliance

For both kinds of apps, and especially in regulated industries, rules can limit where and how you run inference:

- **Data sovereignty**: are your GPUs in a region where you’re legally allowed to send your users’ data?
- **User privacy**: are the model’s inputs and outputs kept private and secure?
- **Regulatory compliance**: are you, and every provider you rely on, compliant with the relevant regulations?

Inference engineers need to work closely with security and legal experts to get this right.

<KeyPoints>
- Optimization is about **balancing latency, throughput, and quality** for *your* product. More constraints means
  better results.
- Know your model, interface, latency budget, unit economics, and usage patterns before optimizing.
- Start on **shared APIs**. Move to **dedicated deployments** for scale, specialization, or orchestration, and only
  when there’s a clear business need.
- **Online** apps optimize for latency; **offline** jobs optimize for throughput. One model can have two deployments.
- **Consumer** apps prioritize cost and flexibility; **B2B** apps prioritize uptime and consistent latency. Compliance
  applies to both.
</KeyPoints>
