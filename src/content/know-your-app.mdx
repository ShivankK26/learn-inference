<TLDR>
  “Optimizing” only means something once you know what *best* means for **your** product. Before touching a GPU, pin
  down your model, interface, latency budget, unit economics, and traffic. Then decide whether to rent intelligence from
  a shared API or run your own dedicated deployment, and shape everything around your use case: online or offline,
  consumer or business, and what compliance allows.
</TLDR>

## Optimization means trade-offs

Inference engineering adds **speed and scale** to AI products by optimizing how generative models are served in
production. And **optimization** has a precise meaning: *identifying the best solution out of a range of options*.

That raises the obvious question: best at *what*? Before you optimize model performance or build robust
infrastructure, you need to know what “best” means for your product. The book’s key observation is that **many
performance improvements come from making trade-offs** among three things:

- **Latency**: how fast each individual user gets a response.
- **Throughput**: how much total work the system does per second, which decides how many GPUs (and dollars) you need.
- **Quality**: how smart and accurate the model’s outputs are.

These pull against each other. A technique that raises throughput (like bigger batches) can slow each user down; a
technique that lowers latency or cost (like aggressive quantization) can cost some quality. So, in the book’s words,
**optimization is often about finding the right balance rather than maximizing a single factor.**

<Analogy title="Think of it like athletes">
  American football players are big, fast, and strong, but they’re not as big as sumo wrestlers, as fast as Olympic
  sprinters, or as strong as champion powerlifters. Their bodies and skills are optimized for the specific demands of
  their position over the course of a full season. The chapter’s first figure makes the same point: just like elite
  athletes, **inference services must be specialized for the demands of their workloads**, not be the best at
  everything.
</Analogy>

Your inference system must be optimized for the specific demands of **your model, your product, and your traffic**. And
here’s the counterintuitive part: **the more constraints you can introduce, the better outcomes you can achieve.** A
system that only has to do one thing, in one way, can be tuned much harder than one that has to handle anything.

### What to know before you optimize

The book lists five things you should know:

| Requirement | The question to answer |
|---|---|
| **Model requirements** | Which model (or models) do you need to run inference on? |
| **Application interface** | How will inputs be delivered to the model, and how is the output expected to be formatted? |
| **Latency budget** | End to end, how fast does your product need to respond to a user action? |
| **Unit economics** | What does it make sense to spend per request, per user, or per month? |
| **Usage patterns** | How many concurrent users are you serving, and is there a pattern (e.g. more activity during business hours)? |

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why each one matters later in the course:
  - **Model** decides the hardware (does it even fit on one GPU?) and which optimizations are available (Chapters 2–4).
  - **Interface** decides things like whether you stream tokens, whether output must be valid JSON, and which protocol
    you use (Chapters 2 and 7).
  - **Latency budget** decides how big your batches can be and whether you need latency techniques like speculation
    and tensor parallelism (Chapter 5).
  - **Unit economics** decides how much GPU capacity you can afford per user, and therefore how hard you must push
    throughput.
  - **Usage patterns** drive autoscaling: how many replicas to keep warm and when to add more (Chapter 7).
</Note>

<Note kind="tip">
  Early in building an AI product, the answers to these questions often **aren’t clear yet**, and that’s fine. At that
  stage, the book says it’s usually better to **use off-the-shelf APIs** whenever possible rather than investing in
  dedicated inference. As the product scales, the requirements become clear, and *then* inference engineering becomes
  a worthwhile pursuit.
</Note>

## Scale and specialization: shared vs. dedicated inference

There are two ways to add AI models to your product:

- **Shared inference**: send your traffic to a **public API endpoint** for a given model, and pay per million tokens
  (or some other consumption-based metric). Many customers share the same servers.
- **Dedicated deployments**: **rent GPUs** and set up an inference service **exclusively for your application**, paying
  per hour of GPU time. (Or purchase GPUs and install them yourself, called *on-premises*.)

### This isn’t the same as open vs. closed

It’s tempting to equate “shared” with closed models and “dedicated” with open ones, but the book points out they’re
separate questions:

- There are **plenty of shared endpoints for open models** (you can pay per token for Llama or DeepSeek from many
  providers).
- **Many model labs offer large customers some kind of dedicated setup** for their closed models.

Still, one of the key motivations for adopting open models is that they **unlock unrestricted dedicated inference**:
with the weights in hand, you can run them however and wherever you want.

### Why most products start shared

Most AI products start with pay-per-token APIs, because the trade-offs make sense while you’re **looking for
product-market fit** or in the **early stages of growth**:

| Shared inference: pros | Shared inference: cons |
|---|---|
| **Zero overhead**: you only pay for consumption | **Cost scales linearly** with usage |
| **No cold start times**: the model is always available | **Provider uptime caps your product’s uptime**, and you suffer **noisy neighbors** |
| **Minimal engineering work**: you just need an API key | **No control** over latency, model quality, or rate limits |

Each of these deserves a closer look:

- **Zero overhead vs. linear cost.** With an API you pay nothing when nobody uses your product, which is perfect when
  traffic is tiny or unpredictable. But every extra token costs exactly the same as the last one, forever. There’s no
  volume point where your marginal cost falls.
- **No cold starts.** A
  <Term def="The delay while a new copy of a model starts up and loads its weights before it can serve requests.">cold start</Term>
  happens when a new copy of a model has to boot up and load its weights before serving. A big shared API is always
  warm, so you never wait for that.
- **Uptime and noisy neighbors.** Your product can never be *more* available than the provider behind it. And because
  other customers share the same servers, their traffic spikes (“noisy neighbors”) can slow your requests down.
- **No control.** You get the provider’s latency, the provider’s version of the model, and the provider’s
  <Term def="Limits on how many requests or tokens you’re allowed to send per minute.">rate limits</Term>.

### Why products eventually go dedicated

Over time, the book says shifting to dedicated deployments becomes essential, for three reasons:

1. **Scale**: you process enough traffic that it’s **more economical to pay per GPU than per million tokens**.
2. **Specialization**: you’re running a **custom or fine-tuned model**, or you have **specific latency or uptime
   requirements** that a shared API can’t guarantee.
3. **Orchestration**: your product relies on **multiple models and multi-step pipelines**, and you need to
   **minimize network latency and deployment complexity** between them.

The first reason is simple arithmetic. Try it:

<Worked title="Where the break-even point comes from">
  Using the widget’s illustrative defaults: an API price of **$1 per million tokens**, a GPU rented at **$3 per hour**,
  and a GPU that can serve about **2,500 tokens per second** when well batched, busy about **50%** of the time.

  1. **Cost of one dedicated GPU per month**: 730 hours × $3 = **$2,190**, whether it’s busy or idle.
  2. **What that GPU can serve per month**: 2,500 tokens/s × 3,600 s/hour × 730 hours × 50% ≈ **3.3 billion tokens**.
  3. **What $2,190 buys on the shared API**: $2,190 ÷ $1 per million = **2.19 billion tokens**.
  4. So below about **2.2 billion tokens a month**, the API is cheaper (you’d be paying for an underused GPU). Above
     it, the dedicated GPU wins, and it keeps winning more as traffic grows, because each additional GPU serves billions
     more tokens for the same flat price.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  These prices and throughputs are illustrative, not from the book; real numbers vary widely by model, provider, and GPU.
  The *shape* is the point: shared cost is a straight line that starts at zero, while dedicated cost is a staircase that
  starts high but rises slowly. Utilization matters enormously. A GPU that sits idle half the day doubles your
  effective cost per token, which is why autoscaling (Chapter 7) is so important.
</Note>

### The cost of going dedicated

<Note kind="warn">
  Switching to dedicated deployments **puts you in charge of your own inference engineering**. That gives you
  flexibility and control, but it **adds to your engineering surface area** (more systems to build, monitor, and fix)
  and **increases the floor of your monthly spend** (you pay for GPUs even when they’re idle). The book’s advice: **only
  switch once there is a clear and immediate business need.**
</Note>

## Everything starts from your use case

**Every inference engineering decision is downstream of your use case.**

<Analogy title="Think of it like a coach recruiting">
  Imagine a sports coach recruiting at a school. Which students would they want to talk to? It depends entirely on
  which sport they coach: the basketball coach wants the tallest kids in the class, but the gymnastics coach selects
  among the shortest. There’s no single “best” recruit, and there’s no single best inference setup.
</Analogy>

### When you must build for everything

The book identifies only **two cases** where inference engineers need to build at the **highest level of generality**:

- **Foundation models**: you’ve trained your own model and will sell consumption directly through a **public shared
  API**, so you need to support **many different usage patterns**.
- **Inference platforms**: you’re building an inference platform, either internally or as a product, that needs to
  support **any model and any use case**.

### Everyone else: add constraints

Most AI-native applications are **vertical apps**, like code editors or customer service agents, where AI creates some
novel user experience. When building inference for these, the book’s advice is to **add as many constraints as
possible by getting specific with your use case.** You don’t need to be good at everything; you need to be excellent
at *your* workload.

### AI-native applications: different apps, different priorities

Generative models have unlocked a new class of applications across industries, and each category relies on different
models and modalities and needs **tailored inference**. The book’s table:

| Category | Example | Key consideration |
|---|---|---|
| **Agents** | Prospecting agent for sales teams | **One user action triggers many inference calls** |
| **Chat** | Front-line customer support chat with <Term def="Retrieval-augmented generation: fetching relevant documents and adding them to the prompt so the model can answer from them.">RAG</Term> | **Time to first token** makes chat feel fast |
| **Voice** | Real-time translation between languages | **End-to-end latency** for natural conversation |
| **Media** | Virtual try-on for clothes, shoes, and jewelry | Balancing **output quality vs. speed** |
| **Search** | Legal document discovery | **Offline** corpus filling vs. **online** user requests |
| **RecSys** | E-commerce product recommendations | **Consistent latency** with high request volume |
| **Completion** | Tab completion for coding in an IDE | A **full completion chunk at the user’s typing speed** |
| **Moderation** | Scanning user-generated content for safety | **High throughput** for cost-effective checks |

<Note kind="beyond">
  The reasoning behind a few of these:
  - **Agents**: if one click triggers ten model calls in sequence, each call’s latency adds up, so a delay that’s
    fine for a single chat reply becomes painful. Agents also multiply cost per user action.
  - **Chat**: people judge chat speed mostly by how quickly text *starts* appearing, which is time to first token
    (next lesson).
  - **Voice**: speech has no “loading” indicator. The whole pipeline (hearing, thinking, speaking) must finish fast
    enough to feel like a conversation, so end-to-end latency is what counts.
  - **Completion**: a code suggestion is only useful if it arrives, complete, before the user types the next
    character, so the whole chunk must land at typing speed.
  - **Moderation**: every piece of content must be checked, but nobody is waiting on any single check, so cost per
    check (throughput) dominates.
</Note>

The book stresses this is a **tiny sample** of the applications being built today, but it shows the breadth of
considerations inference engineers face. As models get faster, cheaper, and smarter, **new use cases that haven’t even
been imagined yet will emerge**.

## Online vs. offline

One of the primary trade-offs in inference engineering is **latency versus throughput**:

- **Lower latency** makes your application **faster**.
- **Higher throughput** makes it **cheaper at scale**, because you can use **fewer GPUs for the same number of users**.

### Online: optimize for latency

Most AI applications (code completion, chat, voice agents) are **online** applications that run in **real time**. With
an impatient user waiting on the other end of every API call, online applications should be **optimized for latency**.

### Offline: optimize for throughput

Some applications have **offline batch inference** needs. These jobs are better served by **high-throughput**
deployments: each individual request might be **too slow for a good user experience**, but the system as a whole
**processes far more requests per hour in parallel**. The book’s examples:

- **Catalog transcription**: transcribing a back catalog of podcasts, interviews, or other audio to make it searchable.
- **Document processing**: embedding, converting, or analyzing a set of documents on a regular schedule.
- **Corpus preparation**: cleaning, embedding, or otherwise preparing massive datasets for model training.

### One model, two deployments

A single model can serve both kinds of jobs. The book’s example is **Whisper**, a speech-to-text model: it could power
both a **real-time dictation app** and a **batch transcription job**. Assuming both use cases have **enough volume**,
it’s more cost-effective to run **two separate deployments of the same model**: one optimized for latency, the other
optimized for throughput.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why not one deployment for both? The settings that make a deployment fast for one user (small batches, spare
  capacity, sometimes extra GPUs per request) are the opposite of the settings that make it cheap per request (large
  batches, GPUs kept near 100% busy). Chapter 2’s bottlenecks lesson explains why batch size creates this trade-off.
  Mixing both workloads on one deployment means either the live users get slow, or the batch job pays latency-level
  prices.
</Note>

## Consumer vs. business (B2B)

Apps for consumers and apps for businesses have different inference needs:

| | Consumer apps | Business apps (B2B) |
|---|---|---|
| **Money** | Much more **cost-sensitive** | Often **better margins** |
| **Traffic** | **Less predictable**. Many are built for virality, so one launch or marketing campaign can spike usage overnight | **More stable** usage |
| **Performance bar** | Latency and availability at a **decent standard** | Mission-critical software in the **revenue path**, held to a high standard of performance and reliability |
| **Prioritize** | **Marginal cost and flexibility** | **Latency and uptime** |
| **Secondary** | Latency and availability | Cost and scale |

<Note kind="beyond">
  “Marginal cost” is the cost of serving *one more* request or user. For a consumer app with millions of free users,
  even a fraction of a cent per request decides whether the business works. “Flexibility” means being able to scale up
  and down fast when a viral moment hits and fades.
</Note>

### Compliance

In both consumer and business applications, and especially in **regulated industries**, compliance can **limit your
infrastructure options**. The essential questions:

- **Data sovereignty**: are your GPUs in a **geographic region where you’re allowed to send user data**?
- **User privacy**: are the inputs and outputs of your model **kept private and secure**?
- **Regulatory compliance**: are **you and your underlying providers** compliant with all relevant regulations?

Inference engineers must **work closely with security and legal experts** to make sure the infrastructure they operate
is compliant. (Compliance comes back in Chapter 7, where it shapes multi-cloud decisions.)

<KeyPoints>
- Optimization is about **balancing latency, throughput, and quality** for *your* product, not maximizing one factor.
  More constraints means better results.
- Know your **model, interface, latency budget, unit economics, and usage patterns** before optimizing.
- **Shared** inference: no overhead or cold starts, minimal engineering, but linear cost, capped uptime, noisy
  neighbors, no control. **Dedicated**: control and lower cost at scale, but a higher spend floor and more engineering.
- Go dedicated for **scale, specialization, or orchestration**, and only with a clear business need.
- Build for generality only for **foundation models** or **inference platforms**; vertical apps should add constraints.
- **Online** apps optimize for latency; **offline** jobs for throughput. One model can justify two deployments.
- **Consumer**: marginal cost and flexibility. **B2B**: latency and uptime. **Compliance** (data sovereignty, privacy,
  regulation) applies to both.
</KeyPoints>
