## Testing before you deploy

Chapter 4 covered benchmarking a single replica. Before you deploy, you also need to test the **whole system, end to
end**. There are three main ways:

- **Manual testing**: writing scripts (or clicking buttons) to send made-up traffic to the service.
- **Load testing**: automatically blasting the system with lots of traffic to see if it scales and stays fast.
- **Shadow traffic**: **copying real, live traffic** to the new deployment in the background, so you see how it performs
  on real-world requests without users ever getting its answers.

Testing inference is **expensive**. Setting up tests and reading the results takes engineering time, and the test
traffic needs real GPUs. Some of that is just the cost of doing business, but be thoughtful. For example, you could
start by shadowing a **random sample** of production traffic, then follow with a **shorter** load test.

<Note kind="tip">
  AI usage rises and falls on **daily and weekly cycles**. A test run on a quiet Sunday night can look very different
  from Monday at noon, so keep the calendar in mind when you plan and read your tests.
</Note>

## Deploying without downtime

Once you're confident the new version works, you need to swap it in without an outage.

**Blue-green deployment** is the traditional approach. You run two identical environments: the current one (**blue**)
and one with the new version (**green**). When green is ready, *all* traffic switches over at once, and blue stays
around so you can roll back if something goes wrong.

The problem at inference scale is GPUs. If blue runs on 100 GPUs, green needs **another 100 GPUs** before you can switch.
That's exactly the capacity and cost problem that makes large-scale testing hard in the first place.

**Canary deployment** gets similar safety with far fewer extra GPUs. The name comes from the canaries coal miners
carried to detect dangerous gas: a canary deploy **catches problems before they reach most users**. It works in four
steps:

1. **Build** the new deployment and get it ready to take requests.
2. **Send a small slice** of live traffic to it.
3. **Watch it closely.** If anything looks wrong, revert.
4. **Gradually increase** its share while you keep watching, until it handles 100% of traffic.

The book's diagram shows traffic shifting over in steps like this, so an issue shows up while only a few users are on
the new version.

<CanaryDeploy />

Canary rollouts can be quick (a few minutes of ramp) or slow and careful. And with autoscaling, they **barely cost
extra**: as traffic moves off the old deployment, it scales itself down.

<Note kind="warn">
  With autoscaling, a new deployment with no traffic sits at its **minimum** replica count. Before you shift traffic
  onto it, make sure it has **enough warm replicas** to handle that traffic. Otherwise users see a latency spike while
  their requests queue and autoscaling catches up.
</Note>

## Estimating cost

Moving from a public API to your own GPUs changes how you have to think about money.

**API cost is simple**: a price per million tokens times the tokens you use. There are a few wrinkles (cached input
tokens are cheaper than uncached ones, and big customers get discounts), but cost still grows **in a straight line**
with usage.

One big reason to invest in inference engineering is to **take control of your unit economics**: to escape per-token
pricing. But it's a hard mental switch, because with dedicated GPUs, cost depends on many things at once:

- **Batch sizing**: is the deployment tuned for latency (small batches) or throughput (big batches)?
- **Traffic patterns**: are your GPUs busy all the time, or often sitting partly idle?
- **Sequence lengths**: how many input and output tokens do requests have, on average *and* in the extreme cases?

Because of all that, and because input and output tokens are priced differently, the book advises **not** trying to
reverse-engineer a per-token price for your GPUs. Instead, **turn your token bill into a total cost, and compare it with
the total cost of running dedicated GPUs.** The book gives an equation for each side. In plain terms:

```text
API total        ≈ (input tokens × input price)
                 + (output tokens × output price)      ← per million tokens, summed over the period

Dedicated total  ≈ number of GPUs × price per GPU-hour × hours in the period
```

Two more rules of thumb:

- **Use a long time window**, ideally **at least a week**, to smooth out the ups and downs of usage.
- **Include engineering time.** Building and running your own inference takes people. That investment buys
  reliability, security, and control, but it belongs in the bill. GPUs plus engineering gives you the **total cost of
  ownership** (<Term def="Total cost of ownership: everything it really costs to run something, including hardware and the people who build and maintain it.">TCO</Term>).

## Observability: watching it run

Inference is mission-critical, so it deserves the same **alerts, logs, and dashboards** as any other critical part of
your app. Here's what to measure:

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Total volume** | How many requests the deployment is receiving |
| **Request and response sizes** | Input and output sequence lengths |
| **Response codes** | Counts of 2XX (success), 4XX (bad request), and 5XX (server error) responses |
| **Latency** | Time to first token, tokens per second, and end-to-end latency at P50, P90, and P99 |
| **Replica count** | How many replicas are serving, and how many are still starting up |
| **Utilization** | How busy the CPU, host memory, GPU, and GPU memory are |
| **Queue depth** | For async traffic, how many requests are waiting |

These metrics **depend on each other**, which is why you want them side by side. A latency spike might come from a flood
of requests, or it might come from a few very long inputs. Seeing volume, sequence sizes, and latency together tells
you not just *what* happened but *why*.

When something breaks, you need details fast. **Logs** deliver them in real time: **server logs** (what the service is
doing) and **audit logs** (who changed what, and when).

And don't build it as an island. Plug inference monitoring into the tools the rest of your app already uses, like
**Grafana, Datadog, PagerDuty, or Sentry**, so inference problems show up in context.

## The client side

There's one area of inference engineering that often gets forgotten: the **client code**. Every call has two sides:

- The **client**: the browser, agent, or app making the request.
- The **server**: the inference service that answers it.

The industry-standard client is the **OpenAI SDK**, which works with many compatible providers, not just OpenAI. Popular
frameworks like **LangChain, Vercel AI SDK, LiteLLM, and LlamaIndex** can act as clients too. Whether you use one of
those or write your own, the client can add latency or become a throughput bottleneck. And real-time apps may need
something other than plain HTTP, like **WebSockets**, to keep a connection open.

The book's diagram makes the key point: **the time the model spends on the server is only part of the total latency a
user experiences.**

### Re-use your connections

Setting up a secure connection between client and server (the <Term def="The back-and-forth a client and server do to set up an encrypted connection before any real data flows.">TLS handshake</Term>)
takes **a few dozen milliseconds**, depending on the user's connection and the protocol.

In a fast system with a **300 ms** latency target (at P95), a handshake alone eats **at least 10%** of the budget
before inference even starts. So later requests from the same client should **re-use the existing session**. The
OpenAI SDK already does this quietly under the hood, but if you build your own client (common for unusual modalities
like audio), follow the same practice.

### Asynchronous inference

Some systems care about **throughput, not latency**. Processing a huge pile of documents or embedding an entire corpus
doesn't need an instant answer. For these, switch to **asynchronous** jobs: "fire and forget."

- A normal, **synchronous** request has a **timeout**, usually a few minutes, after which it fails.
- An **asynchronous** request is acknowledged immediately. When the result is ready, it's delivered to a
  <Term def="A URL you give the service ahead of time; it calls that URL to hand you the result when the job is done.">webhook</Term>
  you provided in the original request.

Async jobs still have time limits, but they're measured in **hours**, not minutes. Combined with good server-side
queueing, this makes big, latency-insensitive workloads sturdier and more efficient.

### Streaming, WebSockets, and gRPC

**Streaming** makes an app feel instant. Tick the streaming box above: the user starts reading after prefill instead of
waiting for the last token. For **text** from a language model, streaming over ordinary **HTTP** is enough.

But live **voice and video** need a lot more data flowing in **both directions** at once. A one-off HTTP request and
response suits text chat, but not a continuous stream. The two most common protocols for two-way streaming are:

| Protocol | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **WebSockets** | Streaming unstructured, real-time data, like audio | Keeps a continuous connection open. Each server accepts a fixed, configurable number of clients; when it's full, new connections wait for a free slot or a new replica. |
| **gRPC** | Well-defined service-to-service communication | Also streams both ways, but for **structured** data. Messages must follow a predefined schema, so the server doesn't have to parse free-form input. That extra validation makes it slightly slower than WebSockets. |

<KeyPoints>
- Test end to end with **manual tests, load tests, and shadow traffic**, and keep the cost of testing in check.
- **Blue-green** doubles your GPUs during a switch. **Canary** rollouts shift traffic gradually and catch bugs early,
  at little extra cost, as long as the new side has enough warm replicas.
- Compare **total cost** (API bill vs. GPUs × hours), over at least a week, **plus engineering time** (TCO).
- Monitor volume, sizes, response codes, latency percentiles, replicas, utilization, and queue depth **together**,
  alongside logs, in your existing tools.
- Client matters: **re-use sessions**, use **async jobs** for bulk work, and **stream** results. Use **WebSockets** for
  unstructured real-time data and **gRPC** for structured service-to-service traffic.
</KeyPoints>
