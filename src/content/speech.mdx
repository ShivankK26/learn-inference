<TLDR>
  Speech models are small and already fast, so the big wins come from **how you run them**, not from the model itself.
  For **transcription**, that means streaming for live audio and **chopping long files into chunks run in parallel**.
  For **text-to-speech**, the goal isn’t maximum speed but **as many real-time voices per GPU as possible**.
</TLDR>

## Speech to text (ASR)

**Automatic speech recognition** (ASR) models take audio in and produce text out. They power transcription and
dictation apps. The most popular open one is **Whisper**, released by OpenAI, which handles dozens of languages
accurately.

Whisper is small by modern standards: the largest, highest-quality version is only **1.55B parameters**. It runs so
fast that it fits comfortably on **a slice of a big GPU**, using [Multi-Instance GPU (MIG)](/learn/instances) to split
one H100 into several smaller GPUs. Lots of smaller variants, distillations and quantizations exist, but in practice
the top-quality models, **Whisper 3 Large** and **Whisper 3 Turbo**, already meet most latency budgets.

Whisper is an **encoder-decoder** model (remember [Chapter 2](/learn/neural-networks)):

- **Encoder**: takes the audio, converted into a <Term def="A picture of sound: how much energy there is at each pitch over time, scaled to match how human hearing works.">log-Mel spectrogram</Term>,
  and turns it into audio features.
- **Decoder**: turns those features into text tokens.

**Almost all the time is spent in the decoder**, which is an autoregressive transformer very much like an LLM. That’s
good news, because LLM tools apply. The main one is **TensorRT-LLM**, which gives the decoder in-flight batching, an
optimized C++ runtime, and efficient CUDA kernels. It works especially well on newer **Hopper** and **Blackwell** GPUs,
which makes MIG slices of those GPUs an even better fit.

### Live transcription: one chunk at a time

For real-time uses like dictation or voice agents, what matters is the **round trip for one chunk of audio**. A great
target is **200 milliseconds**, about the average human reaction time.

With Whisper on an optimized TensorRT-LLM engine, there’s little left to gain in the runtime. The gains come from
**orchestration and infrastructure**, and the biggest one is **streaming**:

- Instead of uploading a finished recording, the app opens a <Term def="A connection that stays open so both sides can keep sending data continuously, instead of one request and one response.">WebSocket</Term>
  (covered in [Chapter 7](/learn/shipping)), streams audio in continuously, and gets text back continuously.
- Streaming is handled in the **API server**, not the model. A small <Term def="Voice activity detection: a tiny model that detects when someone is speaking versus silence.">VAD</Term>
  model watches the incoming audio and cuts it into chunks. Whisper transcribes each chunk as usual, and the text
  streams back over the WebSocket.

This setup can serve several streams at once, and it keeps transcription **in order**. When every chunk from a stream
goes to the same GPU, the **previous chunk’s text can be fed in as a prefix** for the next one, which improves accuracy.

### Long files: go wide

Whisper can only process **30 seconds of audio at a time**, so an hour-long podcast needs a different approach.

Long-file speed is measured with the confusingly named **real-time factor** (RTF): how many times faster than real time
you transcribe. If the world’s fastest typist could transcribe an hour of audio in 30 minutes, that’s an RTF of **2×**.
An optimized Whisper deployment can do an hour in **under four seconds**, an RTF of **1000×**.

It takes a multi-step pipeline (the book’s figure shows it as two stages):

1. **Chunk it smartly.** A VAD model, on its own hardware, **removes the silence** and cuts the audio into
   meaningful segments. Splitting on fixed time intervals would risk **cutting words in half**.
2. **Transcribe the chunks in parallel.** Spread them across many GPUs (or MIG slices), each running several chunks at
   once with in-flight batching. **RTF improves roughly linearly with the number of GPUs.**
3. **Stitch** the pieces back together by timestamp.

Going parallel means you **lose the trick of feeding the previous chunk as a prefix**. But there are quality tricks
that more than make up for it. ASR models sometimes **hallucinate**, for example by repeating a phrase over and over.
You can detect that automatically by checking the output’s **compression ratio** (repetitive text compresses
suspiciously well) and its **words per minute** (too many words for the audio length is a red flag). When a chunk
looks bad:

1. **Re-run it at a higher temperature.** This is counterintuitive, since higher temperature usually means *more*
   randomness, but here the point is to **break the repetition loop** and get a different output.
2. **Re-chunk** the audio (or just that part) into smaller pieces and transcribe again.

In practice these fixes remove the need for prefix passing, so you get transcription that’s both fast and accurate.

### Who said what: diarization

<Term def="Labeling a transcript with who is speaking when.">Diarization</Term> means annotating a transcript with
**who is speaking when**. It’s a neighboring problem, and a completely different kind of model. Where Whisper is a
transformer, diarization systems like **pyannote audio** are **pipelines of classic ML models**:

1. **Segmentation**: find where speech changes.
2. **Embedding**: capture each segment’s voice characteristics.
3. **Clustering**: group segments by speaker across the whole file.

Optimizing it means making each model fast and orchestrating the pipeline well, using tools like PyTorch, pyannote,
and **Torch compilation**. Even so, highly optimized diarization still takes **at least twice as long** as
transcribing the same file.

## Text to speech (TTS)

**Text-to-speech** (or *speech synthesis*) models take text in and produce spoken audio. In 2025, open models like
**Orpheus TTS** brought extremely lifelike voices to open source, and many companies fine-tuned it for better quality
and their own branded voices, which drove wide adoption of open models in voice AI.

**Modern TTS models are fine-tuned LLMs.** Orpheus is built from **Llama 3.2 3B**, so the LLM playbook applies again.
They’re small (3B is on the large end), so like ASR, **MIG slices of H100s** run them efficiently. Unlike ASR models,
which usually run in FP16, TTS models can have their **weights and KV cache quantized to FP8**, on top of TensorRT-LLM’s
optimized kernels and in-flight batching.

### How an LLM learns to talk

To turn an LLM into a TTS model, its **vocabulary is expanded with tens of thousands of audio tokens**, and it’s trained
on pairs of text and tokenized audio. The model then “writes” audio tokens the way a chatbot writes words.

That means you also need an **audio decoder** that turns those tokens into an actual sound wave (for Orpheus that’s a
decoder called **SNAC**). This step can become a bottleneck. The book’s advice:

- Implement it in **PyTorch** and **compile** it for the target GPU.
- Use **dynamic batching with a short timeout** (around **15 milliseconds**): wait briefly to gather work, then run
  it. **In-flight batching isn’t possible** for the audio decoder.

<Analogy>
  The TTS model is like a **composer writing sheet music**, and the audio decoder is the **musician who actually plays
  it**. A brilliant composer doesn’t help if the musician can’t keep up.
</Analogy>

### Measuring speech speed

| Metric | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **TTFB** (time to first byte) | The speech version of time to first token: how soon any audio arrives. |
| **Time to first sentence** | A more user-centered measure: how soon a meaningful phrase is ready. |
| **TPS** (tokens per second) | Decode speed, just like an LLM. |

Lower TTFB is always better. Orpheus can get as low as **150 milliseconds** on a single H100.

But TPS works differently from LLMs. Audio only needs to be generated **as fast as it plays**, which depending on the
model takes about **80 to 100 tokens per second**. Anything faster is wasted. So instead of making one voice faster,
you use the extra performance to **serve more voices at once**. The more real-time streams one GPU can handle, the
cheaper each user gets.

### Streaming TTS in production

Almost all TTS is real-time, and again the runtime is already tuned (TensorRT-LLM, quantization, a compiled SNAC
decoder), so the remaining gains are in **infrastructure**. As with ASR, **streaming over WebSockets** is the biggest
unlock compared to sending text and getting audio back in chunks. Test your engine to find **how many concurrent
real-time streams it can sustain**, then set your **batch size and number of active WebSockets** to match, which keeps
the GPU busy but stable.

<Note kind="warn">
  If you ever use TTS in bulk, like converting a library of documents to audio for accessibility, remember that TTS
  models **struggle with long inputs**. Speech quality starts to degrade after **about 30 seconds**, so split the text.
</Note>

## Speech to speech

A fast-moving research area is **speech-to-speech** models: audio in, audio out.

Today, most voice apps use a **cascade**, a pipeline where an **ASR model listens**, an **LLM thinks**, and a **TTS
model responds**, with helpers like VAD and embedding models to make conversation feel natural and add context. The
book’s figure shows this multi-model pipeline.

Speech-to-speech models like OpenAI’s **gpt-realtime** instead give one core LLM the ability to hear and speak
directly, merging the pipeline into **a single model**. That’s possible because ASR, LLMs and TTS share such similar
architectures, especially on the decoder side.

As of the book’s writing (January 2026), there were **no commercially viable open speech-to-speech models**, and closed
ones like gpt-realtime were **less capable and more expensive** than a good cascade. But research is active, and this
modality will soon need its own flavor of inference engineering.

<KeyPoints>
- **Whisper** (≤1.55B params) is encoder-decoder. The **decoder** is the bottleneck, so optimize it with **TensorRT-LLM**, often on **MIG** slices.
- Live ASR: target **~200 ms** per chunk. **Streaming** via WebSockets + a **VAD** model is the big win, and same-GPU chunks can pass the previous text as a prefix.
- Long files: VAD chunking → **parallel** transcription across GPUs → stitch by timestamp. **RTF** grows roughly linearly with GPUs (1000× is achievable).
- Catch hallucinations via **compression ratio** and **words per minute**, then re-run hotter or re-chunk.
- **Diarization** is a classic-ML pipeline (segment → embed → cluster) and takes ≥2× as long as transcription.
- **TTS** = a fine-tuned LLM + an **audio decoder** (dynamic batching, ~15 ms timeout). Measure **TTFB** (~150 ms possible) and time to first sentence.
- TTS only needs **~80–100 tokens/s** per stream, so scale **concurrent streams**, not speed.
- **Speech-to-speech** merges the ASR → LLM → TTS cascade into one model, but cascades still win today.
</KeyPoints>
