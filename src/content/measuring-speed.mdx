<TLDR>
  For LLMs, speed comes down to two numbers: **TTFT** (how long until the first token) and **TPS** (how fast tokens
  stream after that). But *how* you measure matters just as much: say whether TPS means **one user** or **the whole
  service**, look at **percentiles** instead of averages, and separate **time on the GPU** from **what the user actually
  experiences**.
</TLDR>

## The two headline metrics

The two most common performance metrics for LLMs are **TTFT** (time to first token) and **TPS** (tokens per second).
(Other modalities, like speech and image generation, have their own metrics; those are covered in Chapter 6.)

| | Time to first token (TTFT) | Tokens per second (TPS) |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | With streaming output, how long does it take for a user to see the **first** output token? | How many tokens each second does the user receive **after** the first token is generated? |
| **Driven by** | <Compute>Compute-bound prefill</Compute> | <Memory>Bandwidth-bound decode</Memory> |
| **Better is** | **Lower** TTFT = better latency | **Higher** TPS = better latency |

The chapter’s figure shows a timeline of one streamed response: a gap before the first token appears (that gap is
TTFT), followed by a steady stream of tokens whose pace is the TPS.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why prefill and decode? An LLM answers in two phases. **Prefill** reads your entire prompt at once, which is mostly
  heavy math, so it’s limited by the GPU’s compute. **Decode** then writes the answer one token at a time, and each
  token requires reading the model’s weights from memory, so it’s limited by memory bandwidth. Chapter 2 builds both
  ideas from scratch; for now, just connect **TTFT ↔ prefill** and **TPS ↔ decode**.
</Note>

### “TPS” can mean two different things

TTFT is a clear term, but **TPS is less precise**. It can be a **latency** metric (tokens per second *per user*) or a
**throughput** metric (tokens per second for the *entire inference service*). Most people use TPS to mean the per-user
latency metric. When precision matters, the book recommends more specific terms:

- **Perceived TPS**: the observed tokens per second **per user**, after the first token. *(Latency.)*
- **Total TPS**: the total number of tokens generated each second **by the whole inference service**. *(Throughput.)*
- **Inter-token latency (ITL)**: the **time between subsequent tokens**. An ITL of **10 milliseconds** equates to **100
  tokens per second** per user.

<Worked title="Converting between ITL, TPS, and total throughput">
  1. **ITL → perceived TPS**: TPS = 1,000 ms ÷ ITL. So ITL 10 ms → 1,000 ÷ 10 = **100 TPS**; ITL 25 ms → **40 TPS**;
     ITL 2 ms → **500 TPS**.
  2. **Perceived TPS → total TPS**: if a server is streaming to **64 users at once**, each at **40 TPS**, the service’s
     total throughput is 64 × 40 = **2,560 tokens per second**.
  3. **Same service, two stories**: that server can honestly be described as “40 TPS” (latency) or “2,560 TPS”
     (throughput). This is exactly why the book asks you to say *perceived* or *total*.
</Worked>

### When TTFT and TPS aren’t the right metric

TTFT and TPS are most useful for **user-facing systems where output is streamed**, like chatbots: the user reads text
as it arrives, so both the wait and the pace matter. For other requests, such as **a tool call made by an agent**, the
individual tokens aren’t useful on their own. The next step can’t start until the whole output is ready, so you
measure latency as **total response time** instead.

<Worked title="Estimating total response time from TTFT and ITL">
  A request has a **TTFT of 300 ms**, an **ITL of 20 ms**, and produces **200 output tokens**.

  1. The first token arrives at **300 ms**.
  2. The remaining 199 tokens each take about 20 ms: 199 × 20 ≈ **3,980 ms**.
  3. Total response time ≈ 300 + 3,980 ≈ **4.3 seconds**.

  For a streaming chatbot, the user starts reading after 0.3 s and it feels fast. For an agent’s tool call, nothing can
  happen for 4.3 s. Same model, very different experience, which is why the metric must match the use case.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The formula total ≈ TTFT + (output tokens − 1) × ITL is a standard approximation, not from the book. It also shows
  which lever matters: for long outputs, ITL (decode speed) dominates; for short outputs with long prompts, TTFT
  (prefill speed) dominates.
</Note>

## Latency percentiles: why averages lie

The naive approach is to look at the **average** (mean) TTFT or TPS. The book warns this doesn’t tell the whole story,
because **LLM total response time is generally a right-skewed distribution**: most times concentrate around a
<Term def="The most common value in a distribution: the peak of the histogram.">mode</Term>, but **outliers can take
significantly longer**. As the chapter’s figure shows, those outliers pull the **mean above the median (P50)**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  A distribution is *right-skewed* when it has a long tail stretching to the right: many fast requests, a few very slow
  ones. For LLMs, slow outliers come from things like unusually long prompts or outputs, requests that land in a queue
  during a traffic spike, or a server that’s momentarily overloaded. Averages are especially misleading for skewed
  data, because a handful of huge values drags the mean up while telling you nothing about the typical case, or about
  how bad the bad cases are.
</Note>

Why care about outliers? Because they **dramatically affect user experience and trust**. In the book’s words, it’s not
good enough for most interactions to feel snappy **if one in every ten takes several seconds**.

So inference engineers measure latency in **percentiles**:

| Percentile | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **P50** | Median latency | 1 in every 2 requests is slower |
| **P90** | 90th-percentile latency | 1 in every 10 requests is slower |
| **P95** | 95th-percentile latency | 1 in every 20 requests is slower |
| **P99** | 99th-percentile latency | 1 in every 100 requests is slower |

<Worked title="Computing percentiles from real measurements">
  You measure the total response time of 20 requests and sort them (in seconds):

  0.8, 0.9, 0.9, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.1, 1.1, 1.1, 1.2, 1.2, 1.2, 1.3, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5, 1.7, 2.1, 3.8, 6.0

  1. **Mean**: the sum is 31.6 s, so the mean is 31.6 ÷ 20 = **1.58 s**.
  2. **P50 (median)**: halfway through the sorted list, between the 10th and 11th values (1.2 and 1.2): **1.2 s**.
  3. **P90**: 90% of 20 is 18, so P90 is around the 18th value: **2.1 s**.
  4. **P95**: 95% of 20 is 19, so P95 is around the 19th value: **3.8 s**.
  5. Notice the **mean (1.58 s) is higher than the median (1.2 s)**, even though 16 of the 20 requests were faster than
     the mean. Two outliers (3.8 s and 6.0 s) did that. The mean describes almost nobody’s actual experience.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  There are a few slightly different conventions for computing percentiles from samples (whether to interpolate between
  neighbors, for example). They give nearly identical results with thousands of measurements, which is what you should
  have in practice. With only 20 samples, P99 isn’t meaningful at all: you need at least hundreds of requests to see
  one-in-a-hundred behavior.
</Note>

### Why the tail matters more than it looks

<Worked title="How often does a user hit a slow request?">
  Suppose your P99 is terrible, so **1% of requests** are painfully slow.

  1. **At scale**: at 1,000,000 requests a day, 1% is **10,000 bad experiences every day**.
  2. **Per user**: if a typical session makes 20 requests, the chance that *all 20* are fine is 0.99²⁰ ≈ 0.82. So about
     **18% of sessions** include at least one slow request.
  3. **For agents**: if one user action triggers 20 model calls *in sequence*, the action is only as fast as its
     slowest call, so the tail latency of each call becomes the typical latency of the action.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  This compounding effect is a standard result from probability, not from the book, but it’s the reason the book says
  good performance work must go beyond the average: **driving down average latency matters, but good performance work
  also focuses on reducing P90/P99 latencies for a more reliable user experience.**
</Note>

## Inference time vs. end-to-end time

The other important distinction is **what you’re timing**:

- **Inference time**: solely the **on-GPU time required to generate tokens**.
- **End-to-end time**: a measurement that also accounts for **network latency** (the request traveling to the server
  and the response traveling back) and any **queue time** (waiting for a free spot on a GPU).

**Both are valuable.** Inference time tells you **how effective your model performance work is**. End-to-end metrics
reveal **your users’ perception** of how fast your application is.

<LatencyBreakdown />

<Worked title="Diagnosing where the time goes">
  Users report that responses feel slow. You measure:

  1. **End-to-end TTFT** (what users see): **1,500 ms**.
  2. **Inference-only TTFT** (on the GPU): **200 ms**.
  3. So **1,300 ms**, almost 90% of the wait, is happening *outside* the model: in the network, a queue, or routing.

  Making the model’s prefill twice as fast would save only 100 ms. Fixing the infrastructure could save more than a
  second.
</Worked>

<Note kind="warn">
  The book’s rule: **when inference time is fast but end-to-end time is slow, turn your attention to infrastructure
  rather than model performance optimization.** Chapter 7 covers the infrastructure side: routing, queueing,
  autoscaling, and running close to users.
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- **TTFT** = wait before the first token (compute-bound prefill). **TPS** = streaming speed after that
  (bandwidth-bound decode). Lower TTFT and higher TPS are better.
- Say **perceived TPS** (per user, latency) or **total TPS** (whole service, throughput). **ITL** = time between tokens;
  TPS = 1,000 ÷ ITL in ms, so 10 ms ITL = 100 TPS.
- For non-streamed outputs like agent tool calls, measure **total response time**.
- LLM latency is **right-skewed**, so the **mean sits above the median** and hides outliers. Track **P50, P90, P95,
  P99**, and work on the tail, not just the average.
- Measure both **inference-only** (is my model work effective?) and **end-to-end** (what do users feel?). A big gap
  between them points at **infrastructure**.
</KeyPoints>
