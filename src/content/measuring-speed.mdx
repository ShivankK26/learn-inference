<TLDR>
  For LLMs, speed comes down to two numbers: **TTFT** (how long until the first token) and **TPS** (how fast tokens
  stream after that). But *how* you measure matters just as much. Look at **percentiles**, not averages, and separate
  **time on the GPU** from **what the user actually experiences**.
</TLDR>

## The two headline metrics

You met these in the last chapter. Here they are side by side:

| | Time to first token (TTFT) | Tokens per second (TPS) |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | With streaming, how long until the user sees the *first* token? | After the first token, how many tokens per second does the user get? |
| **Driven by** | <Compute>Compute-bound prefill</Compute> | <Memory>Bandwidth-bound decode</Memory> |
| **Better is** | Lower | Higher |

The book’s figure shows a timeline: a gap before the first token appears (TTFT), then a steady stream of tokens whose
pace is the TPS.

(Other modalities like speech and images have their own metrics. Those are covered in Chapter 6.)

### “TPS” can mean two different things

TTFT is unambiguous, but **TPS** is not. It can be a *latency* metric (speed for one user) or a *throughput* metric
(speed of the whole service). Most people mean the per-user one. When it matters, use sharper terms:

- **Perceived TPS**: tokens per second **for one user**, after the first token. A latency metric.
- **Total TPS**: tokens per second generated by the **whole inference service** across all users. A throughput metric.
- **Inter-token latency (ITL)**: the time **between** consecutive tokens. It’s just TPS flipped around: an ITL of
  **10 ms** means **100 tokens per second** per user.

<Note kind="tip">
  TTFT and TPS matter most when output is **streamed** to a person, like in a chatbot. For something like an agent’s
  tool call, individual tokens are useless until the whole response is done, so you measure **total response time**
  instead.
</Note>

## Percentiles: why averages lie

The naive approach is to report the **average** (mean) TTFT or TPS. But LLM response times form a
<Term def="A distribution where most values cluster on the left, with a long tail of much larger values stretching to the right.">right-skewed</Term>
distribution: most requests cluster around a typical time, while a few **outliers** take far longer. As the book’s
figure shows, that tail drags the **mean** above the **median (P50)**.

Those outliers matter a lot. It’s not good enough for most interactions to feel snappy if **one in ten** takes several
seconds. Users notice, and they stop trusting the product.

So inference engineers measure latency in **percentiles**:

| Percentile | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **P50** | Median latency | 1 in every 2 requests is slower |
| **P90** | 90th-percentile latency | 1 in every 10 requests is slower |
| **P95** | 95th-percentile latency | 1 in every 20 requests is slower |
| **P99** | 99th-percentile latency | 1 in every 100 requests is slower |

<Analogy title="Think of it like a commute">
  If your train usually takes 30 minutes but is an hour late once a week, “average commute: 34 minutes” sounds fine,
  yet you’re late for work every week. The P90 or P99 commute is what decides when you actually have to leave home.
</Analogy>

Bringing down the average still matters, but good performance work also goes after **P90 and P99** to make the
experience *reliably* fast.

## Inference time vs. end-to-end time

The other key distinction is **what you’re timing**:

- **Inference time**: only the on-GPU time spent generating tokens.
- **End-to-end time**: everything the user experiences, including **network latency** (the request traveling to the
  server and back) and any **queue time** (waiting for a free GPU).

Both are worth knowing. Inference time tells you how well your **model performance work** is going. End-to-end time
tells you how fast your app **feels to users**.

<Note kind="warn">
  If inference time is fast but end-to-end time is slow, **stop optimizing the model**. The problem is in your
  infrastructure (network, routing, queueing, or scaling), which Chapter 7 covers.
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- **TTFT** = wait before the first token (prefill). **TPS** = streaming speed after that (decode).
- Say **perceived TPS** (per user) or **total TPS** (whole service) to avoid confusion. **ITL** = time between tokens;
  10 ms ITL = 100 TPS.
- For non-streamed outputs like tool calls, measure **total response time**.
- Latency is right-skewed, so the **mean hides outliers**. Track **P50, P90, P95, P99**.
- Measure both **inference-only** and **end-to-end** time. A big gap between them points at infrastructure.
</KeyPoints>
