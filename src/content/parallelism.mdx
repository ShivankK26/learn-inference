<TLDR>
  Big models don’t fit on one GPU, and even when the weights squeeze in, there’s no room left for the KV cache. So
  you split the model across GPUs. **Tensor parallelism** splits every layer and is the default for speed.
  **Expert parallelism** spreads MoE experts across GPUs for throughput. **Pipeline parallelism** is only for going
  across machines. The limit is always the same: GPUs talking to each other is much slower than GPUs reading their own
  memory.
</TLDR>

## Why one GPU isn’t enough

Every frontier LLM on the market today is too big to fit on a single GPU for batch inference. GPUs keep getting
bigger, but so do models, and that trend shows no sign of reversing.

A handy rule: **in FP8, each billion parameters takes roughly 1 GB** of memory (one byte per parameter). In FP16 it’s
2 GB per billion, and in FP4 about 0.5 GB.

<Worked title="The book’s DeepSeek example, step by step">

**DeepSeek-V3.1** has **671 billion** parameters. In FP8, the weights alone are about **671 GB**.

1. **One B200** (180 GB): the weights alone immediately trigger an
   <Term def="Out-of-memory error: the GPU is asked to hold more data than its memory can fit, so the job crashes.">out-of-memory (OOM) error</Term>.
2. **Four B200s** (4 × 180 = 720 GB): the weights technically fit, but only 720 − 671 = **49 GB** is left over, under 7%
   of the total. The KV cache often takes **80% or more** of the memory remaining after the weights, and here there’s
   almost nothing to give it. Four GPUs couldn’t serve DeepSeek at any reasonable sequence length or batch size.
3. **Eight B200s**, a full node (8 × 180 = 1,440 GB): 1,440 − 671 = **769 GB** is left, and 80% of that is ~615 GB for
   the KV cache. This is the configuration the book says you need for real production traffic.

</Worked>

You can estimate the minimum GPU count for any model by **multiplying the precision (bytes per parameter), the
parameter count, and your expected KV cache allocation together**, then **rounding up to the next instance size**
you can actually rent (usually 1, 2, 4, or 8 GPUs per machine).

<Worked title="A GPU-count estimate you can reuse">

Say you want the KV cache to get about as much memory as the weights, a KV multiplier of **2×**:

1. **DeepSeek-V3.1 in FP8:** 671B × 1 byte × 2 = 1,342 GB. On B200s: 1,342 ÷ 180 ≈ 7.5 → round up to **8 GPUs**,
   matching the book.
2. **Llama 3.1 405B in FP8:** 405B × 1 byte × 2 = 810 GB. On H200s (141 GB): 810 ÷ 141 ≈ 5.7 → **8 GPUs**. On H100s
   (80 GB): 810 ÷ 80 ≈ 10.1 → **16 GPUs**, which means two machines.
3. You could still squeeze 405B onto 8 × H100 (640 GB), leaving 640 − 405 = 235 GB for the cache. It works, but with a
   smaller KV multiplier (about 1.6×), so fewer concurrent users or shorter contexts.

</Worked>

<GpuCount />

In many cases, even for mid-size models like **GPT OSS**, you’ll want **more than the minimum** number of GPUs. The
extra GPUs give you a bigger KV cache (more concurrent users and longer conversations) and unlock better per-user
latency, because the work of every token gets shared across more GPUs.

<Note kind="beyond">
  GPT OSS 120B stores its expert weights in a 4-bit format, so the whole model is only about 60–65 GB and fits on a
  single 80 GB GPU. That’s why it counts as mid-size. Running it on one GPU leaves very little room for the KV cache,
  so production deployments often use 2, 4, or 8 GPUs anyway.
</Note>

## The catch: GPUs have to talk

All of this requires that inference **scales efficiently** from one GPU to many. The limit on scaling is the
**communication overhead** between GPUs. From [Chapter 3](/learn/instances), GPUs connect through **NVLink** and
**NVSwitch** inside a node, and **InfiniBand** between nodes:

| Link | What it connects | Bandwidth (per GPU) |
|---|---|---|
| **VRAM (HBM)** | A GPU to its own memory | 3.35 TB/s (H100) · 8 TB/s (B200) |
| **NVLink + NVSwitch** | GPUs inside one node | 900 GB/s (Hopper) · 1,800 GB/s (Blackwell) |
| **InfiniBand** | Different nodes | ~50 GB/s (a 400 Gb/s network card) |

NVLink and InfiniBand are high-bandwidth links, but they’re only a fraction of the speed of VRAM. On a B200, NVLink is
about 4× slower than the GPU’s own memory, and InfiniBand is another ~36× slower than NVLink. Since
<Memory>decode</Memory> is already bound on memory bandwidth, multi-GPU inference has to be carefully designed so it
doesn’t stall on inter-GPU communication.

This field of study is called
<Term def="Choosing how to split a model across GPUs based on how fast each pair of GPUs can communicate: fast links inside a machine, slower ones between machines.">topology-aware parallelism</Term>:
choosing *how* to split the model based on *how the GPUs are wired together*.

## Three ways to split a model

There are three primary forms of model parallelism in inference:

- **Pipeline parallelism (PP):** splits the *layers* of the model across GPUs.
- **Tensor parallelism (TP):** splits the *tensors within each layer* across GPUs.
- **Expert parallelism (EP):** puts entire *experts* of an MoE model on different GPUs.

| Method | Mechanism | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| **PP** | Each GPU handles one stage (a group of layers) of the pass | Not recommended: poor latency and utilization from the step-by-step pipeline |
| **TP** | Compute-heavy operations like matmuls are split across GPUs | Requires synchronization across GPUs after every layer; not suitable for multi-node |
| **EP** | Each expert lives within a single GPU, so work inside an expert is fast | Tokens must be routed between GPUs to reach their experts; better for throughput than latency |

In short: **TP** is generally best for low-latency inference within a single node. **EP** improves throughput for MoE
LLMs. **PP** is only used for multi-node inference. You’ll see these written with the number of GPUs attached:
**TP8** is tensor parallelism across 8 GPUs, **EP16** is expert parallelism across 16, and **TP8PP2** is 8-way TP inside
each of 2 pipeline stages (16 GPUs in total).

<Analogy title="Think of it like a kitchen team">
  **Pipeline**: one chef chops, the next cooks, the next plates. Each dish passes down the line, and everyone waits on
  the slowest station. **Tensor**: several chefs work on every dish together, each doing a slice, and they huddle
  after every step. That’s fast, but only if they stand close together. **Expert**: each chef is a specialist (pastry,
  grill, sauces), and orders are sent to whoever’s needed. There’s no constant huddling, so the kitchen handles lots
  of orders at once.
</Analogy>

There’s also a family of **data-parallel** strategies, like **context parallelism**, that split the *computation
over the input* across devices instead of splitting the model. They’re rare in LLM inference but essential for video
generation ([Chapter 6](/learn/serving-images-video)).

## Tensor parallelism: lower latency

**Tensor parallelism should be your default strategy** for multi-GPU inference. It supports both dense models like
Llama 405B and the MoE models that dominate the open model landscape today.

TP works by splitting each layer apart (unlike pipeline parallelism, which keeps layers intact) and distributing the
layer fragments across the GPUs. For each layer, the two expensive things, **reading the weights from memory** and
**executing the matrix multiplication**, are shared across the GPUs. With TP4, each GPU stores and reads only a
quarter of every layer’s weights. Because decode is memory-bound, reading a quarter of the bytes makes each token
roughly four times faster, before communication costs. For MoE models, each **expert** is itself sliced across the
GPUs in the same way.

### How the split actually works

The book describes TP at a high level. Here’s the standard way it’s done (introduced in the Megatron-LM paper), which
explains exactly where the communication comes from:

<TPSplit />

1. **The first matrix is split by columns.** In the feed-forward block, the up-projection is cut into vertical
   slices, and each GPU computes its own slice of the hidden units. Activation functions work on each number
   independently, so no GPU needs anything from the others at this point.
2. **The second matrix is split by rows.** The down-projection is cut horizontally, matching the slices above. Each
   GPU multiplies *its* slice of hidden units by *its* rows and gets a **partial** version of the full output.
3. **An all-reduce combines them.** The partial outputs are summed across all GPUs, and every GPU receives the full
   result. This step is the
   <Term def="A communication step where every GPU contributes its partial result and every GPU receives the combined total.">all-reduce</Term>,
   and the next layer can’t start until it finishes.
4. **Attention is split by heads.** Each GPU owns a group of whole attention heads, computes them, and keeps the **KV
   cache for its own heads**. The attention output projection is row-split, so there’s another all-reduce there too.

That’s **two all-reduces per transformer layer, every forward pass**. In a node with high-bandwidth NVLink and
NVSwitch, that overhead is small. Across nodes, it isn’t.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Splitting attention by heads also splits the **KV cache**: with TP8, each GPU holds one-eighth of every request’s
  cache, so TP multiplies how much cache the system can hold. There’s a limit. A model with 8 KV heads (like Llama 3.1
  70B) divides cleanly up to TP8. Beyond that, engines have to duplicate KV heads across GPUs, which wastes memory.
</Note>

### Does more TP always help?

Increasing tensor parallelism improves **tokens per second for each user**, *assuming* the model is large enough and
the sequences long enough that the communication overhead doesn’t outweigh the faster forward pass. That’s the case
for most frontier models. The demo shows this for a 70B model:

<TensorParallel />

<Worked title="TP2 → TP4 → TP8 for a 70B model on H100s">

Llama 70B in FP16 is **140 GB** of weights. Each decode step reads all of them (split across the GPUs). Assume each
all-reduce inside a node costs about **10 µs** (an illustrative figure: at small batch sizes, fixed latency matters
more than bandwidth, since each all-reduce only moves a few kilobytes per token).

1. **Weight reading per token:** 140 GB ÷ N GPUs ÷ 3.35 TB/s. TP2 → **20.9 ms**, TP4 → **10.4 ms**, TP8 → **5.2 ms**.
2. **Communication per token:** 80 layers × 2 all-reduces × 10 µs = **1.6 ms**, roughly the same at every TP size.
3. **Totals:** TP2 ≈ 22.5 ms (~44 tokens/s), TP4 ≈ 12.0 ms (~83 tokens/s), TP8 ≈ 6.8 ms (~147 tokens/s).
4. Notice the diminishing returns. Communication is 7% of the step at TP2, 13% at TP4, and 24% at TP8. Doubling the GPUs
   no longer doubles the speed.
5. **TP16 across two nodes:** weight reading halves again to 2.6 ms, but each all-reduce now crosses InfiniBand.
   At ~80 µs each, that’s 160 × 80 µs = **12.8 ms**, for a total of ~15.4 ms. That’s **slower than TP8**, which is why
   TP isn’t used across nodes.

</Worked>

## Expert parallelism: higher throughput

**Expert parallelism** neatly divides whole experts across GPUs. In a model with **128 experts** served with **EP8**
across eight GPUs, each GPU hosts **16 complete experts**. (The book’s figure contrasts this with TP for MoE, where
every expert is sliced across all eight GPUs.)

EP improves **total system throughput**, which makes inference more scalable and less expensive. Each expert
processes its tokens whole, on one GPU, so **each token takes just as long** as before. But the system as a whole can
handle many more tokens at the same time. Remember from [the MoE demo](/learn/attention-and-kv-cache) that a large
batch wakes up almost every expert. EP gives each GPU its own set of busy experts to work through.

Many deployments **mix TP and EP** to get both benefits. A common layout, shown in the book’s figure, uses **TP for the
attention layers** and **EP for the sparse MoE layers**.

### Why EP needs less communication

EP requires **less inter-GPU communication than TP**. Here is what happens at each MoE layer:

1. **Routing happens locally.** The expert **router**, the small network that decides which experts each token
   activates, is a tiny part of the model, so a copy lives on every GPU.
2. **Dispatch.** Each token’s hidden state is sent to whichever GPUs host its chosen experts.
3. **Expert work.** Each GPU runs its experts on all the tokens it received, at full size, with no splitting.
4. **Combine.** The results travel back and are blended together, weighted by the router’s scores.

Communication is still necessary to pass tokens to their experts. But unlike TP, there’s no need to all-reduce the
results of every layer across every GPU. Thanks to this lower overhead, **EP scales well to multi-node deployments**
and to systems with limited interconnect bandwidth.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Take Qwen3-235B-A22B from Chapter 2: 128 experts with 8 active per token. With EP8, a token’s 8 experts are spread
  over the GPUs at random. If routing were perfectly uniform, a token would visit about 5 of the 8 GPUs at each MoE
  layer on average. Real routing is uneven, though, and some experts are far more popular than others. Those experts’
  GPUs become hotspots, so large deployments often **duplicate the most popular experts** onto extra GPUs to balance
  the load.
</Note>

## Multi-node inference

Sometimes eight GPUs aren’t enough. You might be serving a huge model at high precision, supporting input sequences
of **millions of tokens**, or just trying to run inference as fast as possible. GPUs are designed to work together
across nodes, and multi-node training has been standard for years for building frontier models. But multi-node
**inference** introduces new challenges:

- **Infrastructure:** How do you reliably provision two or more interconnected GPU nodes, and build abstractions that
  work across cloud providers ([Chapter 7](/learn/multi-cloud))?
- **Parallelism:** How do you communicate effectively over InfiniBand, which is much slower than NVLink?

InfiniBand adds a new wrinkle to topology-aware parallelism. Tensor parallelism generally needs too much communication
to be a good fit across nodes (the TP16 example above). You have two options that work well over InfiniBand:

1. **For dense models,** use tensor parallelism **within** each node and pipeline parallelism **between** nodes, for
   example **TP8PP2**.
2. **For MoE models,** you can also try **expert parallelism**, for example **EP16**, because it has lower communication
   overhead than TP.

For MoE models, **TP8PP2 will generally offer lower latency per user**, and **EP16 will yield higher overall system
throughput**.

### Why pipeline parallelism works across nodes, and why it’s slow

With TP8PP2, the first node holds the first half of the layers (split 8 ways by TP), and the second node holds the
second half. The only thing that crosses InfiniBand is the **activation handed from the last layer of stage 1 to the
first layer of stage 2**. That’s one vector per token per forward pass.

<Worked title="How little TP8PP2 sends between nodes">

For a model with a hidden size of 8,192 in 16-bit precision:

1. **PP boundary:** one activation of 8,192 × 2 bytes ≈ **16 KB per token**, sent **once** per forward pass.
2. **TP16 across nodes instead:** 2 all-reduces × 80 layers = **160** latency-bound exchanges per forward pass, every one
   of them crossing InfiniBand.
3. That one-handoff-versus-160-exchanges difference is why PP tolerates slow links and TP doesn’t.

</Worked>

The weakness is the pipeline itself. A token has to go through stage 1 *and then* stage 2, one after the other, so
per-token latency doesn’t improve, and while stage 2 works on a token, stage 1 sits idle unless there’s other work to
fill it. Those idle gaps are called **pipeline bubbles**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  To keep S pipeline stages busy, you need at least S batches in flight at once, each at a different stage. With M
  batches in flight, stage utilization is roughly M ÷ (M + S − 1). The book’s table phrases PP in training terms
  (“forward and backward pass”). In inference there’s only a forward pass, but the bubble problem is the same.
</Note>

Unless your model and KV cache are so large that they *require* multi-node inference, spreading one model copy across
nodes probably isn’t the best use of the extra hardware. You’re often better off using extra nodes for **horizontal
scaling** (more independent replicas) or for **disaggregated serving**, the next lesson.

<KeyPoints>
- In FP8, a billion parameters ≈ **1 GB**. Estimate GPUs as **precision × parameters × KV multiplier**, then round up to an instance size: DeepSeek-V3.1 (671 GB in FP8) needs a full 8 × B200 node.
- Inter-GPU links are a fraction of VRAM speed (B200: 8 TB/s VRAM vs. 1.8 TB/s NVLink vs. ~50 GB/s InfiniBand), so splits must follow the wiring: **topology-aware parallelism**.
- **TP** (the default) splits every matmul, column-wise then row-wise, with **two all-reduces per layer**. It gives the best per-user latency in one node, with diminishing returns as communication grows.
- **EP** puts whole experts on each GPU (128 experts at EP8 = 16 per GPU). Tokens are dispatched to their experts and combined back, with no per-layer all-reduce. That means higher throughput and good scaling across nodes.
- **PP** sends only one activation per token between stages, so it suits InfiniBand, but pipeline bubbles hurt latency. Multi-node options: **TP8PP2** (lower latency) or **EP16** (higher throughput). Otherwise, prefer more replicas or disaggregation.
</KeyPoints>
