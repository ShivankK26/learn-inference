<TLDR>
  Big models don't fit on one GPU, and even when the weights squeeze in, there's no room left for the KV cache. So
  you split the model across GPUs. **Tensor parallelism** splits every layer and is the default for speed.
  **Expert parallelism** spreads MoE experts across GPUs for throughput. The limit is always the same: GPUs talking to
  each other is much slower than GPUs reading their own memory.
</TLDR>

## Why one GPU isn't enough

Every frontier LLM today is too big to serve on a single GPU. GPUs keep getting bigger, but so do models, and that
trend isn't reversing.

A handy rule: **in FP8, each billion parameters takes about 1 GB** of memory. Take **DeepSeek-V3.1**, with **671
billion** parameters:

- On **one B200**, the weights alone would immediately cause an
  <Term def="Out-of-memory error: the GPU is asked to hold more data than its memory can fit, so the job crashes.">out-of-memory (OOM) error</Term>.
- On **four B200s** (720 GB total), the weights technically fit, but there's nothing left for the KV cache. The KV
  cache often takes **80% or more** of the memory left after the weights, so four GPUs couldn't serve any realistic
  batch size or sequence length.
- In practice you need **a full machine of eight B200s** to serve real traffic on a model this size.

To estimate the minimum, multiply **precision × parameter count**, add the room you expect the **KV cache** to need,
then **round up to the next instance size** you can actually rent (1, 2, 4 or 8 GPUs per machine):

It often makes sense to use **more than the minimum**, even for mid-size models like GPT OSS. Extra GPUs give you a
larger KV cache (more users and longer conversations) and better per-user speed.

## The catch: GPUs have to talk

All of this only works if splitting across GPUs doesn't create a new bottleneck. GPUs connect to each other through
**NVLink** and **NVSwitch** inside a machine and **InfiniBand** between machines (more in Chapter 3). These are fast,
but still only a fraction of the speed of a GPU reading its own memory. Since <Memory>decode</Memory> is already
limited by memory bandwidth, a careless split quickly stalls on communication.

Designing splits around how GPUs are wired together is called
<Term def="Choosing how to split a model across GPUs based on how fast each pair of GPUs can communicate: fast links inside a machine, slower ones between machines.">topology-aware parallelism</Term>.

## Three ways to split a model

| Method | How it splits | Downside |
|---|---|---|
| **Pipeline parallelism (PP)** | Different GPUs get different *layers*; each handles one stage in turn | Poor latency and utilization: GPUs wait for each other step by step |
| **Tensor parallelism (TP)** | Every *layer* is cut into pieces, and the big matmuls are shared across GPUs | GPUs must sync after every layer, so it isn't suited to multi-machine setups |
| **Expert parallelism (EP)** | Whole *experts* of an MoE model live on different GPUs | Tokens must be routed between GPUs to reach their experts, so it favors throughput over latency |

In short: **TP** is best for low latency within one machine, **EP** raises throughput for MoE models, and **PP** is
only used when you go multi-machine.

<Analogy title="Think of it like a kitchen team">
  **Pipeline**: one chef chops, the next cooks, the next plates. Each dish passes down the line, and everyone waits on
  the slowest station. **Tensor**: several chefs work on every dish together, each doing a slice, and they huddle
  after every step. That's fast, but only if they stand close together. **Expert**: each chef is a specialist (pastry,
  grill, sauces), and orders are sent to whoever's needed. No constant huddling, so the kitchen handles lots of orders
  at once.
</Analogy>

There's also a fourth family: data-parallel strategies like **context parallelism**, which split the *input* across
devices. They're rare for LLMs but essential for video generation (Chapter 6).

## Tensor parallelism: lower latency

**TP should be your default** when a model needs multiple GPUs. It works for dense models (like Llama 405B) and for the
MoE models that dominate open models today.

TP cuts each layer into fragments and gives one fragment to each GPU. For every layer, the work of **reading the
weights from memory** and **doing the matmul** is shared, so each GPU does a fraction of the reading. The book's
figures show a weight matrix sliced across GPUs. For MoE models, each expert is itself sliced across the GPUs.

The cost: after every layer, the partial results from all GPUs must be combined into one output (an
<Term def="A communication step where every GPU contributes its partial result and every GPU receives the combined total.">all-reduce</Term>)
before the next layer can start. Inside one machine, NVLink and NVSwitch keep that overhead small.

<TensorParallel />

More TP generally means **more tokens per second for each user**, as long as the model is big enough and sequences are
long enough that the faster forward pass outweighs the communication. That's true for most frontier models. Notice
in the demo how TP16 gets *slower*: once the split crosses machines, the huddles get expensive.

## Expert parallelism: higher throughput

**EP** spreads whole experts across GPUs. A model with **128 experts** served with **EP8** across eight GPUs puts **16
complete experts** on each GPU.

EP improves **total system throughput**, which makes serving cheaper and more scalable. Each token takes just as
long as before, but the system as a whole handles far more tokens at once. (Remember from the
[MoE demo](/learn/attention-and-kv-cache) that a large batch wakes up almost every expert; EP is how you put all
those busy experts to good use.)

Many deployments **mix TP and EP**: one common layout uses **TP for the attention layers** and **EP for the MoE
layers**.

EP also needs **less communication than TP**. The router (the tiny network that picks experts) is small, so a copy
lives on every GPU. GPUs still have to pass tokens to wherever their experts live, but they don't have to collect
and combine every layer's results. That's why EP **scales well across machines** and on systems with slower
connections between GPUs.

## Multi-node inference

Sometimes eight GPUs aren't enough: a huge model at high precision, inputs of **millions of tokens**, or simply chasing
the fastest possible speed. Multi-machine training has been standard for years, but multi-node *inference* brings
two challenges:

- **Infrastructure**: reliably getting two or more connected GPU machines, and building the tooling to manage them
  across cloud providers (Chapter 7).
- **Parallelism**: making the split work over **InfiniBand**, which is much slower than NVLink.

TP needs too much chatter to work well over InfiniBand. Two layouts that do work:

1. **Dense models**: TP inside each machine, PP between machines. For example, **TP8PP2** is 8-way TP within each of 2
   machines.
2. **MoE models**: you can also try pure **EP**, e.g. **EP16**, which communicates less than TP.

For MoE models, **TP8PP2 usually gives lower latency per user**, while **EP16 gives higher total throughput**.

<Note kind="tip">
  Unless your model and KV cache truly *require* multiple machines, spreading one copy across nodes usually isn't the
  best use of the hardware. You're often better off using extra machines as **more replicas**, or for
  **disaggregation** (next lesson).
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- Estimate GPUs as **precision × parameters + KV cache room**, then round up to an instance size. In FP8, ~1 GB per billion parameters.
- Communication between GPUs is the limiting factor, so match the split to the wiring (**topology-aware parallelism**).
- **TP** (split every layer) is the default: lower per-user latency within one machine, but it needs fast links.
- **EP** (whole experts per GPU) boosts throughput for MoE models and communicates less, so it scales across machines.
- **PP** is only for multi-node setups. Multi-node options: **TP8PP2** for lower latency, **EP16** for higher throughput.
</KeyPoints>
