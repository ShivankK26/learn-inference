<TLDR>
  Traffic goes up and down all day, so the number of model copies (**replicas**) should too. **Autoscaling** adds
  replicas when demand rises and removes them when it falls. It only works well if you know exactly how much each
  replica can handle (its **concurrency**), can start new ones quickly (**cold starts**), and can route and queue
  requests sensibly while capacity catches up.
</TLDR>

## The goal

The book states the goal of autoscaling in one sentence: **always have enough resources to serve all incoming requests
while maintaining your latency SLAs, without wasting money on idle GPUs.** Both halves matter. Missing the first half
means slow or failed requests; missing the second means burning money.

Without autoscaling you’re stuck with a fixed fleet, and it’s wrong most of the day. The book shows this with two
figures. In Figure 7.3, a fixed number of replicas **wastes resources during traffic lulls** (the gap between capacity
and demand at night) and **misses SLAs during spikes** (demand poking above capacity). In Figure 7.4, a strong
autoscaling system keeps capacity **hugging the demand curve**. You can recreate both below: start with a fixed fleet,
then switch on autoscaling.

## How it works: Kubernetes, replicas, and clusters

Autoscaling systems are built on **Kubernetes**, an open-source container orchestration system, together with a
cluster-level system for provisioning and deallocating compute (actually adding and removing machines).

- Kubernetes can run one or more **replicas** of a model’s container, each on its own **instance**. The instance
  includes the GPUs and other hardware resources the container requires.
- Kubernetes composes a group of hardware resources into a **cluster**, which has two types of components:
  - The **control plane** makes routing and scaling decisions.
  - The **worker plane** runs the actual containerized applications.

The book’s Figure 7.5 shows a single control plane orchestrating many workers. A single cluster can run many replicas of
many different models.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Kubernetes vocabulary you’ll meet in practice.** A *node* is one machine in the cluster (for inference, typically a
  GPU instance). A *pod* is the smallest thing Kubernetes schedules: one or more containers that run together, so each
  model replica is usually one pod. A *deployment* declares “I want N copies of this pod,” and Kubernetes keeps reality
  matching that number, restarting pods that crash and moving them off unhealthy nodes. A pod asks for GPUs explicitly
  (for example, 8 GPUs), and the scheduler only places it on a node that has them free. Kubernetes ships with a generic
  autoscaler that scales on metrics like CPU usage, but CPU usage says little about a GPU inference server, which is why
  inference platforms scale on **traffic** and **GPU** signals, as this lesson describes.
</Note>

So how many replicas should each model get? Unless your traffic is unusually consistent, **there isn’t one number of
replicas that perfectly matches your needs**. Autoscaling is the practice of dynamically adjusting the number of replicas
allocated to a given model within a cluster.

## What to scale on: utilization or traffic?

There are two ways to make autoscaling decisions:

- **Utilization**: scale up and down on GPU signals like **memory usage** or **compute usage**.
- **Traffic**: scale up and down on the **number of requests being processed** in the system.

They don’t always match. The book’s example is LLM <Compute>prefill</Compute>: a *few* requests with hundreds of
thousands of **uncached** input tokens can cause far higher utilization than *many* small requests with high cache hit
rates. Ten requests could be light work or crushing work depending on their shapes.

The other difference is timing. **Traffic-based decisions can be proactive**: you see requests arriving before they
turn into GPU work. **Utilization is a lagging indicator**: it only rises after the work has started, so by the time it
spikes, users are already waiting. The book’s recommendation is to **use both in combination** to keep resources
matched with demand: traffic to react early, utilization to catch cases where a few requests are much heavier than they
look.

## The five knobs of traffic-based autoscaling

When designing a traffic-based autoscaler, you configure five factors:

| Setting | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Min replicas** | What is the minimum number of replicas that stay running, regardless of traffic? |
| **Max replicas** | What is the maximum number of replicas you can allocate when traffic is high? |
| **Autoscaling window** | How long is the sliding timeframe used to measure traffic and make decisions? |
| **Scale-down delay** | After a scale-down is suggested, how long do you wait in case of another spike? |
| **Concurrency target** | How many requests can each replica handle at once? |

What each one really controls:

- **Min replicas** is your floor, and your insurance. It sets the capacity you have *instantly*, with no cold start, when
  a burst arrives. Higher means safer and more expensive; zero is the special case of *scale to zero*, covered below.
- **Max replicas** is your ceiling: a cost guardrail, and often a reflection of how many GPUs you can actually get. If
  traffic exceeds max replicas × concurrency target, the excess waits in the queue no matter what.
- **The autoscaling window** trades stability against speed. A **longer** window averages out noise, so replica counts
  stay steady; a **shorter** one reacts faster to spikes, but can thrash (adding and removing replicas over momentary
  blips).
- **The scale-down delay** is asymmetric protection. Scaling *up* should be quick; scaling *down* should be patient,
  because removing a replica you’ll need again in five minutes costs a full cold start. The book’s example: a longer
  scale-down delay prevents premature scale-downs for spiky traffic, but can mean unnecessary spend after traffic
  truly cools off.
- **The concurrency target** is the conversion factor between “requests in the system” and “replicas needed.” Get it
  wrong and every other setting is working from a bad number (next section).

The exact configuration determines how well the autoscaler achieves its two goals: maintaining latency SLAs without
wasting resources. Go back to the simulator and try each knob.

<Worked title="One autoscaling decision, step by step">

Suppose min replicas = 2, max replicas = 40, a 5-minute window, a 15-minute scale-down delay, and a concurrency target
of 16. The service currently runs 6 replicas.

1. **Measure.** Over the last 5 minutes, the average number of requests in flight was **150**.
2. **Convert to replicas.** 150 ÷ 16 = 9.4, rounded up to **10 replicas needed**.
3. **Clamp.** 10 is between the minimum (2) and maximum (40), so the target is 10.
4. **Act.** 10 is more than the current 6, so the autoscaler **scales up immediately** by 4 replicas. They start their
   cold starts; meanwhile any overflow waits in the queue.
5. **Later, traffic falls.** The window average drops to **70** in flight: 70 ÷ 16 = 4.4, so **5 replicas needed**.
   Instead of removing 5 replicas right away, the autoscaler starts the **15-minute scale-down delay**.
6. **If traffic spikes again** within those 15 minutes, the scale-down is cancelled and no cold starts are wasted. If it
   stays low for the full delay, the extra replicas are removed.

</Worked>

## How much traffic is “in the system”? Little’s law

The traffic signal is the number of **requests in flight**: requests that have arrived but not yet finished. It depends
on two things, how fast requests *arrive* and how *long* each one takes.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Little’s law** is a simple, very general result from queueing theory: in a steady system, the average number of
  items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each item spends inside. For inference: **requests in
  flight = requests per second × seconds per request.** It explains why the same traffic can need very different
  capacity: if your average response doubles in length (say, users start asking for long reports), the number of
  requests in flight doubles too, and so do the replicas you need, even though no more users showed up.
</Note>

<Worked title="Sizing a deployment with Little’s law">

1. Requests arrive at **20 per second**, and each one takes about **6 seconds** from arrival to last token.
2. Requests in flight: 20 × 6 = **120**.
3. Each replica’s concurrency target is **16**, so you need 120 ÷ 16 = 7.5, rounded up to **8 replicas** just to keep up.
4. Now add a margin for cold starts. If traffic can grow **10% per minute** during a morning ramp and a new replica
   takes **4 minutes** to come online, traffic can be about 40% higher by the time the replica is ready.
   120 × 1.4 = 168 in flight, and 168 ÷ 16 = 10.5, so about **11 replicas**.
5. Those three extra replicas are the price of slow cold starts. Halve the cold start and the margin halves too.

</Worked>

## Concurrency and batch sizing

To operate a traffic-based autoscaler properly, you need a strong understanding of **how much concurrent traffic each
instance can handle**. Most model inference services can handle more than one request at a time via batching, and there
are three approaches:

- **Static batching**: the server waits until the batch is full before starting inference.
- **Dynamic batching**: the server waits until the batch is full *or* a configured amount of time has passed, whichever
  comes first.
- **Continuous batching**: the server runs inference continuously, swapping in requests as slots become available.

The book’s three diagrams show the difference. In **Figure 7.6** (static), the batch size is fixed and inference waits
for the batch to fill, so early requests sit idle waiting for latecomers. In **Figure 7.7** (dynamic), a cutoff time is
added: the batch runs whether or not it’s full once the timer expires, capping the wait. In **Figure 7.8**
(continuous), the batch operates at the **token level**, switching new requests in as old ones finish, so a free slot
never sits empty for long.

<BatchingModes />

<Worked title="Why static batching punishes early arrivals">

1. A batch holds **8** requests, and requests arrive about **one every 0.5 seconds**.
2. **Static:** the first request waits for 7 more to arrive, about 7 × 0.5 = **3.5 seconds**, before any work starts.
   If traffic is light, it could wait much longer.
3. **Dynamic** with a 1-second cutoff: the first request waits at most **1 second**; the batch runs with the 2 or 3
   requests that have arrived.
4. **Continuous:** the first request starts on the next step. And because slots are refilled token by token, a short
   request that finishes early frees its slot for a waiting request immediately, instead of the whole batch waiting for
   its longest member to finish.

</Worked>

Inference engines like **vLLM**, **SGLang**, and **TensorRT-LLM** implement robust continuous batching (TensorRT-LLM
calls it **in-flight batching**), where requests are batched at the token level. This minimizes latency relative to
static batching, and it’s why LLM serving has largely left the older approaches behind.

**Batch sizing trades latency for throughput.** Increasing the batch size produces more throughput overall, but each
user’s latency gets worse (the [bottlenecks lesson](/learn/bottlenecks) showed why: bigger batches squeeze more work out
of each trip through the weights, but each step takes a little longer). The book’s advice is to **test performance
across multiple batch sizes** to find the right fit for your model, instance, latency target, and budget. In practice
that usually means finding the **largest batch size that still meets your latency SLA** at P90 or P99, since every
extra slot per replica means fewer replicas to pay for.

This number is controlled in two places, and **they should match**:

- At the **autoscaling configuration** level, as the **concurrency target**.
- At the **replica** level, as the engine’s **batch size**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **What goes wrong when they don’t match.** If the concurrency target is *higher* than the batch size, the autoscaler
  thinks replicas have room when they don’t: extra requests pile up in a queue *inside* each replica, latency climbs,
  and the autoscaler doesn’t add capacity because by its count nothing is full. If the target is *lower* than the batch
  size, the autoscaler adds replicas while existing ones still have free batch slots, so you pay for idle capacity.
</Note>

Once they match, the scaling rule is simple. The book puts it this way: **once every active replica reaches its maximum
concurrency, the autoscaler knows to spin up more replicas. If enough replicas are kicking off half-full batches, it’s
time to scale back down.**

## Cold starts

A **cold start** is the time it takes to spin up a new replica of a model. It quietly decides how good your whole
autoscaling system can be. The book’s key insight: **if you can’t spin up replicas fast, it’s hard to confidently scale
down**, because you won’t be able to scale back up in time when traffic returns. The result is **over-provisioning**:
paying for idle GPUs as insurance. (The replica calculator above shows this: cold start time directly sets how much
headroom you need.)

Several factors affect cold start time, and each one adds to the total timeline (the book’s Figure 7.9 lays them end to
end):

1. **GPU procurement**: how quickly can you add the necessary GPUs to your cluster and allocate them to the model?
2. **Image loading**: how quickly can you load the container image onto the newly procured instance?
3. **Model loading**: how quickly can you load the model weights into the container?
4. **Engine startup**: how quickly can you start the inference engine, including any compilation time?

**Each of these needs to be optimized separately.**

<ColdStartBreakdown />

### 1. GPU procurement

Unless you have a **pool of warm nodes** that you flex between models, GPU procurement speed is mostly a function of
your **cloud provider**. How quickly a provider starts nodes is one of the negotiable factors in a GPU contract (the
[multi-cloud lesson](/learn/multi-cloud) covers procurement). The warm pool trick works because moving an already-running
node from one model to another skips the provider entirely.

### 2 and 3. Loading images and weights

Engineers can do a lot about the next stages. Loading images and model weights is a function of **how quickly you can
write gigabytes, often hundreds of gigabytes, of data onto your instance**. There are only two ways to go faster:
**make the data smaller, or get more bandwidth.**

- **Make it smaller.** Include only the necessary steps and dependencies so images are smaller and faster to build into
  containers (the [containers lesson](/learn/containers)). **Quantizing** model weights has an extra benefit here: FP8
  weights are half the size of FP16 weights, so they also load twice as fast during a cold start.
- **Keep weights out of the image.** For small models, the strategy used to be baking the weights into the image to
  simplify caching and loading. Now that most models have dozens or hundreds of billions of parameters, the weights
  **dwarf** the image and are better loaded separately.
- **Load from somewhere close.** Where you load weights *from* has a massive impact on bandwidth. From a third party like
  **Hugging Face**, you’re limited by their egress (download) speed. Storing weights in an **S3 bucket** introduces
  network latency and data transfer costs. For multi-hundred-billion-parameter models you need **gigabytes per second**
  of bandwidth, and the book says the best way to get it is **loading over the network within a node from a source
  cached physically near the GPU instance, in the same datacenter.**

<Worked title="How long do the weights take to arrive?">

Take a model with about 670 billion parameters stored in FP8: roughly **670 GB** of weights.

1. At **0.2 GB/s** (a slow external download): 670 ÷ 0.2 = 3,350 s, about **56 minutes**.
2. At **1 GB/s** (a cloud storage bucket): 670 s, about **11 minutes**.
3. At **5 GB/s** (a cache in the same datacenter): 134 s, a bit over **2 minutes**.
4. The same model in **FP16** is about 1,340 GB, so every one of those times doubles.

(The bandwidth figures are illustrative; the point is the ratio. Moving the source closer and quantizing are
multiplicative wins.)

</Worked>

### 4. Engine startup

Inference engines like **vLLM** and **SGLang** are fast to start up. But engines like **TensorRT-LLM**, and models
optimized with **PyTorch** compilation, have a **compilation step** that targets the specific hardware to build the
model inference engine. These compilations **often take several minutes**.

In those cases, **caching built engines** massively improves cold start times. Both TensorRT-LLM and PyTorch have
caching mechanisms that make this possible. The catch: you always need to load a cached engine onto an instance with
**exactly the same GPU type, CUDA version, and software dependencies** as the environment it was built in, or it won’t
run properly. A compiled engine is specialized to one precise setup, which is also why the pinned, reproducible
containers from the last lesson matter so much.

## Routing, load balancing, and queueing

Once multiple replicas are online, the system has to decide which requests go to which replicas. Two kinds of
components make these decisions:

- A **router** works at the **request** level to determine the ideal place for a given request. It answers: *where
  **should** this request go?*
- A **load balancer** works at the **system** level to even out requests between multiple options. It answers: *where
  **could** this request go?*

In complex systems there isn’t just one of each. Routing occurs throughout the stack, with load balancers injected at key
points to keep system-wide performance stable.

Overall, you want to split load evenly across replicas. But the book warns it’s not as simple as “I have 3 replicas and
12 requests, so let’s put 4 on each.” Two things break the naive split:

- **Requests aren’t equal.** Each request may have a different number of input tokens. If most requests have 100 input
  tokens, one request with 10,000 will unbalance a simple system.
- **Some replicas are better for some requests:**
  - **KV cache-aware routing** sends a request to a replica that already has a matching prefix in its KV cache (see
    [Caching](/learn/caching)), skipping prefill work.
  - **LoRA-aware routing** sends a request to a replica that already has the desired
    <Term def="A small add-on fine-tune that changes a model’s behavior. Replicas can keep several in memory and swap between them.">LoRA</Term>
    fine-tuned weights in memory, instead of one that would have to load them.

Intelligent request routing uses information from the inference engine and from orchestrators like **NVIDIA Dynamo** to
route requests based on **sequence length, prefix, and LoRA needs**.

<Worked title="Why “4 requests each” goes wrong">

1. Three replicas, twelve requests. Eleven have **100** input tokens; one has **10,000**.
2. Total prefill work: 11 × 100 + 10,000 = **11,100 tokens**. A fair share is 3,700 tokens per replica.
3. **Naive split (4 requests each):** the replica that gets the big request has 3 × 100 + 10,000 = **10,300 tokens**;
   the other two have **400** each. One replica does 25× the work of its neighbors, and the three users stuck behind
   the big request wait far longer than everyone else.
4. **Token-aware split:** give the 10,000-token request a replica largely to itself and spread the eleven small ones
   across the other two. Now no small request waits behind the giant one.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Common load-balancing strategies.** *Round robin* hands requests out in turn; it’s simple but blind to request size,
  which is exactly the failure above. *Least outstanding requests* sends each request to the replica with the fewest in
  flight, which adapts to slow replicas. *Power of two choices* picks two replicas at random and sends the request to
  the less loaded one: nearly as good as checking every replica, at a fraction of the cost. *Consistent hashing* maps
  something like a user or conversation ID to the same replica every time, the classic way to get cache affinity. Real
  inference routers blend these: prefer a replica with the right prefix or LoRA, as long as it isn’t overloaded.
</Note>

Load balancing and routing are not enough. When the system receives **more traffic than it can handle**, it needs a way
to hold onto requests while it scales up or waits for existing capacity to free up. The infrastructure primitive for
this is a **queue**:

- A standard queue is **first-in, first-out** for excess requests.
- A more complex implementation, like a **priority queue**, can, for example, **give paid users priority over free
  users** in high-traffic scenarios.

One detail the book calls out: **as new replicas come online, make sure the queue sees them**, so requests don’t keep
waiting for the existing replicas. **Each new replica should immediately be assigned up to its concurrency limit in
queued traffic once it’s active.** Otherwise you’ve paid for a cold start and the new replica sits idle while users
wait.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Queues need limits too.** An unbounded queue during a huge spike just turns fast failures into slow failures:
  requests wait minutes and then time out anyway. Production systems usually cap how long a request may wait, and when
  the queue is too deep they reject new requests right away (commonly with an HTTP 429 “too many requests” response) so
  clients can back off and retry, rather than piling on.
</Note>

<Analogy title="Think of it like a busy restaurant">
  The **load balancer** is the host looking at which sections have free tables. The **router** is the host remembering
  that *this* customer is a regular whose favorite waiter is in section B (the matching KV cache). The **queue** is the
  waiting area by the door, maybe with a VIP line. And when a new section opens, the host must actually start seating
  people there, immediately and up to its capacity.
</Analogy>

## Scale to zero

Advanced autoscaling systems implement **scale to zero**: the system scales down to **zero active replicas** when there
is no traffic, then scales up automatically when traffic is received. It relies on two prerequisites:

- **Fast cold starts**, because a user may be waiting live on that first request.
- **Robust queueing**, to hold incoming requests until a replica is live.

Even with both, scale to zero isn’t a fit for every workload. It’s great for:

- **Development**, when testing is bursty and latency on the first request is unimportant.
- **Production apps that only get traffic periodically**, like an agent only used during business hours in one country,
  or an offline system built for daily batch jobs.

<Worked title="What scale to zero saves for a business-hours agent">

1. An internal agent is used **9 hours a day, 5 days a week**: 45 hours out of the 168 hours in a week.
2. Always on, one replica costs 168 hours of GPU time per week. Scaled to zero outside business hours, it costs about
   **45 hours**, roughly **27%** of the always-on bill, a saving of about 73%.
3. The price: the first user each morning waits through a cold start (or you schedule a warm-up just before the
   workday starts).

</Worked>

<Note kind="warn">
  The book’s warning: if you’re relying on scale to zero to keep costs low for a **latency-sensitive** app that gets
  **light, unscheduled** traffic, it’s probably a sign your AI application **isn’t ready for dedicated infrastructure
  yet**, and should use pay-per-token APIs until greater scale is reached. Users of such an app would hit cold starts
  constantly.
</Note>

## Scaling each component independently

AI applications are increasingly **multi-model, multi-stage compound AI workloads**, where inference engineers
coordinate several steps to fulfill a single request. Those steps can have very different hardware needs. The book’s
voice example:

- A **voice activity detector** needs a far less powerful GPU than the transcription model it chunks audio for.
- The **transcription model** needs more.
- The **LLM** that processes the transcript may need a full node with multiple GPUs.

The scaling parameters differ for each step too (concurrency targets, cold start times, traffic patterns). So you need to
**decompose autoscaling decisions and scale each step individually**, right-sizing resources per step and avoiding both
bottlenecks and overprovisioning. The book’s Figure 7.10 shows each model in the pipeline with its own hardware and its
own scaling.

But **every model in the pipeline should run in the same cluster.**

<Worked title="Why pipelines stay in one cluster">

1. Sending a message within a cluster takes **10 ms**; between clusters it takes **50 ms**. That’s **40 ms** extra per
   hop.
2. A **5-step** pipeline crosses clusters 5 times: 5 × 40 ms = **200 ms** added.
3. Against a **one-second** latency SLA, 200 ms is **20%** of the entire budget, spent on nothing but network hops.

</Worked>

<KeyPoints>
- Autoscaling keeps capacity matched to demand: enough to meet latency SLAs, no idle GPUs.
- Kubernetes runs **replicas** (each on its own instance) in a **cluster** with a **control plane** (routing and scaling
  decisions) and a **worker plane** (containers).
- Scale on **traffic** (proactive) *and* **utilization** (lagging); they disagree when a few requests are huge.
- Five knobs: min and max replicas, the window, the scale-down delay, and the **concurrency target**.
  Requests in flight = arrival rate × time per request (Little’s law), divided by the target, gives replicas needed.
- **Continuous batching** swaps requests in token by token. Pick the largest batch size that meets your SLA, and make
  the concurrency target match it.
- **Cold starts** = GPU procurement + image loading + weight loading + engine startup. Slow cold starts force
  over-provisioning. Warm pools, smaller images, quantized weights, a nearby weight cache, and cached compiled engines
  (on identical hardware and software) each attack one stage.
- **Routers** pick the best replica (by prefix, LoRA, and sequence length), **load balancers** even out load, and
  **queues** (FIFO or priority) absorb overflow. New replicas must pull from the queue immediately.
- **Scale to zero** needs fast cold starts and robust queueing, and suits bursty or scheduled workloads.
- **Scale each pipeline step independently**, but keep them all in one cluster.
</KeyPoints>
