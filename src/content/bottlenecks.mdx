<TLDR>
  A GPU can be held back by two things: <Compute>compute</Compute>, how fast it can **do math**, or <Memory>memory bandwidth</Memory>,
  how fast it can **move data**. LLM **prefill hits the math limit**; LLM **decode hits the memory limit**. Almost every optimization in this book is a way to push on whichever limit you're stuck against.
</TLDR>

## A GPU's two resources

- <Compute>Compute</Compute>: how many math operations per second the GPU can do, measured in **FLOPS**. An H100 does
  roughly **989 trillion** 16-bit operations per second.
- <Memory>Memory bandwidth</Memory>: how many bytes per second it can move from its memory to its math units. An H100
  moves about **3.35 terabytes per second**.

In a perfect world both would be busy 100% of the time. In reality, one is always waiting on the other. Whichever one
is maxed out is the **bottleneck**, and here's the crucial part: **speeding up the other one does nothing.** If you're
stuck waiting for data, faster math is useless, and vice versa.

<Analogy title="Think of it like a kitchen">
  The **chef** is compute. The **waiter** carrying ingredients from the storeroom is memory bandwidth. If the chef is
  a genius but the waiter is slow, the chef stands around waiting. Hiring a faster chef won't help. You need a faster
  waiter, or you need to get more cooking out of every trip the waiter makes.
</Analogy>

## The magic ratio: ops per byte

Divide a GPU's compute by its bandwidth and you get its **ops:byte ratio**:

```
H100:  989 TFLOPS ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 295 operations per byte
```

This means that to keep an H100 perfectly balanced, your work needs to do **~295 math operations for every byte it
loads from memory**.

Every piece of work has its own version of that ratio, called **arithmetic intensity**: *how much math you do per byte
you move*.

- Arithmetic intensity **below** the GPU's ratio (not much math per byte) means you're **memory-bound**. The chef is
  waiting on the waiter.
- Arithmetic intensity **above** the GPU's ratio (lots of math per byte) means you're **compute-bound**. The chef is
  the busy one.

## Why prefill and decode are opposites

<Compute>Prefill</Compute> loads the model's weights once and uses them on *every* prompt token at once. That's
thousands of tokens' worth of math for a single trip through memory: **very high arithmetic intensity → compute-bound.**

<Memory>Decode</Memory> produces **one token per step**, but to produce that one token it has to read **every single
weight in the model**. That's a mountain of data moved for a tiny bit of math: **low arithmetic intensity →
memory-bound.**

The book works through the exact numbers for one attention calculation during decode and gets an arithmetic intensity
of about **62**, far below the H100's 295. The GPU spends most of its time waiting for data.

| Workload | Bottleneck |
|---|---|
| LLM **prefill** | <Compute>Compute</Compute> |
| LLM **decode** | <Memory>Memory bandwidth</Memory> |
| **Image & video** generation | <Compute>Compute</Compute> (like prefill, it works on everything at once) |

## Feel it: the decode speed limit

Because decode must read all the weights for every token, there's a simple back-of-the-envelope ceiling for how fast
**one user** can ever get tokens:

```
max tokens/sec ≈ memory bandwidth ÷ model size in bytes
```

<SpeedLimit />

Notice what moves the number: a **smaller model**, **smaller numbers** (FP8 instead of FP16, called *quantization*), or
a **GPU with more bandwidth**. Faster compute doesn't appear anywhere in that formula, because decode isn't
limited by compute.

## The roofline: seeing the bottleneck

Engineers draw this as a **roofline chart**. The horizontal axis is arithmetic intensity. The "roof" has a slanted part
(limited by memory bandwidth) and a flat part (limited by compute). Wherever your workload sits tells you what's holding
it back.

<Roofline />

Play with it and notice three things:

1. **At batch size 1, decode sits deep in memory-bound territory.** The GPU's math units are mostly idle.
2. **Increasing the batch pushes the dot to the right.** The weights are loaded once and shared by the whole batch, so
   you get more math per byte. **Total throughput shoots up**, and per-user speed barely changes at first. This is
   why batching is the #1 trick in inference.
3. **Now raise the conversation length.** Every user's KV cache has to be read too, so memory traffic grows again,
   per-user speed drops, and eventually you **run out of GPU memory**. That tension (batch size vs. KV cache) is
   something you'll run into again and again.

<Note kind="deeper">
  The chart is a simplified model (an 8B model in FP16, ignoring overheads). Calculating arithmetic intensity by hand
  isn't something inference engineers do every day, but doing it once builds the intuition you'll use constantly.
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- GPUs have two limits: <Compute>compute</Compute> (math/sec) and <Memory>memory bandwidth</Memory> (bytes/sec).
- **Ops:byte ratio** = compute ÷ bandwidth (~295 for an H100). **Arithmetic intensity** = math ÷ bytes for your work.
- Below the ratio → memory-bound. Above → compute-bound. Only fixing the actual bottleneck helps.
- **Prefill** and **image/video** are compute-bound. **Decode** is memory-bound.
- **Batching** raises decode's arithmetic intensity, massively improving throughput. The KV cache pushes back.
</KeyPoints>
