<TLDR>
  NVIDIA releases a new GPU **architecture** every year or two, named after a scientist: Hopper, Blackwell, Rubin. The
  **letter** in a GPU's name (H100, B200) tells you the architecture, and each new one brings more speed plus new
  inference features like lower-precision number formats. Most inference today runs on **Hopper and Blackwell**.
</TLDR>

## Hardware moves slowly

Designing a chip takes years. Between locking in a design
(<Term def="The point where a chip design is finalized and sent off for manufacturing.">tapeout</Term>), testing it, and
shipping it, there's a long lead time. So even brand-new GPUs were designed when AI looked very different from today.
A good GPU architecture has to *predict* how people will use it over its lifetime.

For years, the main job of GPUs was **training**. Now that **inference** is becoming the dominant use, the latest
architectures are adding inference-focused features.

## How to read a GPU's name

Take **B200**:

- The **letter** is the architecture generation (B = Blackwell). This part is very meaningful.
- The **number** identifies a specific model within that generation. The numbers are a bit arbitrary, but **bigger
  usually means larger, more powerful, and more expensive**.

For example, the **H100** replaced the previous generation's **A100**. The **H200** is a bigger GPU in the same Hopper
generation, and it was in turn surpassed by the **B200**.

## The family tree

NVIDIA has named its architectures after famous scientists since 1998. The book's timeline covers 2017 onwards:

| Year | Architecture | Named after | Example GPUs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | Volta | Alessandro Volta | V100 |
| 2018 | Turing | Alan Turing | T4 |
| 2020 | Ampere | André-Marie Ampère | A10, A100 |
| 2022 | Hopper | Grace Hopper | H100, H200 |
| 2022 | Ada Lovelace | Ada Lovelace | L4, L40 |
| 2024 | Blackwell | David Blackwell | B200, B300 |
| 2026 | Rubin | Vera Rubin | (upcoming) |
| 2028 | Feynman | Richard Feynman | (announced) |

Inference engineers usually work with the **three to five most recent generations**. Even for cost-sensitive work, newer
GPUs are often *cheaper* at scale because they're so much more efficient. You'll still bump into **Turing (T4)** and
**Ampere (A10, A100)** in older or low-traffic systems, but most deployments today use **Lovelace, Hopper, or
Blackwell**.

## Hopper (H100, H200)

| GPU | FP8 compute (dense) | Memory | Bandwidth |
|---|---|---|---|
| H100 | 1,979 teraFLOPS | 80 GB | 3.35 TB/s |
| H200 | 1,979 teraFLOPS | 141 GB | 4.8 TB/s |

Named after Rear Admiral **Grace Hopper**, Hopper launched in **March 2022** with the H100. What it brought:

- **FP8 support**: an 8-bit number format. FP8 Tensor Cores are **twice as fast** as FP16 ones, and moving FP8 numbers
  takes **half the memory bandwidth**. This doesn't mean everything runs exactly 2× faster (the quantization lesson in
  Chapter 5 explains why), but it's a big win for workloads that can use FP8.
- **Fourth-generation Tensor Cores** inside more, faster Streaming Multiprocessors.
- New tools for kernel writers: dynamic programming instructions, thread block clusters, and distributed shared memory.
- **Asynchronous** data movement and execution, meaning the GPU can load the next chunk of data while it's still
  crunching the current one. **FlashAttention 3** uses this to speed up attention on Hopper.

The H100 and H200 are among the **most widely used inference GPUs**, and for good reason: new enough to be fast,
established enough to have industry-wide support and highly tuned kernels, and right-sized for common workloads of
every kind.

## Ada Lovelace (L4, L40)

| GPU | FP8 compute (dense) | Memory | Bandwidth |
|---|---|---|---|
| L4 | 242 teraFLOPS | 24 GB | 300 GB/s |
| L40 | 362 teraFLOPS | 48 GB | 864 GB/s |

Named after **Ada Lovelace**, the first computer programmer, this architecture arrived just **six months after
Hopper**. It's more of a sibling than a successor: it also supports FP8, but it's aimed more at **graphics** than AI.

Its big limitation: **no NVLink**, the high-speed GPU-to-GPU connection. Eight-GPU Hopper and Blackwell machines rely on
NVLink to split big models across GPUs efficiently. Lovelace GPUs have to work alone, or use slower methods like
<Term def="Splitting a model’s layers across GPUs in sequence, like an assembly line. Less efficient than other ways of splitting.">pipeline parallelism</Term>.

- **L4**: a cheap, convenient way to run **small models**, like text embeddings and computer vision.
- **L40**: generally **not a great choice** for inference. For the same amount of memory, a *slice* of an H100 (see
  <a href="/learn/instances">MIG in the next lesson</a>) gives you much more compute and bandwidth.

## Blackwell (B200, B300)

| GPU | FP8 compute (dense) | Memory | Bandwidth |
|---|---|---|---|
| B200 | ~5 petaFLOPS | 192 GB | Up to 8 TB/s |
| B300 | ~5 petaFLOPS | 288 GB | Up to 8 TB/s |

Named after mathematician **David Blackwell**, Blackwell launched in **November 2024** with the B200, followed by the
B300. (A B100 exists, but it's rare for inference.)

- Where Hopper added FP8, Blackwell goes further with **FP4** (4-bit numbers) plus
  <Term def="Number formats that store small groups of values with a shared scale factor, to keep more accuracy at very low precision.">microscaling formats</Term>
  (**MXFP8, MXFP4, NVFP4**) that preserve quality better at low precision. Chapter 5 covers these.
- More asynchronous features for moving data between memory and the Tensor Cores. **FlashAttention 4** leans heavily on
  them, splitting loads, math, and writes into overlapping pipelines.

The B200 and B300 are the **new gold standard**: the best performance for large language models and heavy workloads
like video generation. Software support, optimized kernels, and availability have only recently caught up, which marks
a big transition in the industry.

## Rubin and Feynman: what's next

**Rubin**, named for astronomer **Vera Rubin**, launches in **2026**. A word of caution from the book: **don't judge a
new architecture until you can benchmark it on real workloads.** It typically takes about a year after launch for the
software ecosystem to fully catch up.

What's known so far:

- Rubin uses **HBM4** memory, an upgrade from the HBM3 and HBM3e of recent generations. Memory-bound work like
  <Memory>LLM decode</Memory> will benefit directly.
- Rubin introduces the **CPX**, a separate chip built for compute-bound work like <Compute>LLM prefill</Compute>. It
  will be part of NVIDIA's rack-scale systems for high-volume inference. (This matches the idea of splitting prefill and
  decode onto different hardware, called *disaggregation*, in Chapter 5.)

After Rubin comes **Feynman** in **2028**. Little is known yet, but expect bigger, more powerful chips with faster memory.

## Grace and Vera: NVIDIA's CPUs

NVIDIA also makes its own ARM-based CPUs and pairs them with GPUs on **superchips** like the **GH200** and **GB200**.
The "G" stands for **Grace** (as in Grace Hopper).

Grace CPUs are strong overall, but what matters for inference is their **much faster connection to the GPU**: NVLink
Chip-to-Chip gives up to **900 GB/s** (both directions combined) between CPU memory and GPU memory. That's several times
faster than
<Term def="The standard connection between a CPU and add-in cards like GPUs in ordinary servers.">PCIe</Term>, the usual
CPU-to-GPU link. The book's diagram shows this: an ordinary CPU talks to the GPU through a narrow pipe, while Grace gets
a much wider one.

Why care? Some setups **offload** data to CPU memory, which is far larger than GPU memory: things like
<Term def="Small fine-tune add-ons that change a model’s behavior.">LoRA</Term> weights or KV caches from earlier
requests. With Grace, fetching them back is much faster.

For Rubin systems, the **Vera** CPU (also named for Vera Rubin) replaces Grace.

<KeyPoints>
- GPU name = **architecture letter** + **model number** (bigger number, bigger GPU).
- Most inference today: **Lovelace (L4, L40)**, **Hopper (H100, H200)**, **Blackwell (B200, B300)**.
- **Hopper** added **FP8** and async features (FlashAttention 3). It's the workhorse of the industry.
- **Lovelace** has **no NVLink**. The L4 is good for small models, and the L40 is usually a poor pick.
- **Blackwell** adds **FP4** and microscaling formats. It's the new gold standard.
- **Rubin** (2026) brings **HBM4** and the prefill-focused **CPX**. **Grace/Vera** CPUs have a 900 GB/s link to the GPU.
</KeyPoints>
