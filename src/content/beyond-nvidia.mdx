## Other datacenter accelerators

NVIDIA's lead in AI hardware has made it the world's most valuable company, but it's far from the only option. Giants
like Amazon and Google and a big crop of startups are pouring billions into alternatives. The book's short list:

| Company | Stage | Flagship product |
|---|---|---|
| **AMD** | Public | **MI350 GPU**: a datacenter GPU with competitive specs, running on AMD's own software stack. |
| **AWS** | Public | **Inferentia** and **Trainium**: a pair of chips built for inference and training, respectively, on AWS. |
| **Cerebras** | Startup | **WSE-3**: a chip the size of a whole silicon wafer, with extremely high memory bandwidth to remove decode bottlenecks. |
| **Etched** | Startup | **Sohu**: an <Term def="Application-Specific Integrated Circuit: a chip designed to do one specific job extremely well, rather than general-purpose work.">ASIC</Term> built specifically for the transformer architecture. |
| **Furiosa** | Startup | **RNGD**: a power-efficient accelerator designed for tensor operations. |
| **Google** | Public | **TPU** (Tensor Processing Unit): an AI-specific ASIC for inference and training. |
| **Groq** | Startup | **LPU**: a composable "language processing unit" that relies on SRAM for high memory bandwidth. |
| **Qualcomm** | Public | **Cloud AI 100 Ultra**: a full-sized accelerator built from multiple power-efficient mobile GPUs. |
| **SambaNova** | Startup | **RDU**: a Reconfigurable Dataflow Unit with lots of memory for trillion-parameter models. |

### What each one is betting on

GPUs are fairly general-purpose. Each competitor is betting on one specific place where it can win:

- **Memory bandwidth**: Cerebras and Groq get very high tokens-per-second on LLMs by speeding up
  <Memory>decode</Memory> with ultra-high-bandwidth memory. (Remember: decode is memory-bound.)
- **Power efficiency**: Furiosa and Qualcomm design chips that use less power, so they're cheaper to operate.
- **Platform integration**: Amazon and Google tie their chips deeply into their own clouds and their own closed models.

### What they all struggle with

- **Software**: without CUDA (NVIDIA's software platform, covered in Chapter 4), each company has to rebuild the entire
  inference software stack for its chips.
- **Manufacturing**: they have to build what the book calls the most complex object humans have ever made.
- **Distribution**: once a chip exists, it still has to be installed in datacenters and brought online before anyone
  can use it.

More competition is good for every inference engineer. It speeds up innovation, and it'll only grow as inference gets
more valuable, including options outside the datacenter entirely.

## Local inference

**Local inference** (also called *edge*, *client-side*, or *on-device* inference) means running the model **directly on
the user's own device** instead of a server.

<Analogy>
  Cloud inference is **ordering from a restaurant**: professional kitchen, huge capacity, but you wait for delivery and
  pay every time. Local inference is **cooking at home**: instant and private, but your kitchen is small and there's
  only so much you can make.
</Analogy>

**Four big advantages:**

1. **Zero network latency**: no round trip to a server, saving tens or even hundreds of milliseconds.
2. **Independence**: works without internet, and doesn't care about server traffic or outages.
3. **Privacy**: the user's data never leaves their device.
4. **Cost**: datacenter GPUs are expensive, but on-device inference is free for the developer, which enables new
   business models.

**Four big weaknesses** (and why most inference still happens in the cloud):

1. **Weak hardware**: even high-end home machines are a fraction of a datacenter GPU.
2. **Heat**: devices have worse cooling than datacenters, which limits speed further.
3. **Fragmentation**: endless combinations of hardware and software make it hard to standardize.
4. **Battery**: inference is demanding and drains laptop and phone batteries fast.

<Note kind="warn">
  Build for your **real users**. An AI enthusiast might have the newest phone and a powerful computer, but the median
  user probably has older, weaker devices.
</Note>

Still, local inference is moving from experiments into production, with a strong ecosystem of hardware and software and
an active community closely tied to open models.

### Desktop inference

The classic setup is a workstation or gaming PC with one or two high-end NVIDIA or AMD cards. Researchers and
enthusiasts use these, but they're a small part of the desktop market.

Increasingly, **Apple leads desktop inference**. Apple's M-series chips have the CPU and GPU share one pool of
<Term def="A single pool of memory shared by the CPU and GPU, instead of separate RAM and VRAM.">unified memory</Term>,
so the GPU gets access to **far more memory**, but at **slower speeds**. The top machines from each side show the
trade-off:

| | NVIDIA RTX 5090 | Apple M3 Ultra |
|---|---|---|
| Memory | 32 GB | 512 GB |
| Bandwidth | 1,792 GB/s | 819 GB/s |
| Cost (full computer) | $5,000 | $10,000 |

The same pattern holds for mid-range machines at more reasonable prices. Low-end machines like Chromebooks can't run
meaningful local inference at all.

What people run at home:

- **Big open LLMs**: with tools like **Ollama** and **llama.cpp**, it's possible to run heavily quantized models with
  **100B+ parameters** on high-end personal hardware.
- **Mixture of Experts models** help a lot here. A single user's request only touches the **active** experts, a
  fraction of the total weights. (Try the MoE option in the demo above.)
- **Image generation** is popular on personal computers, especially through **ComfyUI**, a tool for wiring image model
  components into pipelines.
- **Smaller LLMs and speech models** run fine on mid-range computers, and browser libraries like **WebLLM** are working
  to bring this to everyday users.

### Mobile inference

**Phones are where most on-device inference happens today.** Both major platforms give developers tools for it:

- **Android**: Google's **AI Edge SDK** and **ML Kit GenAI APIs**, which work with **Gemini Nano** and open **Gemma**
  models.
- **iOS**: Apple's **Foundation Models** and **Core ML** frameworks, with APIs for models across modalities.

Phones have very limited hardware and battery, so this is even harder. **Even high-end phones struggle with models over
one or two billion parameters.** But some jobs fit well:

- **Transcription and speech synthesis** are latency-sensitive, and some models are small enough to run in real time
  on modern phones.
- **Discrete tasks like translation** can be handled by small fine-tuned models.

The future isn't local *or* cloud; it's **both**. Small models and quick requests run on the device, and heavier work
stays on datacenter GPUs.

<KeyPoints>
- Alternatives to NVIDIA bet on **memory bandwidth** (Cerebras, Groq), **power efficiency** (Furiosa, Qualcomm), or **cloud
  integration** (AWS, Google).
- Their shared hurdles: **software** (no CUDA), **manufacturing**, and **distribution**.
- **Local inference**: no network delay, works offline, private, free for the developer, but has weak hardware, heat,
  fragmentation, and battery limits.
- **Desktops**: NVIDIA = faster memory, Apple = much more memory. Quantization and MoE make 100B+ models possible at home.
- **Phones**: most on-device inference today, but realistically only models up to about 1–2B parameters.
</KeyPoints>
