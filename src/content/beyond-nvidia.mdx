<TLDR>
  NVIDIA leads, but many companies build AI chips, each betting on one edge: **faster memory**, **lower power**, or
  **tight integration with a cloud**, and all fighting the same three battles: **software, manufacturing,
  distribution**. Models can also run **on the user's own laptop or phone**: instant, private, and free for the
  developer, but on far weaker hardware. The real future is **both**: small, quick work on the device, heavy work in the
  cloud.
</TLDR>

## Other datacenter accelerators

NVIDIA's leadership in AI hardware has made it **the world's most valuable company**, but it's far from the only
company making hardware that can run inference. Fellow giants like **Amazon** and **Google**, plus a large crop of
**startups**, are pouring **billions of dollars** into alternatives. The book focuses on NVIDIA, but gives this short
list of the other notable options:

| Company | Stage | Flagship product |
|---|---|---|
| **AMD** | Public | **MI350 GPU**: a datacenter GPU with competitive specs on AMD's own software stack. |
| **AWS** | Public | **Inferentia** and **Trainium**: a pair of chips purpose-built for inference and training, respectively, on AWS. |
| **Cerebras** | Startup | **WSE-3**: a wafer-scale chip with extremely high memory bandwidth to remove decode bottlenecks. |
| **Etched** | Startup | **Sohu**: an <Term def="Application-Specific Integrated Circuit: a chip designed to do one specific job extremely well, instead of general-purpose work.">ASIC</Term> for the transformer architecture. |
| **Furiosa** | Startup | **RNGD**: a power-efficient accelerator designed for tensor contraction operations. |
| **Google** | Public | **TPU**: a Tensor Processing Unit, an AI-specific ASIC built for inference and training. |
| **Groq** | Startup | **LPU**: a composable language processing unit that relies on SRAM for high memory bandwidth. |
| **Qualcomm** | Public | **Cloud AI 100 Ultra**: a full-sized accelerator composed of multiple power-efficient mobile GPUs. |
| **SambaNova** | Startup | **RDU**: a Reconfigurable Dataflow Unit with a large memory allocation for trillion-parameter models. |

<Note kind="beyond">
  A few terms from the table, unpacked:

  - **Wafer-scale** (Cerebras): chips are normally cut out of a round silicon wafer, many per wafer. A wafer-scale chip
    uses **the whole wafer as one chip**, so a huge amount of fast on-chip memory sits right next to the compute.
  - **ASIC** (Etched, Google): instead of general-purpose hardware, an ASIC is designed for a narrow job, like running
    the transformer architecture, and does it with less wasted silicon and power.
  - **Tensor contraction** (Furiosa): the general name for operations like matrix multiplication, where numbers along a
    shared dimension are multiplied and summed.
  - **SRAM for bandwidth** (Groq): recall from the <a href="/learn/gpu-anatomy">GPU anatomy lesson</a> that SRAM is the
    fast, expensive on-chip memory GPUs only use for small caches. Building the main memory out of SRAM gives enormous
    bandwidth but very little capacity per chip, which is why the LPU is *composable*: many chips combine to hold one
    model.
</Note>

### What each competitor is betting on

GPUs are **fairly general-purpose** accelerators. Every company competing for inference workloads is betting on a
**specific edge** where its product can win:

- **Memory bandwidth**: startups like **Cerebras** and **Groq** get very high **tokens-per-second** scores on LLMs by
  accelerating <Memory>decode</Memory> on ultra-high-bandwidth memory. This follows directly from the
  <a href="/learn/bottlenecks">bottlenecks lesson</a>: decode is memory-bound, so faster memory means faster tokens.
- **Power efficiency**: companies like **Furiosa** and **Qualcomm** design chips that use less power, which means
  **cheaper operating costs**.
- **Platform integration**: enterprises like **Amazon** and **Google** build deep integrations with their **cloud
  platforms** and their **proprietary closed models**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Some public numbers show how extreme the memory-bandwidth bet is. Cerebras says the WSE-3 has about 44 GB of on-chip
  SRAM with on the order of 20 petabytes per second of memory bandwidth, thousands of times an H100’s 3.35 TB/s. Groq
  says each LPU has only about 230 MB of SRAM, so holding a 70B model in 8-bit (70 GB) takes roughly 300 chips working
  together. The trade-off is the one from the GPU anatomy lesson: SRAM is fast but tiny and expensive, DRAM is big
  but slower.
</Note>

### What they all struggle with

Each accelerator has its winning use cases, but they share three common challenges:

1. **Software.** Without **CUDA**, NVIDIA's software platform (the <a href="/learn/cuda-and-kernels">CUDA lesson</a>),
   hardware providers have to **rebuild the entire inference stack** for their accelerators: kernels, frameworks,
   engines, everything. This is NVIDIA's deepest advantage.
2. **Manufacturing.** A company has to assemble what the book calls **the most complex object humankind has ever
   created**.
3. **Distribution.** Even after a chip is manufactured, it has to be **installed and brought online** in datacenters
   before it puts any capacity on the market.

<Note kind="beyond">
  A risk specific to specialized chips (our reasoning, not the book’s): the more tightly a chip is designed around one
  architecture, the more it depends on that architecture staying dominant. A transformer-only ASIC is extremely
  efficient for transformers, but the <a href="/learn/faster-attention">faster attention lesson</a> showed research
  into alternatives like Mamba-style state-space models and hybrids. General-purpose GPUs can run whatever comes next;
  a narrow ASIC might not.
</Note>

The book's view of all this is positive: **competition accelerates innovation**, and a market with more hardware
options is good for every inference engineer. That competition will grow as inference workloads become more valuable,
and so will exploration of options **outside the datacenter**, like local inference.

## Local inference

**Local inference**, also called **edge**, **client-side**, or **on-device** inference, means running the model
**directly on the end user's device** instead of on a centralized server.

<Analogy>
  Cloud inference is **ordering from a restaurant**: a professional kitchen with huge capacity, but you wait for
  delivery and someone pays for every meal. Local inference is **cooking at home**: instant, private, and free, but
  your kitchen is small and there's only so much you can make.
</Analogy>

### Four massive advantages

1. **Zero network latency.** No communication with a server, which saves **tens or even hundreds of milliseconds**
   per request.
2. **Independence.** No dependency on an internet connection, and no impact from high server traffic or downtime.
3. **Improved privacy.** The user's data **never leaves their device**.
4. **Cost.** Datacenter GPUs are expensive, but edge inference is **free for the developer**: the user's device does
   the work. That **unlocks new business models** that couldn't afford per-request GPU costs.

### Four weaknesses (and why most inference is still in the cloud)

Local inference sounds perfect in theory. In practice there's a reason most inference happens in the cloud:

1. **Hardware capabilities.** Even high-end prosumer desktops offer a **fraction** of the speed and power of datacenter
   GPUs.
2. **Thermal constraints.** Local devices have **worse cooling** than datacenters, which limits their speed and power
   further: they slow themselves down when they get hot.
3. **Fragmented support matrix.** There are **endless combinations** of hardware and software (chips, operating
   systems, drivers, versions), which makes standardization hard.
4. **Battery life.** Inference is demanding and **quickly drains** laptop and phone batteries.

<Note kind="warn">
  **Build for your real audience.** An AI enthusiast may have the latest phone and a powerful computer, but the
  **median user** is more likely to have **older devices with less powerful components**. A local feature that only
  works on flagship hardware only works for a small slice of your users.
</Note>

Even so, the book sees local inference **turning the corner from experimentation to production**, with a strong
ecosystem across hardware and software and a **vibrant community closely tied to the world of open models**.

## Desktop inference

The classic local device is a **workstation or gaming PC** with one or two high-end consumer GPUs from NVIDIA or AMD.
Researchers and enthusiasts use these setups, but they're a **small portion** of the desktop inference market.

Increasingly, **Apple leads desktop inference**. Apple's custom **M-series** chips have the CPU and GPU draw from a single
<Term def="One pool of memory shared by the CPU and GPU, instead of separate system RAM and GPU VRAM.">unified memory</Term>,
which gives the GPU access to **far more memory**, but at **slower speeds**. The highest-end options from each company
show the trade-off between **memory capacity and speed**:

| | NVIDIA RTX 5090 | Apple M3 Ultra |
|---|---|---|
| Memory | 32 GB | 512 GB |
| Bandwidth | 1,792 GB/s | 819 GB/s |
| Cost (full computer) | $5,000 | $10,000 |

This trend continues through **midrange hardware** at more reasonable prices. At the low end, computers like
**Chromebooks** aren't equipped to run any meaningful local inference.

<Worked title="The RTX 5090 vs. the M3 Ultra, by the numbers">

Using the book's table and the <a href="/learn/bottlenecks">decode speed limit</a> (bandwidth ÷ bytes read per
token):

1. **Price per GB of memory:** $5,000 ÷ 32 GB ≈ **$156/GB** for the 5090, vs. $10,000 ÷ 512 GB ≈ **$20/GB** for the Mac.
2. **An 8B model at 4-bit** (about 4 GB): fits both. Ceiling ≈ 1,792 ÷ 4 ≈ **448 tokens/s** on the 5090, vs.
   819 ÷ 4 ≈ **205 tokens/s** on the Mac. The 5090 wins: more bandwidth.
3. **A 70B model at 4-bit** (about 35 GB): **doesn't fit** the 5090's 32 GB. On the Mac: 819 ÷ 35 ≈ **23 tokens/s**.
4. **A 200B model at 4-bit** (about 100 GB): Mac only, ≈ 819 ÷ 100 ≈ **8 tokens/s**. It fits, but it's slow.
5. **An MoE model with 5B active parameters at 4-bit**: each token reads only about 2.5 GB of weights, so the Mac's
   ceiling jumps to ≈ **330 tokens/s**, as long as the whole model fits in its memory.

The pattern: **NVIDIA for speed when the model fits, Apple for capacity**, and MoE changes the math entirely.

</Worked>

### What people actually run at home

- **Frontier open LLMs.** The enthusiast-led open-source ecosystem focuses on running **frontier open models on
  desktops and laptops**. Running **aggressively quantized 100B+ parameter models** on high-end personal hardware is
  possible today with tools like **Ollama** and **llama.cpp**.
- **Mixture of Experts** is a tailwind. MoE models have **fewer active parameters**, so an individual user's single
  request only **touches a fraction of the model's total weights**. (In the datacenter, a big batch touches most
  experts anyway, as the <a href="/learn/attention-and-kv-cache">MoE section</a> showed. On a desktop with one user,
  you get the full benefit, as step 5 above shows.)
- **Image generation** is popular on personal computers, especially via **ComfyUI**, a tool for assembling multiple
  image model components into a single pipeline.
- **Smaller language models and speech models** are feasible on **midrange** computers.
- **In the browser**: the industry is rapidly developing browser inference libraries like **WebLLM** and other
  cross-platform standards to bring these capabilities from early adopters to the mainstream.

## Mobile inference

**Phones represent the majority of on-device inference workloads today.** Both major operating systems give developers
tools to add edge inference to their apps:

| Platform | Tools | Models |
|---|---|---|
| **Android** | Google's **AI Edge SDK** and **ML Kit GenAI APIs** | **Gemini Nano** and open-source **Gemma** models |
| **iOS** | Apple's **Foundation Models** and **Core ML** frameworks | APIs for models across modalities |

Mobile devices have **extremely limited** hardware and battery capacity, which makes inference even harder than on
desktops. **Even high-end phones struggle to run models with more than one or two billion parameters.**

<Note kind="beyond">
  A rough sense of why: flagship phones have memory bandwidth on the order of tens of GB/s (a small fraction of an RTX
  5090’s 1,792 GB/s) and must share it with everything else the phone is doing. A 2B model at 4-bit is about 1 GB of weights,
  so even the theoretical ceiling is a few dozen tokens per second, before heat and battery force the chip to slow
  down.
</Note>

Still, some modalities are **well-suited to phones**:

- **Transcription and speech synthesis** are **latency-sensitive** (a voice interface can't wait on a slow network),
  and some of these models are **small enough to run in real time** on modern phones.
- **Discrete tasks** like **translation** can be handled on the device by **small fine-tuned models**.

## Local and cloud, together

The book closes the chapter with a prediction: like any other software, the future of inference **isn't local or
cloud; it's both**, working together to power fast, seamless user experiences. **Small models and quick queries** will
run on end-user devices, while **more demanding workloads** stay on datacenter GPUs in the cloud.

<KeyPoints>
- Alternatives to NVIDIA: **AMD** (MI350), **AWS** (Inferentia/Trainium), **Cerebras** (WSE-3, wafer-scale), **Etched**
  (Sohu, transformer ASIC), **Furiosa** (RNGD), **Google** (TPU), **Groq** (SRAM-based LPU), **Qualcomm** (Cloud AI
  100 Ultra), **SambaNova** (RDU).
- Their bets: **memory bandwidth** (Cerebras, Groq → fast decode), **power efficiency** (Furiosa, Qualcomm → cheaper to
  run), **platform integration** (AWS, Google).
- Shared hurdles: **software** (no CUDA → rebuild the stack), **manufacturing**, **distribution**.
- **Local inference**: zero network latency, independence, privacy, free for the developer, vs. weak hardware, heat,
  fragmentation, and battery. Design for the **median user's device**.
- **Desktops**: Apple leads; **RTX 5090** = speed (1,792 GB/s, 32 GB), **M3 Ultra** = capacity (512 GB, 819 GB/s).
  Quantization (Ollama, llama.cpp) and **MoE** make 100B+ models possible at home.
- **Phones**: most on-device inference today (AI Edge SDK/ML Kit + Gemini Nano/Gemma; Foundation Models/Core ML), but
  realistically **≤ 1–2B parameters**. Good for speech and translation.
- The future is **local + cloud**.
</KeyPoints>
