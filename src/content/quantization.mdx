<TLDR>
  **Quantization** stores a model’s numbers with fewer bits, for example 8 instead of 16. Fewer bits means less data to
  move and faster math, so everything speeds up. The catch is that fewer bits also means less precision, so the craft
  is choosing *which* numbers to shrink, *into which format*, and *proving* the model didn’t get dumber.
</TLDR>

## Before we start: how to think about Chapter 5

This chapter covers the five big families of speed-up techniques: **quantization, speculation, caching, parallelism,
and disaggregation**. The book opens the chapter with a handful of principles that apply to all of them. They’re worth
slowing down on, because they explain *why* inference engineering looks the way it does.

**Research reaches production astonishingly fast.** In most industries, a new academic idea takes years or decades to
be adopted. In inference, techniques from a new paper are often live in production within **months or even weeks**.
There is still a gap between “it works in a paper” and “it works under real traffic at scale”, and some of the most
visible inference engineering work is exactly the work of bridging that gap.

**Constraints buy performance.** The book calls this a *core* principle: the more constraints you can introduce into an
inference system, the better performance you’ll achieve. A system that has to handle every possible request shape well
can’t be tuned for any of them. A system that only handles, say, prefill for long prompts can be tuned ruthlessly for
that. Disaggregation (later in this chapter) is the clearest example: it constrains each engine to do *only* prefill or
*only* decode.

**More traffic unlocks more optimizations.** This is the new principle the chapter adds. The more traffic you have, the
more optimizations you can afford *while keeping unit economics reasonable*. Wide model parallelism across many GPUs,
KV-aware routing, and dynamic disaggregation only make sense when a large number of GPUs (often several nodes) serve
the **same model**, both scaled up (bigger replicas) and scaled out (more replicas). A startup with one replica can’t
use most of them; a platform with hundreds of GPUs on one model can use all of them.

**Real traffic defies constraints, so tuning never ends.** Real-world usage never matches the neat shape you designed
for. But with enough volume, you can adapt the system over time to match how usage actually changes. Tuning engine
parameters, speculation settings, and model servers isn’t a one-time job: you keep improving through iterative
deployments or dynamic adjustments at runtime.

**Finding the right combination takes patient experimentation.** The book’s illustration: during an internal
hackathon, an engineer working on a **code autocomplete** model wrote a script that tried **77 different
configurations** before finding a non-obvious one that **doubled tokens per second** for a customer’s model. Nobody
could have reasoned their way to that answer from first principles; it was found by systematic search.

**Techniques interact, sometimes helping and sometimes fighting.** Two examples from the book:

- **Symbiotic:** quantizing the KV cache relieves a bottleneck in disaggregation (a smaller cache is faster to ship
  from the prefill machine to the decode machine).
- **Incompatible:** increasing the batch size reduces the spare compute that speculative decoding depends on.

The goal is always a **balanced set of optimizations** that delivers more than the sum of its parts. As you read each
technique, pay special attention to **when it’s recommended** and **what bottleneck or trade-off it introduces**.

## What quantization does

Every model is trained with its numbers (weights, activations, and other components) stored in some **native** number
format, usually **BF16** or **FP16** (16 bits per number). Native 8-bit and even 4-bit training is becoming more popular.

<Term def="Converting a finished model’s numbers to a lower-precision format after training, using calibration to preserve accuracy.">Post-training quantization</Term>
changes those weights and other values from their native format to a lower-precision one. Cutting precision in half
improves **both** phases of inference, for two different reasons:

- <Compute>Prefill</Compute> is compute-bound. Tensor Cores run lower-precision math at roughly **twice the FLOPS**
  (for example, an H100 does about 989 TFLOPS of dense FP16 math but about 1,979 TFLOPS of FP8). Twice the math per
  second means prefill finishes sooner.
- <Memory>Decode</Memory> is memory-bound. Each value now takes **half the bytes**, so the same memory bandwidth moves
  twice as many values per second. It’s *effectively* doubling bandwidth.

Quantization therefore improves **time to first token, tokens per second, and total throughput** all at once. It also
frees memory and compute that make other techniques (disaggregation, speculation, prefix caching) more effective.

<Worked title="What FP8 does to a 70B model">

Take a 70-billion-parameter model on an H100 (80 GB of memory, 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth).

1. **Size in FP16:** 70 × 10<sup>9</sup> parameters × 2 bytes = **140 GB**. That doesn’t fit on one H100 at all.
2. **Size in FP8:** 70 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 1 byte = **70 GB**. Now the weights fit, though that leaves only about 10 GB
   for the KV cache and working memory, so in practice you’d still use two GPUs or a bigger one.
3. **Decode speed limit.** From the bottlenecks lesson, the ceiling for one user is bandwidth ÷ bytes read per token:
   - FP16: 3,350 GB/s ÷ 140 GB ≈ **24 tokens/s** (on a GPU with enough memory to hold it)
   - FP8: 3,350 GB/s ÷ 70 GB ≈ **48 tokens/s**

The *theoretical* gain is 2×. The book’s real-world rule of thumb is more modest, and for good reasons (next section).

</Worked>

### Why it’s not a clean 2×

Working with quantized data introduces overhead, so going from 16 to 8 bits doesn’t make things linearly twice as fast.
In practice, **quantizing down a single level of precision generally gives 30 to 50 percent better performance for
LLMs**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Where does the rest of the 2× go? A few places:

  - **Not everything is quantized.** Attention, softmax, normalization and often the KV cache stay at higher precision,
    and their cost doesn’t shrink.
  - **Scale factors cost something.** They have to be stored, loaded, and multiplied in (more on this below).
  - **Weights-only schemes don’t speed up the math.** If only the weights are stored in low precision, they’re
    typically converted back to 16-bit just before each matmul. Decode still benefits (fewer bytes to read), but prefill
    barely does, because the math itself still runs at 16-bit speed.
  - **Other overheads don’t shrink.** Kernel launches, scheduling, networking, and tokenization take the same time
    regardless of precision.
</Note>

<Analogy>
  It’s like rounding prices in your head. “About \$3” is much faster to work with than “\$2.97”, and usually good
  enough. But if you round every item on a long receipt and then multiply the totals together, the answer can drift
  noticeably.
</Analogy>

## The risk: errors that compound

When quantization goes wrong, it can **materially reduce the model’s output quality**. The reason is that precision
errors don’t stay small: they **compound** as calculations build on each other. The book shows this by squaring and
cubing π at different precisions:

| π stored as | π² | π³ |
|---|---|---|
| 3.14159 | 9.869588 | 31.006198 |
| 3.14 | 9.8596 | 30.959144 |
| 3 | 9 | 27 |

<Worked title="How the error grows with each multiplication">

Compare each row with the true values (π² ≈ 9.869604, π³ ≈ 31.006277):

| π stored as | error in π | error in π² | error in π³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.14 | 0.05% | 0.10% | 0.15% |
| 3 | 4.5% | 8.8% | 12.9% |

The pattern: every multiplication by the slightly-wrong number adds roughly the same relative error again. A 4.5% error
becomes about 9% after one more multiplication and about 13% after two.

</Worked>

A model performs billions of multiplications per token, and its outputs feed back in as inputs for the next token. So
**most of the work in quantization is preventing precision errors and minimizing their impact on the final output**.

## The number formats you’ll meet

Quantization introduces a pile of terms and abbreviations. These are the essential number formats, with the NVIDIA GPU
architecture that first supported each:

| Name | Short | First architecture |
|---|---|---|
| 64-bit floating point | FP64 | Fermi (2010) |
| 32-bit floating point | FP32 | Kepler (2012) |
| 16-bit floating point | FP16 | Pascal (2016) |
| Brain floating point 16 | BF16 | Ampere (2020) |
| 8-bit floating point | FP8 | Hopper (2022) |
| Microscaling FP8 | MXFP8 | Blackwell (2024) |
| 8-bit integer | INT8 | Pascal (2016) |
| 6-bit floating point | FP6 | Blackwell (2024, experimental) |
| 4-bit floating point | FP4 | Blackwell (2024) |
| Microscaling FP4 | MXFP4 | Blackwell (2024) |
| NVIDIA FP4 | NVFP4 | Blackwell (2024, proprietary) |
| 4-bit integer | INT4 | Turing (2018) |

(The book spells out MXFP8 and MXFP4 as “mixed-precision” formats; the “MX” comes from *microscaling*, which is how
they work, as you’ll see below.)

Some of these you can set aside:

- **FP64** (“double precision”) is only for high-precision scientific computing, not AI training or inference.
- **FP32** is sometimes used for training but almost never for inference.
- **FP6** is still experimental at the time of writing, though AMD GPUs are adopting it quickly.

That leaves **16, 8, and 4 bits** as the primary formats for inference.

## What defines a number format

Every format has three attributes:

- **Precision**: how many bits express a single value. FP16 uses 16.
- **Type**: whether the bits represent an **integer** (no decimal point) or a **floating-point** number (with one).
- **Scale factor**: a multiplier used to map values from the low-precision format back to the higher-precision one.

Together, those determine the two properties that decide how well a format represents a model’s values:

- <Term def="The span between the smallest and largest values a number format can represent.">Dynamic range</Term>:
  the difference between the lowest and highest value the format can represent.
- <Term def="How many values share one scale factor. Fewer values per scale factor = finer granularity.">Granularity</Term>:
  how many parameters (or other values) are quantized along a single scale factor.

**Dynamic range is essential to low-precision inference without quality loss.** 16 bits can represent **65,536**
distinct values; 8 bits can represent only **256**. With so few values available, *where* they sit matters enormously:
how far apart the smallest and largest are, and how they’re spread in between.

## How floating point works (the part the book assumes)

Dynamic range explains why **floating-point formats beat integer formats for inference**. A floating-point number has
three parts:

- **Sign**: a single bit saying positive or negative.
- **Exponent**: a group of bits that together pick a power of two, i.e. *how big* the number is.
- **Mantissa**: a group of bits that give the detail: the value that gets multiplied by two to the exponent.

The book’s Figure 5.1 draws exactly this layout: sign, exponent bits and mantissa bits for floats, versus just a sign
and value bits for integers. A format like **FP8 E4M3** has a **4-bit exponent**, a **3-bit mantissa**, and the
remaining bit for the sign.

<Note kind="beyond">
  The exact decoding rule (the IEEE-style convention every format here follows) is:

  ```
  value = (−1)^sign × 2^(exponent − bias) × (1 + mantissa / 2^m)
  ```

  where **m** is the number of mantissa bits and the **bias** is 2<sup>e−1</sup> − 1 for an **e**-bit exponent (15 for
  FP16, 127 for BF16, 7 for E4M3, 1 for E2M1). The bias lets the stored exponent (always a non-negative integer) stand
  for negative powers of two too, so the format can reach small numbers. When the stored exponent is all zeros, the
  leading “1 +” is dropped (these are *subnormal* numbers), which lets the format get even closer to zero. Formats also
  reserve a few bit patterns for special values like infinity and NaN (“not a number”).
</Note>

<Worked title="Storing π in four formats">

π = 3.14159265… = 1.5707963 × 2<sup>1</sup>. So the true exponent is 1 and the mantissa has to approximate 0.5707963.

1. **FP16** (1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 mantissa bits; bias 15). Stored exponent = 1 + 15 = 16 = `10000`. Mantissa:
   0.5707963 × 2<sup>10</sup> = 584.495 → rounds to 584 = `1001001000`. Value = (1 + 584/1024) × 2 = **3.140625**.
   Bits: `0 10000 1001001000`. Error: 0.03%.
2. **BF16** (1, 8, 7; bias 127). Stored exponent = 1 + 127 = 128 = `10000000`. Mantissa: 0.5707963 × 2<sup>7</sup> =
   73.06 → 73 = `1001001`. Value = (1 + 73/128) × 2 = **3.140625**. Same value as FP16 here, by coincidence.
3. **FP8 E4M3** (1, 4, 3; bias 7). Stored exponent = 1 + 7 = 8 = `1000`. Mantissa: 0.5707963 × 2<sup>3</sup> = 4.57 →
   5 = `101`. Value = (1 + 5/8) × 2 = **3.25**. Bits: `0 1000 101`. Error: 3.5%.
4. **FP4 E2M1** (1, 2, 1; bias 1). Stored exponent = 1 + 1 = 2 = `10`. Mantissa: 0.5707963 × 2 = 1.14 → 1 = `1`.
   Value = (1 + 1/2) × 2 = **3**. Bits: `0 10 1`. Error: 4.5%.

</Worked>

The two 16-bit formats split their bits differently, and that’s a deliberate trade:

| Format | Bits (sign / exponent / mantissa) | Largest value | Smallest normal value | Relative precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP16 | 1 / 5 / 10 | 65,504 | about 0.00006 | about 3 decimal digits |
| BF16 | 1 / 8 / 7 | about 3.4 × 10<sup>38</sup> | about 1.2 × 10<sup>−38</sup> | about 2 decimal digits |
| FP8 E4M3 | 1 / 4 / 3 | 448 | 0.015625 | about 1 decimal digit |
| FP4 E2M1 | 1 / 2 / 1 | 6 | 1 | only 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6 |

BF16 keeps FP32’s 8-bit exponent (so it has FP32’s enormous range) and sacrifices mantissa precision. FP16 has more
precision but overflows above 65,504. **70,000 cannot be stored in FP16 at all, but BF16 stores it as 70,144** (only
0.2% off). That extra range is why BF16 became the standard training format: training produces occasional huge values,
and overflowing to infinity is catastrophic while being slightly imprecise usually isn’t.

### Why integers struggle: outliers

The exponent in floating-point numbers gives a **higher dynamic range**, meaning they can express very large and very
small numbers at the same time. This matters because **outlier values are significant in inference**, and
floating-point formats represent outliers much better after quantization. Integers have only sign and value bits:
their 256 values (for INT8) are spaced **evenly**, so a single huge value forces huge spacing for everyone.

<Worked title="One outlier wipes out its neighbors in INT8">

The standard way to quantize to INT8 (called *absmax* scaling) is:

- scale = (largest absolute value) ÷ 127
- stored integer = round(value ÷ scale)
- recovered value = stored integer × scale

**Without an outlier.** Values: 0.12, −0.47, 0.83, 0.05, −1.20, 0.33. Largest absolute value = 1.20, so
scale = 1.20 ÷ 127 = 0.00945.

| Original | ÷ scale | Stored | Recovered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.12 | 12.7 | 13 | 0.123 |
| −0.47 | −49.7 | −50 | −0.472 |
| 0.83 | 87.8 | 88 | 0.832 |
| 0.05 | 5.3 | 5 | 0.047 |

Every value comes back within about 0.005. Great.

**With one outlier.** Now suppose one value in the same tensor is 40.0. Scale = 40 ÷ 127 = 0.315.

| Original | ÷ scale | Stored | Recovered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.12 | 0.38 | 0 | **0** |
| −0.47 | −1.49 | −1 | −0.315 |
| 0.83 | 2.63 | 3 | 0.945 |
| 0.05 | 0.16 | 0 | **0** |

Two of the four values vanish entirely and the others are off by 14–33%. One outlier destroyed the precision of every
other value that shares its scale factor.

</Worked>

A floating-point format suffers far less here: because its values are spaced *relative* to their size, small numbers
keep fine spacing even when a large number sits in the same tensor. That’s the book’s core argument for floats.

## Granularity: how many numbers share a scale factor

Within floating-point formats there are several options at each precision, like **FP4, MXFP4, and NVFP4**. They differ
in **granularity**: the number of values that are quantized by a single scale factor. Quantization can be applied at
three levels:

- **Tensor level**: calculate a single scale factor for the entire tensor (for example, the whole QKV tensor).
- **Channel level**: calculate a different scale factor for each feature vector within the tensor.
- **Block level**: within each feature vector, divide it into blocks of *N* values and calculate a scale factor for
  each block.

**More granular quantization has a lower chance of smoothing over outliers, preserving quality.** The outlier example
above shows why: if the value 40.0 had its own scale factor (or shared one only with a few neighbors), the small values
elsewhere would keep their own, fine-grained scale. **But more granularity introduces more overhead** for storing and
applying the scale factors.

<BlockScaling />

**MXFP8 and MXFP4**, the new formats supported by Blackwell, are
<Term def="Formats that give every small block of values (32 in the MX formats) its own scale factor.">microscaling</Term>
formats: they compute a **blockwise scale factor for every 32 parameters**, which reduces the impact of these formats’
lower dynamic range. **NVFP4**, NVIDIA’s 4-bit format, goes further: a **block size of 16**, plus a **secondary 32-bit
global scale factor**, to further combat the quality loss that 4-bit formats introduce.

The trade-off to a microscaling format:

- The small-block scale factors must **also be stored in memory**, slightly reducing the gains from quantization.
- Both the tensor and block scale factors need to be **applied**, which adds a bit of compute.
- Blackwell GPUs offset that compute overhead by **applying scale factors inside the Tensor Cores**.

<Worked title="What the scale factors cost in memory">

Every block carries its own scale factor, so the *effective* bits per value is a little above the nominal size.

1. **MXFP4**: 32 values × 4 bits = 128 bits, plus one 8-bit scale per block = 136 bits per 32 values = **4.25 bits per
   value**.
2. **NVFP4**: 16 values × 4 bits = 64 bits, plus one 8-bit scale per block = 72 bits per 16 values = **4.5 bits per
   value** (the single 32-bit global scale per tensor is negligible).
3. **MXFP8**: 32 values × 8 bits = 256 bits, plus one 8-bit scale = **8.25 bits per value**.

For a 70B model: NVFP4 takes about 70 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 4.5 ÷ 8 ≈ **39 GB**, versus 35 GB if 4 bits were truly free. That
roughly 12% extra is the price of NVFP4’s finer granularity.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The two scale formats differ too. The MX formats store each block’s scale as a pure power of two (an 8-bit exponent
  with no mantissa), which makes applying it cheap but coarse. NVFP4 stores each block scale as an FP8 E4M3 number, so
  the scale itself can be more precise, and adds the FP32 per-tensor scale on top so those small FP8 scales can cover
  the tensor’s full range.
</Note>

## A detour: quantization on your own computer

The book focuses on datacenter inference, but quantization is **essential for local and edge inference**, especially
for large models. **GGUF**, a binary format for storing models, is the most popular choice for distributing highly
quantized models on Hugging Face. Individual researchers and companies use it to squeeze huge models like **DeepSeek**
onto consumer hardware like Apple computers.

These schemes fight quality loss through **dynamic quantization**: some layers or components stay in their original
precision while others are quantized to **integers with as little as one bit** of precision. Dynamic formats are named
by their *average* precision, which is why you see names like fine-tuning lab **Unsloth’s 1.58-bit** quantization.

<Note kind="warn">
  These are impressive feats of engineering and great for local inference, but the book’s advice for production is
  firm: **stick with floating-point formats.** Integer formats are not suitable for quality-sensitive workloads because
  they lack dynamic range.
</Note>

**8-bit floating point (FP8, MXFP8) is generally the sweet spot** for improving performance without sacrificing
quality. FP4 is promising, especially NVFP4 with its higher granularity for better accuracy, but **FP8 and MXFP8
provide the most flexibility, especially when quantizing the KV cache.**

## How quantization gets done

**The more parameters a model has, the less sensitive it is to quantization**, because each individual parameter
matters less. But even for very large models, it’s essential to quantize carefully. Quantization can happen during or
after training:

- <Term def="Training the model and its scale factors together so the final weights are accurate at a low precision.">Quantization-aware training</Term>:
  weights are trained and scale factors computed **together**, so the final converged weights are accurate at the
  target precision.
- **Post-training quantization**: finished weights are converted to a new precision by **computing scale factors and
  preserving accuracy via calibration**.

Some labs release models made with quantization-aware training, like **GPT-OSS in MXFP4** and **Kimi K2 Thinking in
INT4**. But inference engineers working with open models only have **finished weights**, so **post-training
quantization is the only option** for them.

A leading tool is **NVIDIA TensorRT Model Optimizer (ModelOpt)**, an open-source library that also supports pruning,
distillation, and sparsity. Its outputs are compatible with all the major inference engines: **vLLM, SGLang, and
TensorRT-LLM**.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **What calibration actually does.** Weights are known ahead of time, so their scale factors can be computed directly
  from the weights themselves. *Activations* are different: they depend on the input, so you don’t know their range
  until the model runs. Calibration runs a few hundred representative samples through the model and records the range
  each activation tensor actually reaches. Those observed ranges set **static** scale factors that are then fixed for
  inference. The alternative, **dynamic** activation scaling, measures the range on the fly for every input: more
  accurate, but it costs extra computation on every step. Calibration data should look like your real traffic. If you
  calibrate on English prose and serve code, the recorded ranges can be wrong.
</Note>

After picking a precision, you have **two decisions** to make before quantizing:

1. **What parts of the model** should be quantized: weights, activations, KV cache, attention?
2. **What number format** offers the appropriate dynamic range and granularity?

These decisions turn quantization from a **binary choice into a spectrum of trade-offs** between performance and quality.

## What to quantize: from safest to riskiest

Components of a model differ in how sensitive they are to quantization, and quantizing more sensitive components
carries a higher risk of quality loss. From **least** to **most** sensitive:

1. **Weights**, specifically the **linear layers**, are the least sensitive.
2. **Activations**, the intermediate outputs of activation functions, are only **somewhat** sensitive. (The activation
   *functions* themselves are rarely quantized; they’re a tiny fraction of the model.)
3. **KV cache**, the cached results of the attention calculation, is **moderately** sensitive.
4. **Attention** layers are **highly** sensitive, especially operations like **softmax**.

The book’s Figure 5.2 summarizes this as a risk scale: **low** for weights and activations, **moderate** for the KV
cache, **high** for attention.

You can be more selective *within* each component too. Even in linear layers and activations (the least sensitive
parts, thanks to their size), the **early and late layers**, such as the input and output layers of the network, are
often left in their original precision because they’re more sensitive.

### Why the KV cache is tempting but risky

Quantizing weights and activations helps performance directly. Quantizing the **KV cache** gives an *additional* boost
to techniques like **prefix caching and disaggregation**. The KV cache is a valuable resource: quantizing it lets the
engine **store more of it in memory** and **read it faster**.

But each token’s KV cache is **used by every subsequent token**. So precision errors introduced by quantizing it can
**compound from token to token**.

### Why attention is the riskiest

Compounding errors are exactly why **attention layers are the riskiest to quantize**. Attention is very sensitive to
dynamic range, *and* each attention calculation relies on the results of every previous one. Over a sequence of
thousands of tokens, the errors accumulate quickly. **All but the most aggressive quantization schemes run functions
like softmax in their original precision.**

<Note kind="beyond">
  Softmax is especially fragile because it exponentiates: e<sup>x</sup> turns small differences in its inputs into
  large differences in its outputs. If two attention scores are 10.0 and 10.5, softmax gives them weights in a ratio of
  e<sup>0.5</sup> ≈ 1.65. Round both to 10 (a small error in the input) and the ratio becomes 1.0, so the model now pays
  equal attention to two tokens it was meant to weigh quite differently.
</Note>

**The moderate, recommended approach:** use a format with **high dynamic range like FP8**, ideally a **microscaling
format like MXFP8**, to carefully quantize **select linear layers, activations, and often KV cache values**. Even with
these high-dynamic-range formats, **components of the attention layer are rarely quantized**.

## Proving it didn’t get dumber

**The standard for production-ready quantization is zero perceptible quality loss.** After quantizing a model, you
must test its output quality thoroughly against the original precision. There are three methods:

1. **Perplexity**: calculate the quantized model’s perplexity score and compare it with the original.
2. **Intelligence benchmarks**: run a standard benchmark like **MMLU** or **SWE-bench** and compare scores.
3. **Custom evals**: run a product-specific evaluation suite on the quantized model and compare with the original.

In every case you’re looking for a **difference that’s indistinguishable from noise**. LLMs are non-deterministic, so
scores vary slightly from run to run anyway.

### Perplexity

<Term def="A score for how ‘surprised’ a model is by a known piece of text. Lower is better.">Perplexity</Term> is the
simplest check. Instead of asking the model to generate anything, you give it **known text** and measure how likely it
thought each actual next token was. Higher perplexity means the model is more “surprised” by the text, which is not
what you want from a model whose whole job is predicting tokens. After quantization, you’re looking for a **minimal
increase** in perplexity.

<Worked title="Computing perplexity by hand">

Perplexity is e raised to the average negative log-probability of the real tokens:

```
perplexity = exp( −(1/N) × Σ log p(tokenᵢ | everything before it) )
```

Say the original model gives the four real next tokens of a sentence probabilities **0.50, 0.25, 0.80, 0.10**.

1. Natural logs: −0.693, −1.386, −0.223, −2.303. Sum = −4.605.
2. Average = −4.605 ÷ 4 = −1.151.
3. Perplexity = e<sup>1.151</sup> ≈ **3.16**.

Intuitively, the model is about as uncertain as if it were choosing among 3.16 equally likely options at each step.

Now the quantized model gives the same tokens **0.48, 0.24, 0.78, 0.09**:

1. Logs: −0.734, −1.427, −0.248, −2.408. Sum = −4.817.
2. Average = −1.204.
3. Perplexity = e<sup>1.204</sup> ≈ **3.33**, an increase of about 5%.

Every probability dropped only slightly, yet perplexity rose noticeably. That sensitivity is what makes it a useful early
warning. Real measurements average over many thousands of tokens, not four.

</Worked>

### Benchmarks and custom evals

A more comprehensive check uses a **public intelligence benchmark**, or better yet a **domain-specific eval** that
matches your real-world usage. Here you’re looking for a **minimal reduction in the quality score**. To get the full
picture, **run all three types of checks** and make **apples-to-apples comparisons** with the original weights: same
prompts, same settings, same scoring.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **How big is “noise”?** For an eval scored as right or wrong, the random wobble in the score is roughly
  √(p × (1 − p) ÷ N), where p is the accuracy and N the number of questions. For a 500-question eval at 80% accuracy,
  that’s √(0.8 × 0.2 ÷ 500) ≈ 1.8 percentage points, so two runs can easily differ by 3 or more points by chance alone.
  For MMLU’s roughly 14,000 questions at 70%, it’s about 0.4 points. Small evals can’t detect small regressions: if you
  need to be sure quantization cost less than one point, you need a large eval or several repeated runs.
</Note>

<Note kind="tip">
  Remember that quantization is **a scale, not a binary decision**. You can still get some performance gains with a
  lower risk of quality loss by quantizing to **FP8 instead of FP4**, or by quantizing fewer components, for example
  <Term def="Quantizing only the model’s linear-layer weights, leaving activations, KV cache, and attention at original precision.">weights-only quantization</Term>.
  And if you work in a highly sensitive domain and can’t risk *any* quality change: **every other technique in this
  chapter is lossless.**
</Note>

<KeyPoints>
- Chapter 5 principles: research reaches production in weeks; **constraints buy performance**; **more traffic unlocks more optimizations**; tuning never ends; techniques can help or hinder each other.
- Quantization = fewer bits per number. Prefill gets faster Tensor Core math; decode moves fewer bytes. Expect **30–50%** per precision step, not 2×.
- Precision errors **compound**: each multiplication adds roughly the same relative error again.
- Floats are sign × 2<sup>exponent − bias</sup> × (1 + mantissa). The exponent gives **dynamic range**, which is why **floats beat integers**: in INT8 one outlier can zero out its neighbors.
- **Granularity** (tensor → channel → block) protects outliers at the cost of storing and applying scales. MX formats: blocks of 32; NVFP4: blocks of 16 plus a 32-bit global scale (≈ 4.5 bits per value).
- Only post-training quantization is available on finished open weights; ModelOpt works with vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM. Calibration sets activation scales.
- Risk ladder: **weights → activations → KV cache → attention**. Keep first/last layers and softmax at full precision. **FP8/MXFP8** is the sweet spot.
- Verify with **perplexity, benchmarks, and custom evals**, looking for differences indistinguishable from noise.
</KeyPoints>
