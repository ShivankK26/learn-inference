<TLDR>
  A GPU is a machine for doing the **same simple math on huge amounts of data at once**. Inside, it has thousands of
  small math units (<Compute>compute</Compute>) and a ladder of memory, from tiny-and-fast caches to big-and-slow VRAM
  (<Memory>memory</Memory>). Two numbers on the spec sheet matter most: **dense Tensor Core FLOPS at your precision**
  (prefill, images, video) and **memory bandwidth** (decode). A third, **VRAM size**, decides whether the model fits at
  all.
</TLDR>

## Accelerators, and where they live

Inference runs on **accelerators**: hardware designed to load terabytes of data and perform trillions of operations
per second. The most common accelerator for inference is the **GPU**, and the market leader is **NVIDIA**. Like the
book, this course focuses on NVIDIA GPUs **in the datacenter**. Other vendors come in the
<a href="/learn/beyond-nvidia">last lesson of this chapter</a>, along with running models on your own devices.

Across vendors, GPUs come in three types:

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Datacenter** | Racked servers with interconnected, high-performance GPUs | NVIDIA B200 |
| **Workstation** | Individual desktop GPUs for professional workflows | NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 |
| **Personal computing** | Individual desktop GPUs for everyday use | NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 |

Inference at scale uses **datacenter GPUs mounted on racks**: refrigerator-sized chassis with standardized power,
networking, and cooling. A datacenter GPU like the B200 has the highest individual performance, but the book stresses
that three other properties matter *more*:

1. **High-bandwidth GPU-to-GPU interconnects**, so several GPUs can act as one big one (covered in the
   <a href="/learn/instances">instances lesson</a>).
2. **Highly standardized configurations**, so software and operations work the same way everywhere.
3. **Availability by the millions** in datacenters worldwide, so you can actually get capacity.

You almost certainly don't have a B200 running under your desk. (The author asks for a photo if you do.) So datacenter
inference runs in one of three **modes**:

- **Cloud**: GPUs rented in someone else's datacenter, usually a
  <Term def="The giant general-purpose cloud providers, like AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure.">hyperscaler</Term>
  like AWS or GCP, or a
  <Term def="A newer cloud company that specializes in renting out GPUs, like CoreWeave or Nebius.">neocloud</Term>
  like CoreWeave or Nebius.
- **On-premise**: GPUs purchased and installed in a datacenter you control directly.
- **Air-gapped**: on-premise GPUs with no outside network connection, so you need **physical access** to run
  inference.

Most inference engineers use **cloud GPUs**. Large enterprises and governments run on-premise and air-gapped
deployments, but the cloud offers the **flexibility and access** that fast-growing AI products need to scale. Even
within the cloud, the landscape is complex: providers vary in what they offer, and NVIDIA's own naming conventions have
plenty of nuances. The rest of this chapter untangles both.

## GPUs are throughput machines

A **CPU** is great at complex, sequential execution: lots of branching logic, one step depending on the last. A **GPU**
is designed for the opposite: **simple, massively parallel workloads**. Specifically, GPUs excel at performing **one
uniform operation on thousands of independent pieces of data**. Since model inference is a long series of vector and
matrix multiplications, where each output number can be computed independently of its neighbors, GPUs are a natural
fit.

<Analogy>
  A CPU is a handful of **master chefs** who can each cook anything, one complex dish at a time. A GPU is a
  **stadium full of line cooks** who each only know a few simple moves, but all do them at the same moment. If the job
  is "chop ten million onions," the stadium wins easily. If the job is "invent a new recipe," the master chef wins.
</Analogy>

The principle is simple, but GPUs themselves are extraordinarily complex: hardware engineering spans everything from
the physics of powering and cooling the chips to the impossibly tight tolerances of manufacturing each component.
Inference engineers work at a comfortable level of abstraction above all that, but the book is clear that a **strong
mental model of what's inside the box** is essential for building high-performance systems. That mental model has two
halves: compute and memory.

## Compute: SMs, three kinds of cores, and threads

### Cores mean something different on a GPU

On a CPU, a "core" is a big, independent processor, like the 8 cores in a high-end Intel i9 gaming PC. On a GPU the
top-level building block is a **Streaming Multiprocessor (SM)**, and **each SM contains multiple cores** of three
types:

| Unit | Works on | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **CUDA Core** | Individual numbers (scalars) | General arithmetic and element-wise operations |
| **Tensor Core** | Vectors and matrices | Matrix multiplication: the bulk of inference |
| **Special Function Unit (SFU)** | Individual numbers | Fast sin, cos, log, exponentials |

**Measure GPU compute for inference in Tensor Core terms.** The SFUs are essential (softmax, the step in attention that
turns scores into probabilities, needs exponentials), but the Tensor Cores execute the instruction that dominates
inference: **Matrix Multiply and Accumulate (MMA)**.

### Matrix Multiply and Accumulate

MMA multiplies matrix A by matrix B, **adds matrix C**, and stores the result as matrix D. The book's Figure 3.1 draws
exactly this:

```
D = A × B + C
```

The "accumulate" part (adding C) looks like a small detail, but it's what makes big matrix multiplications possible
on small hardware units. A Tensor Core works on small tiles of a matrix at a time. To multiply two huge matrices, the
GPU multiplies one pair of tiles, **adds the result to a running total**, moves on to the next pair of tiles, adds
again, and so on. Each step is one MMA, where C is "the total so far" and D is "the new total."

<Note kind="beyond">
  Tensor Cores process fixed-size tiles per instruction (on the order of 16×16 values, depending on the generation and
  number format). A matmul of two 8,192×8,192 matrices is therefore broken into many thousands of tile-sized MMAs
  running across all the SMs at once. Writing kernels that keep every Tensor Core fed with tiles is much of what
  Chapter 4’s kernel libraries (cuBLAS, CUTLASS, FlashAttention) do.
</Note>

### Threads: the same idea, a very different scale

Unlike "core," the word **thread** means roughly the same thing on CPUs and GPUs: one stream of instructions. The
difference is scale. A CPU has **dozens to hundreds** of threads. A GPU has **tens to hundreds of thousands** that can
work concurrently, **switch tasks in a single clock cycle**, and execute simple instructions in parallel. That
instant switching is important: when one group of threads is stuck waiting for data from memory, the SM immediately
runs another group instead of sitting idle.

<Note kind="beyond">
  GPU threads are scheduled in groups of 32 called **warps**, which execute the same instruction in lockstep on
  different data (NVIDIA calls this SIMT: single instruction, multiple threads). An H100 SM can keep up to 2,048
  threads resident at once, so across 132 SMs that’s roughly 270,000 threads in flight: squarely in the book’s “tens
  to hundreds of thousands.”
</Note>

## Reading a spec sheet: FLOPS

Compute is measured in **FLOPS**: floating-point operations per second. Datacenter GPUs do trillions (**teraFLOPS**,
10¹²) or quadrillions (**petaFLOPS**, 10¹⁵). Spec sheets list many FLOPS numbers, and two rules from the book tell you
which one to believe.

**Rule 1: dense, not sparse.** Spec sheets show two measurements for Tensor Core compute:

- **Dense**: the raw operations per second when every element of the matrix is used.
- **Sparse**: for matrices with <Term def="A pattern where, in every group of 4 values, exactly 2 are zero. Hardware can skip the zeros and only multiply the other 2.">2:4 structured sparsity</Term>,
  where 50% of the values are zero, the Tensor Cores can skip the multiplications by zero.

The sparse number is **often, but not always, double** the dense number at the same precision. But **inference is
dense by default**: real model weights don't come in that neat half-zero pattern. So always read the number **without
sparsity**.

**Rule 2: compare at identical precision.** FLOPS **generally double each time precision halves**. A GPU that does
1 petaFLOPS on 16-bit numbers will do about 2 petaFLOPS on 8-bit numbers. That's great news for low-precision
inference (Chapter 5's <a href="/learn/quantization">quantization lesson</a>), but it means a comparison is only fair
if both GPUs are measured at the **same precision**, ideally the one your model will actually run in.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Why does halving precision double the FLOPS? A Tensor Core moves a fixed number of bits through its multipliers each
  clock cycle. If each number is half as wide, twice as many numbers fit through in the same cycle, so twice as many
  operations complete per second.
</Note>

Stack both rules and the gap between a headline number and reality gets large:

<Worked title="The H100 headline number vs. what an FP16 model gets">

The book's table gives the H100 **1,979 teraFLOPS of dense FP8** compute. Apply the two rules:

1. **FP8 with sparsity** (a common headline): 1,979 × 2 ≈ **3,958 teraFLOPS**.
2. **FP8 dense**: **1,979 teraFLOPS**. The honest number if your model runs in FP8.
3. **FP16 dense**: 1,979 ÷ 2 ≈ **989 teraFLOPS**. The honest number if your model runs in 16-bit. This is the figure
   the <a href="/learn/bottlenecks">bottlenecks lesson</a> used.

So for a 16-bit model, the real compute is **one quarter** of the biggest number you might see quoted.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  NVIDIA’s own product pages typically headline the with-sparsity figures (for the H100 SXM, “3,958 teraFLOPS” of FP8),
  with the dense numbers in footnotes. That’s exactly why the book’s two rules matter.
</Note>

## Memory: VRAM and the caches

### VRAM

GPUs contain their own high-speed onboard memory called **VRAM**. Just as the "G" in GPU stands for *graphics*, the
"V" in VRAM stands for *video*: a callback to what these chips were originally built for. Today, VRAM is **HBM**
(high-bandwidth memory) in one of three generations: **HBM3, HBM3e, or HBM4**. Datacenter GPUs have **dozens to
hundreds of gigabytes** of it.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Physically, HBM is several DRAM chips stacked on top of each other and placed right next to the GPU die inside the
  same package, connected by thousands of tiny wires. That very wide, very short connection is where the terabytes per
  second come from; ordinary memory on a separate circuit board can’t get close.
</Note>

### Two kinds of memory: DRAM and SRAM

Every chip, CPU or GPU, has two kinds of memory:

| Kind | Where it sits | Typical size | Speed and cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DRAM** (dynamic RAM) | Off the processor die | Gigabytes | General-purpose; slower |
| **SRAM** (static RAM) | On the processor die | Kilobytes to megabytes | Much faster; much more expensive per byte |

VRAM is a type of **DRAM**. The GPU's **SRAM** comes as its caches, in three levels:

- **L0**: an instruction cache for a single Tensor Core.
- **L1**: shared memory for each Streaming Multiprocessor. An H100 has **256 KB of L1 per SM**.
- **L2**: a global cache shared across all the SMs. An H100 has **50 MB of L2** in total.

The book's Figure 3.2 shows the layout: a GPU holds many SMs, each SM holds multiple Tensor Cores and its own L1, and
one L2 cache is shared across them all. Above it sits the VRAM.

<GpuHierarchy />

Put the numbers side by side and you can see why memory, not math, is so often the problem. An H100's entire on-chip
L2 is **50 MB**. An 8B-parameter model in 16-bit is **16 GB**, about **320 times** larger. The weights cannot live in
the fast caches; they have to stream in from VRAM over and over, and every decode step reads all of them. That's the
<a href="/learn/bottlenecks">memory-bound decode</a> problem, seen from the hardware side.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Below L1, each SM also has a register file (on the H100, 256 KB per SM), the fastest storage of all, holding the
  values threads are working on right now. High-performance kernels are largely about choreographing data up this
  ladder: VRAM → L2 → L1/shared memory → registers → Tensor Cores, reusing each piece as many times as possible before
  it’s evicted.
</Note>

### VRAM bandwidth

**VRAM bandwidth** is the peak transfer rate between the GPU cores and VRAM, over the memory bus. In practice it
decides **how quickly VRAM can feed data into the cache hierarchy**, and so how quickly the Tensor Cores can be kept
busy with fresh data. High-end GPUs have **terabytes per second**: 3.35 TB/s on an H100 and 4.8 TB/s on an H200.

### Compute vs. bandwidth on real GPUs

Dividing a GPU's compute by its bandwidth gives its **ops:byte ratio**, which you met in the
<a href="/learn/bottlenecks">bottlenecks lesson</a>. Running it across the book's spec tables shows how differently
GPUs are balanced:

<Worked title="Ops:byte ratios from the book’s spec tables (FP8, dense)">

| GPU | FP8 compute | Bandwidth | Ops per byte |
|---|---|---|---|
| L4 | 242 TFLOPS | 0.3 TB/s | ≈ 807 |
| L40 | 362 TFLOPS | 0.864 TB/s | ≈ 419 |
| H100 | 1,979 TFLOPS | 3.35 TB/s | ≈ 591 |
| H200 | 1,979 TFLOPS | 4.8 TB/s | ≈ 412 |
| B200 | ~5,000 TFLOPS | 8 TB/s | ≈ 625 |

How to read it:

1. The **H200** has exactly the H100's compute but ~43% more bandwidth, so its ratio is lower. Work needs *less*
   arithmetic intensity before it stops being memory-bound. That's what "better for decode" means in numbers.
2. The **L4** has a very high ratio: plenty of math relative to a thin memory pipe. On an L4, almost every LLM decode
   workload is deeply memory-bound.
3. At FP16 each ratio halves (the FLOPS halve, the bandwidth doesn't), e.g. ≈ 295 for the H100.

</Worked>

## Picking a GPU: will it fit, and how fast will it go?

### VRAM size: does it fit?

**The total VRAM limits the size of model you can load.** The book's rule of thumb: VRAM should hold **the model
weights plus at least 50% headroom**, mainly for the KV cache. You need **more** than 50% for long contexts, high
batch sizes, or video generation models. Two failure modes:

- **Not enough VRAM for the weights**: loading fails immediately with an **OOM** (out of memory) error.
- **Weights fit, but not enough headroom**: inference is **slow**, or it **crashes with an OOM** later, when the KV
  cache grows under real traffic.

Weight size is simple arithmetic: **parameters × bytes per parameter**. 16-bit numbers take 2 bytes, 8-bit take 1, and
4-bit take half a byte.

<Worked title="A 70B model on different GPUs">

**In FP16:** weights = 70B × 2 bytes = **140 GB**. With 50% headroom you want **210 GB**.

1. One **H100** (80 GB): the weights alone don't fit → **OOM on load**.
2. Two **H100s** (160 GB): the weights load, but only 20 GB is left, far below the 70 GB of headroom → slow or
   crash-prone.
3. Four **H100s** (320 GB) or one **B300** (288 GB): fits with room to spare.

**In FP8:** weights = 70 GB, and you want **105 GB**.

1. One **H100** (80 GB): loads, with only 10 GB of headroom → too tight.
2. One **H200** (141 GB): fits, with 71 GB of headroom.

Halving precision halves the weights, which is one reason FP8 matters so much in practice.

</Worked>

<FitCalculator />

Where does the headroom go? The book frames it as KV cache, and that's the big one. But it's worth seeing how fast KV
cache grows, because it explains why the book calls 50% a *minimum*.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Estimating KV cache size.** Every token in every active conversation stores a key and a value vector in every
  layer. So per token:

  KV bytes per token = 2 (K and V) × layers × KV heads × head size × bytes per number

  For Llama 3 8B (32 layers, 8 KV heads, head size 128, 16-bit): 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = **131,072 bytes ≈ 128 KB per
  token**. One 8,000-token conversation needs about **1 GB** of KV cache. For Llama 3 70B (80 layers, same other
  numbers) it’s about **320 KB per token**, or about 2.5 GB per 8,000-token conversation.

  So on an H100 running Llama 3 8B, the 50% rule reserves 8 GB of headroom, which is enough for only about **eight**
  8K-token conversations at once. In practice engines give the KV cache nearly all free VRAM. vLLM, for example,
  claims 90% of GPU memory by default, then uses what’s left after weights and working space for KV cache. A smaller
  slice also goes to **activations** (temporary results inside each forward pass) and the software’s own overhead.
</Note>

### Memory bandwidth: how fast will it decode?

**Memory bandwidth is the bottleneck for LLM decode at low to medium batch sizes.** If you're choosing a GPU to
generate **more tokens per second**, pick the one with **higher memory bandwidth**. The book's example is an **H200
instead of an H100**.

<Worked title="Why the H200 decodes faster than the H100">

The <a href="/learn/bottlenecks">decode speed limit</a> for a single user is roughly bandwidth ÷ bytes of weights read
per token. For a 70B model in FP8 (70 GB):

1. **H100**: 3,350 GB/s ÷ 70 GB ≈ **48 tokens per second**, at most.
2. **H200**: 4,800 GB/s ÷ 70 GB ≈ **69 tokens per second**, at most.

Same compute, same model: about **43% faster decode**, purely from bandwidth. (And from the previous example, the H200
is also the one where this model fits with proper headroom.)

</Worked>

<KeyPoints>
- Datacenter GPUs win on **interconnect, standardization, and availability**, not just raw speed. Most teams rent them
  in the **cloud**; on-premise and air-gapped setups are for enterprises and governments.
- A GPU = many **Streaming Multiprocessors**, each with **CUDA Cores** (scalars), **Tensor Cores** (matrices, via
  **MMA**: D = A × B + C), and **SFUs** (sin/cos/log, used by softmax). Measure inference compute on **Tensor Cores**.
- GPUs run **tens to hundreds of thousands of threads** and switch between them in a single cycle.
- Read spec sheets with two rules: **dense, not sparse** (sparse is often 2× and irrelevant to inference) and **same
  precision** (FLOPS double as precision halves).
- Memory ladder: **L0** (per Tensor Core) → **L1** (256 KB per SM on H100) → **L2** (50 MB shared) → **VRAM** (HBM3,
  HBM3e, HBM4: tens to hundreds of GB). VRAM is DRAM; the caches are SRAM.
- **Fit rule**: VRAM ≥ weights + **at least 50% headroom** (more for long context, big batches, video). Too little →
  **OOM** or slowness.
- **More FLOPS** → faster prefill and image/video. **More bandwidth** → faster decode (e.g. H200 over H100).
</KeyPoints>
