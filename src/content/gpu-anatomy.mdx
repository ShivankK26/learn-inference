<TLDR>
  A GPU is a machine for doing the **same simple math on huge amounts of data at once**. Inside, it has lots of small
  math units (<Compute>compute</Compute>) and a ladder of memory, from tiny-and-fast caches to big-and-slow VRAM
  (<Memory>memory</Memory>). Knowing which part is the bottleneck tells you which GPU to pick.
</TLDR>

## Accelerators, and where they live

Inference runs on **accelerators**: hardware built to move terabytes of data and do trillions of operations per
second. The most common one is the **GPU**, and the market leader is **NVIDIA**. This course (like the book) focuses on
NVIDIA's datacenter GPUs. Other chips and running models on your own devices come later in this chapter.

GPUs come in three flavors:

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Datacenter** | Racked servers packed with connected, high-performance GPUs | NVIDIA B200 |
| **Workstation** | A single powerful desktop GPU for professional work | NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 |
| **Personal** | A desktop GPU for everyday use and gaming | NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 |

Inference at scale uses **datacenter GPUs** mounted in racks: fridge-sized cabinets with standard power, networking, and
cooling. They're the fastest individually, but what matters more is that they come with **very fast GPU-to-GPU
connections**, are installed in **standardized setups**, and exist **by the millions** worldwide.

You almost certainly don't have a B200 under your desk. So datacenter inference runs in one of three ways:

- **Cloud**: you rent GPUs in someone else's datacenter. That's either a
  <Term def="The giant cloud providers: AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure.">hyperscaler</Term> like AWS or GCP, or a
  <Term def="A newer cloud company focused specifically on renting out GPUs, like CoreWeave or Nebius.">neocloud</Term>
  like CoreWeave or Nebius.
- **On-premise**: you buy the GPUs and install them in a datacenter you control.
- **Air-gapped**: on-premise GPUs that are cut off from outside networks, so you have to be physically there to use them.

Most inference engineers use **cloud GPUs**, because fast-growing products need the flexibility. Big enterprises and
governments are more likely to run on-premise or air-gapped.

## GPUs are throughput machines

A **CPU** is great at complex, step-by-step work. A **GPU** is built for the opposite: **one simple operation applied to
thousands of independent pieces of data at the same time**. Since inference is mostly vector and matrix
multiplication, GPUs are a natural fit.

<Analogy>
  A CPU is a handful of **master chefs** who can each cook anything, one complex dish at a time. A GPU is a
  **stadium full of line cooks** who each only know how to chop, but they all chop at once. If your job is "chop ten
  million onions," the stadium wins easily.
</Analogy>

GPUs are incredibly complex under the hood (the physics of power and cooling, absurdly tight manufacturing tolerances),
but inference engineers work a comfortable level above that. You just need a solid mental picture of what's inside.

## Compute: SMs, cores, and threads

On a CPU, a "core" is a big, independent processor (like the 8 cores in a high-end gaming PC). On a GPU the word means
something smaller. A GPU is made of many **Streaming Multiprocessors (SMs)**, and each SM contains several kinds of
cores:

- **CUDA cores**: do math on individual numbers.
- **Tensor Cores**: do math on whole vectors and matrices at once. **These are what matter for inference.**
- **Special Function Units (SFUs)**: speed up specific math like sin, cos, and log. They're needed for softmax (part of
  attention), but they're not where most of the work happens.

So when you measure a GPU's compute for inference, **measure its Tensor Core compute**. Tensor Cores run a core
instruction called **Matrix Multiply and Accumulate (MMA)**: multiply matrix A by matrix B, add matrix C, and store the
result as matrix D.

```
D = A × B + C
```

The word **thread** means roughly the same thing on CPUs and GPUs: one stream of instructions. The difference is scale.
A CPU runs dozens to hundreds of threads. A GPU runs **tens to hundreds of thousands** at once, can switch between tasks
in a single clock cycle, and executes simple instructions in parallel.

### Reading a spec sheet: FLOPS

Compute is measured in **FLOPS** (floating-point operations per second). Datacenter GPUs do trillions
(**teraFLOPS**) or quadrillions (**petaFLOPS**). Spec sheets can be misleading, so watch for two traps:

1. **Dense vs. sparse.** The *dense* number is raw speed with every value used. The *sparse* number assumes a special
   pattern (<Term def="A pattern where 2 out of every 4 values in a matrix are zero, so the hardware can skip them.">2:4 structured sparsity</Term>)
   where half the values are zero and can be skipped. Sparse numbers are often about double the dense ones. **Normal
   inference is dense**, so ignore the sparse number.
2. **Precision.** FLOPS roughly **double every time you halve the precision**. A GPU that does 1 petaFLOPS in 16-bit
   does about 2 petaFLOPS in 8-bit. Only compare GPUs **at the same precision**.

<Note kind="tip">
  Compute is the bottleneck for **LLM prefill** and for **image and video generation**. If that's your workload, pick
  the GPU with **more FLOPS**.
</Note>

## Memory: VRAM and the caches

A GPU has its own fast memory called **VRAM** (the "V" is for *video*, a leftover from when GPUs were mainly for
graphics, just like the "G"). Today's VRAM is **high-bandwidth memory**: generations named **HBM3, HBM3e, and HBM4**.
GPUs have dozens to hundreds of gigabytes of it.

Every chip has two kinds of memory:

| Kind | Where | Size | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DRAM** (dynamic RAM) | Off the main chip | Gigabytes | Slower |
| **SRAM** (static RAM) | On the chip itself | Kilobytes to megabytes | Much faster, more expensive |

VRAM is a type of DRAM. The GPU's SRAM comes as three levels of cache:

- **L0**: an instruction cache for a single Tensor Core.
- **L1**: shared memory for each Streaming Multiprocessor. An H100 has **256 KB per SM**.
- **L2**: one global cache shared by all SMs. An H100 has **50 MB** in total.

**VRAM bandwidth** is the top speed at which data moves between VRAM and the GPU's cores over the memory bus. In
practice, it decides how fast VRAM can feed the caches, and so the compute.

## Picking a GPU: will it fit, and how fast?

**VRAM size decides what model you can load.** The book's rule of thumb: VRAM should hold the **model weights plus at
least 50% extra** for the KV cache. You need more for long contexts, big batches, or video models.

- If the **weights don't fit**, loading fails with an **OOM** (out-of-memory) error.
- If the weights fit but there's **not enough headroom**, inference is slow or crashes with an OOM later.

<FitCalculator />

**Memory bandwidth decides decode speed** at low to medium batch sizes (remember the <a href="/learn/bottlenecks">bottleneck lesson</a>).
If you want more tokens per second, pick the GPU with more bandwidth, e.g. an **H200 instead of an H100**.

<KeyPoints>
- Inference runs mostly on **NVIDIA datacenter GPUs**, usually rented in the **cloud**.
- GPUs = **Streaming Multiprocessors** full of cores. **Tensor Cores** (matrix math, MMA) are what count for inference.
- Compare **dense** FLOPS at the **same precision**. More FLOPS → faster prefill and image/video.
- Memory ladder: tiny fast **SRAM caches** (L0, L1, L2) → big slower **VRAM** (HBM).
- VRAM must fit **weights + ≥50% headroom**. More **bandwidth** → faster decode.
</KeyPoints>
