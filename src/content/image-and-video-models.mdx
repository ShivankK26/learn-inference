<TLDR>
  Image generators don’t write pixels one at a time. They’re **pipelines of models**: a text encoder reads the prompt, a
  denoising model starts from **pure noise** in a compressed **latent space** and refines the *whole* image a little on
  each of ~30–50 steps (two forward passes per step), and a VAE turns the result into pixels. Video models do the same
  thing for a whole clip at once, with a time axis added, which makes them some of the most compute-hungry models in
  existence. And because they process everything at once, image and video inference is **compute-bound**.
</TLDR>

## The second style of generative model

The [neural networks lesson](/learn/neural-networks) introduced the two styles of transformer-based model. LLMs use
**autoregressive token generation**: start from a sequence and predict the next token, over and over. Image and video
models mostly use **iterative denoising**: start from random noise and refine it, over and over, toward the most likely
output. That process is called **diffusion**.

Image generation models slightly **predate the public rise of LLMs**: both closed models from **Midjourney** and open
**Stable Diffusion** models were first released in the summer of 2022, a few months before ChatGPT.

## Not one model, a pipeline

An LLM is basically one monolithic model. An image generation model isn’t: it’s a **pipeline of multiple models**
working together. Within a foundation model for image generation there are three essential components:

1. **Text encoder**: converts the text prompt into instructions (embeddings) that the image model can understand.
2. **Denoising model**: the heart of the model. It iterates from noise to an image, guided by the prompt.
3. **VAE** (<Term def="Variational autoencoder: converts between the compact ‘latent’ version of an image and actual pixels.">variational autoencoder</Term>):
   converts the model’s output from **latent space** to **pixel space**, the actual image.

<Note kind="beyond">
  The denoiser reads the prompt through **cross-attention** (from the [attention lesson](/learn/attention-and-kv-cache)):
  the image being generated supplies the queries, and the text encoder’s embeddings supply the keys and values. That’s
  how each patch of the image “looks at” the relevant words of the prompt. The VAE also has an encoder half, used to turn
  real images into latents during training (and for image-to-image editing).
</Note>

This **pipeline mentality** extends through the whole image-model ecosystem. Beyond the base model, image inference
often includes:

- **LoRAs**: lightweight fine-tunes that change the style or enhance quality.
- **ControlNets**: outlines and edges that steer the output image to match broad shapes and colors.

The open-source ecosystem around image models is vast. Tools like **ComfyUI** exist for building complex pipelines of
foundation models and adaptations, swapping components in and out to produce unique outputs.

For an inference engineer, the pipeline structure matters: a single “image request” is really several models run in
sequence, each with its own cost, and each of which can be swapped, cached or optimized on its own.

## Latent space: why nobody denoises pixels

The **entire pipeline operates in latent space**. An ordinary HD image of 1,024 × 1,024 pixels has well over a million
pixels, and the denoising model needs to **calculate attention over the entire image in parallel**. Doing that in pixel
space would be infeasible.

**Latent space** is a low-dimensional representation of the image. A latent for a 1,024 × 1,024 image might be
**128 × 128**: about one percent of the values of the pixel space it represents. Only at the very end does the VAE expand
it back into full-resolution pixels.

<Worked title="Why latent space makes attention affordable">

1. **Pixel space**: 1,024 × 1,024 = 1,048,576 positions.
2. **Latent space**: 128 × 128 = 16,384 positions, which is 16,384 ÷ 1,048,576 ≈ **1.6%** of the positions.
3. Attention compares every position with every other, so its cost grows with the **square** of the number of positions:
   - Pixel space: (1,048,576)² ≈ 1.1 × 10¹² pairs.
   - Latent space: (16,384)² ≈ 2.7 × 10⁸ pairs.
4. Ratio: (1,048,576 ÷ 16,384)² = 64² = **4,096× fewer** attention pairs.

Shrinking the image by 8× on each side makes attention about four thousand times cheaper. That’s the difference between
impossible and routine.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  In most modern pipelines, the VAE shrinks each side of the image by 8× (1,024 → 128), and the diffusion transformer
  then groups the latent into small patches (commonly 2 × 2), each becoming one token. A 1,024 × 1,024 image ends up
  as about 64 × 64 = **4,096 tokens** of attention: the same size as the sequence in the book’s attention example.
</Note>

<Analogy>
  Like an artist working on a **small, detailed thumbnail** instead of a giant canvas. Every real decision (composition,
  color, what goes where) happens on the thumbnail. At the very end, an expert enlarger (the VAE) blows it up to full
  size and fills in the fine texture.
</Analogy>

## From noise to picture: the denoising loop

The latent space is **initialized with random values**: pure noise. The denoising model then refines that noise into an
image over a series of **steps**, based on the text prompt. The book’s Figure 2.10 shows this as a strip of frames: a
field of static, then blurry blobs of color, then recognizable shapes, then a finished image, over **30 to 50 steps**.

Two contrasts with LLMs are worth holding onto:

- **Each step updates the entire latent space.** An LLM adds one token per forward pass; a denoising step rewrites
  every part of the image at once.
- **The number of steps is fixed up front.** An LLM runs until it emits a stop token. An image model runs its configured
  number of steps, then stops.

<Denoise />

### Two forward passes per step

Within each step, the model actually runs **two forward passes**: one **with conditioning** (the text prompt) and one
**without conditioning**. The two results are then combined, and how they’re combined is set by the **guidance scale**.
Because of this two-part step, **a 50-step image generation actually takes 100 forward passes** of the denoiser.

<Worked title="Counting forward passes">

1. 50 steps × 2 passes per step = **100** denoiser forward passes.
2. 30 steps × 2 = **60** passes. Cutting steps from 50 to 30 removes 40% of the denoising work.
3. Compare an LLM: a 100-token answer also takes about 100 forward passes, but each one processes a single new token,
   while each image pass processes the entire latent. That’s why an image pass is so much heavier.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">

**What the guidance scale does, in math.** This technique is called **classifier-free guidance**. At each step the
model predicts the noise to remove twice, once with the prompt (ε-conditional) and once without (ε-unconditional),
then combines them:

```
guided prediction = ε_unconditional + s × (ε_conditional − ε_unconditional)
```

The difference (ε_conditional − ε_unconditional) is “the direction the prompt pushes the image.” With a guidance scale
*s* of 1 you get the plain prompted prediction. Larger *s* exaggerates the prompt’s push: more prompt adherence, less
creative freedom, and eventually over-saturated, unnatural images. In most pipelines the **negative prompt** is used in
place of the empty prompt for the unconditional pass, which is how it steers the image *away* from what you describe.

</Note>

### The knobs on every request

The generation process is controlled request by request through **inference arguments**. The most important ones:

| Argument | What it does | Effect on cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Prompt** | Describes what the image should look like | Tiny (one text-encoder pass) |
| **Negative prompt** | Separately describes styles or objects that should *not* be in the image | Usually free: it fills the unconditional pass that runs anyway |
| **Number of steps** | Trades speed against quality; 30 to 50 for most models | Linear: half the steps, about half the denoising time |
| **Guidance scale** | Balances creativity against prompt adherence; generally an integer around 4 | The *scale* is free, but guidance is why each step costs two passes |
| **Image size** | Chosen from a fixed menu of resolutions and aspect ratios | Large: more pixels means a bigger latent, and attention grows with its square |

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Why image size is so expensive.** Doubling an image from 1,024 to 2,048 pixels per side gives 4× as many latent
  positions, so attention in each pass does roughly 4² = **16×** the work, while the linear layers do about 4× the work.
  Models also only produce good results at the resolutions they were trained on, which is why the size comes from a
  fixed menu rather than being any number you like.
</Note>

## What’s inside: diffusion transformers

While these core mechanisms are common to all image generation models, their architecture has evolved a lot. Modern
image models are built on **diffusion transformers**. A diffusion transformer is very similar to the transformers LLMs
use, but instead of processing embeddings of discrete tokens, it **processes image data**.

Diffusion transformers look at images in **patches**. When a text-to-image model is trained, the images in the training
data are fed in as **patches of 2 × 2 or 4 × 4** pixels, which are embedded into latent space. Inference runs in the
opposite direction: the model works in latent space, and the latent is transformed back to pixels once the generation
is final.

### A worked pipeline: SDXL

A clean example of the multi-model pipeline is **Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL)**. It’s an old model, but its architecture
remains relevant. The book’s Figure 2.11 (adapted from the SDXL paper) traces a request through it:

1. The **prompt** goes into the **text encoder**, which produces the conditioning embeddings.
2. A latent full of **random noise** goes into the **base** diffusion model, which denoises it step by step into a
   coherent (but not yet polished) latent image.
3. That latent goes into the **refiner**, a second diffusion model, which adds details and improves prompt adherence.
4. The **VAE decoder** turns the final latent into pixels.

SDXL’s pipeline contains **two diffusion models for denoising**, the base and the refiner, trained for separate tasks:
the base goes from pure noise to a coherent image, while the refiner adds details and makes sure the image follows the
prompt.

<Note kind="beyond">
  Two details of SDXL: it actually uses **two** CLIP-family text encoders whose outputs are combined, and its denoiser
  is a **U-Net** (a convolutional network with transformer attention blocks inside) rather than a pure diffusion
  transformer. Pure diffusion transformers took over in the generations after SDXL, including the Qwen Image family
  below.
</Note>

### How modern models compare

Modern models **substantially outperform SDXL**: better prompt adherence; accurate faces, hands and details; legible
text rendered inside the image; and support for image-to-image inference. Models like the **Qwen Image** family are
broadly similar to the SDXL pipeline, but with **larger and more capable models at every step**:

| Component | SDXL (2023) | Qwen Image (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Text encoder | CLIP-based model | Qwen 2.5 VL (7B) |
| Denoiser | Under 4B parameters | 20B parameters |
| VAE | Single encoding | Dual encoding |

According to the book, the big jump in capability comes from **larger component models and more complex pipelines**:

- **Legible text and photorealistic faces** come from switching from tiny NLP models to **full LLMs** (here a 7B
  vision-language model) for text encoding. A model that really understands language can follow a detailed prompt and
  knows how words are spelled.
- The **denoiser grew about 5×**, from under 4B to 20B parameters.

These larger models take more resources to run. GPUs have become more powerful as models grew, but inference engineers
**can’t rely on hardware gains alone** to run them efficiently.

### Image models that work like LLMs

The latest research direction is **blending diffusion transformer architecture with LLM architecture**. The book’s
reasoning:

- **Anything that can be tokenized can be modeled as an LLM**, and images can be tokenized.
- LLMs have **baked-in text understanding**, and they’re already an important component of image pipelines (as the text
  encoder).
- LLMs solve problems inherent to diffusion. Diffusion models can only produce a **fixed-size output**; LLMs are
  autoregressive and can produce **variable-length output**. And where image models need **up to 100 forward passes** to
  produce an image, an LLM generates each token in a **single forward pass**.

Models like **HunyuanImage-3.0** use this LLM-style architecture.

<Note kind="beyond">
  One pass per token doesn’t automatically mean faster: an image still has to be represented by thousands of tokens,
  and each needs its own pass. The trade-off is different rather than strictly better, which is part of why this is
  still a research frontier rather than the default.
</Note>

## Few-step models: skip most of the steps

The most time-consuming part of image generation is the **30 to 50 denoising steps**. Rather than making each step
faster, what if a model could just use **fewer steps**?

**Few-step image generation models** are trained to do exactly that: create high-resolution images in **eight or fewer
denoising steps**. They’re **80 to 90 percent faster** out of the box than traditional image models, though their output
quality is **noticeably lower**.

There are two main ways to create them:

- **Latent consistency**: train a model to **predict the target latent directly**, then repeat the prediction **two to four
  times** to improve quality.
- **Distillation**: use **adversarial distillation** and/or **progressive distillation** to train a small model to
  emulate a larger one in fewer steps.

**Distillation is more common today.** When new image models like **FLUX** and **Qwen Image** are released, the open
community creates distilled versions alongside quality- and style-oriented LoRAs.

The book’s advice: if you have a **latency-sensitive** use case where **quality is less important**, like real-time
generative filters, consider a few-step model. (Tick the few-step box in the demo above to feel the difference.)

<Worked title="Where “80–90% faster” comes from">

1. Standard model: 50 steps × 2 passes = **100** denoiser passes.
2. Few-step model at 4 steps × 2 passes = **8** passes, which is 92% fewer.
3. The measured end-to-end speedup (80–90%) is a bit smaller than the pass count suggests, because some work doesn’t
   shrink with the step count: running the text encoder once and the VAE decoder once.

</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  Many distilled models also have guidance “baked in” during distillation, so they run **one** pass per step instead of
  two, which roughly halves the remaining denoising cost. Adversarial distillation is the technique behind models like
  SDXL Turbo, and latent consistency models (LCMs) come from the paper of the same name. Both are in the book’s reading
  list.
</Note>

## Video generation: images plus time

Video generation models are **architecturally similar to image generation models, just bigger**. They have **three to
five times more parameters** and encode **ten to one hundred times more information** in latent space.

### Why not just generate frame by frame?

The naive approach is to work frame by frame, and early video generation did exactly that: generate a starting frame,
then use that frame to generate the next one, and so on. The problem is **error accumulation**. Small issues early on
compound with every frame, and the video **goes off the rails quickly**.

Instead, modern video models **hold the entire video in latent space** and modify all of it on each denoising step.
**Each frame attends to every other frame**, and every frame is updated on every forward pass.

If the latent space of an image model represents two physical dimensions, **X and Y**, the latent space of a video model
represents three: **X, Y and T (time)**.

### The limits of video models

- **Fixed length.** Just as image models have fixed aspect ratios, videos have a **fixed number of frames**. Modern video
  models create sequences of **a few seconds**.
- **The constraint is compute.** Even on the latest GPUs, attention over such a massive latent space is extremely
  expensive, taking **several seconds of inference for each second of video**.
- **Batch size of one.** Video models are so compute-intensive that they typically run with a **batch size of one**: a
  **full node of eight GPUs** works on a single request.
- **Same step count.** Although each denoising step is expensive, video models use about the same total number of steps
  as image models, **generally around 50**.

<Worked title="Why “10–100× more information” is so painful">

Attention cost grows with the **square** of the number of latent positions that attend to each other.

1. Suppose an image is ≈ 4,096 latent tokens (see the note on latent space above).
2. A video with 10× the latent information is ≈ 41,000 tokens; with 100× it’s ≈ 410,000 tokens.
3. If every position attends to every other, attention per pass grows by 10² = **100×** up to 100² = **10,000×**.

That squared growth, not the parameter count, is why a single short clip can occupy a whole 8-GPU node.

</Worked>

### Where video is today

Video generation is a recent modality compared with LLMs and image generation. The book observes that **its limitations
line up with the limitations LLMs had about two years earlier**:

| LLMs (late 2023) | Video generation models (late 2025) |
|---|---|
| High time to first token, low tokens per second | Slow generation times |
| Frequent hallucinations | Unrealistic physics |
| Maxed out Ampere GPUs | Max out Blackwell GPUs |
| Limited context windows | Short video outputs |

Today those limitations are mostly gone from LLMs. Removing them from cinematic video generation, and from related areas
like **world models**, **3D object generation** (which works in X, Y and Z rather than X, Y and T), and other generative
rendering models, is a **highly active area of research**.

One important direction comes back around to **autoregressive generation**, just like blending LLM architecture into
image models. Rather than pure frame-by-frame generation, with its unusable error accumulation, new techniques like
**Self Forcing** combine a **global view of quality** with an **iterative approach** to generation. Adding
autoregressive components to video models can **partially address the attention bottleneck**, though attention remains
the most important and most expensive component of inference.

## What this means for inference

Image and video models are relatively small, about **a tenth as many parameters as frontier language models**, but
their attention is **just as computationally demanding**. Like LLM prefill, attention for media has to consider the
**entire image or video at once** in latent space. So image and video inference is **bottlenecked on compute**, not
memory. The [next lesson](/learn/bottlenecks) explains exactly what that means, and
[Serving image & video models](/learn/serving-images-video) covers the specific optimizations for these modalities.

<KeyPoints>
- Image generation is a **pipeline**: text encoder → denoiser → VAE, often plus LoRAs and ControlNets (assembled in tools like ComfyUI).
- Everything happens in **latent space**: 128 × 128 instead of 1,024 × 1,024, about 1% of the values and ~4,096× fewer attention pairs.
- The latent starts as **noise** and the **whole image** is refined over **30–50 steps**; each step is **two forward passes** (with and without the prompt), so 50 steps = 100 passes.
- Request knobs: prompt, negative prompt, steps, guidance scale (≈ 4), image size (fixed menu).
- Modern models (e.g. Qwen Image: 7B VLM text encoder, 20B denoiser) beat SDXL mainly through **bigger components**, especially LLM text encoders.
- **Few-step** models (≤ 8 steps, via latent consistency or distillation) are 80–90% faster with noticeably lower quality.
- **Video** = the same idea over X, Y and T: 3–5× the parameters, 10–100× the latent, all frames attending to all frames, batch size 1 on an 8-GPU node.
- Image and video models are compute-bound.
</KeyPoints>
