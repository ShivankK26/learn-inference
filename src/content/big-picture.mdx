<TLDR>
  A trained AI model is just a big file of numbers. **Inference** is everything it takes to turn that file into a fast,
  cheap, reliable service that answers millions of people. That's what this whole course is about.
</TLDR>

## Two stages in every model's life

Every AI model goes through two stages:

1. **Training**: the model learns. You show it a mountain of data and it slowly adjusts billions of internal numbers
   (called <Term def="The numbers inside a model that were learned during training. A ‘70B’ model has 70 billion of them.">weights</Term>)
   until it gets good at its task. This happens once (or a few times) and costs a fortune.
2. **Inference**: the model *works*. Someone sends a question, and the model uses its weights to produce an answer. This
   happens every time anyone uses the model: millions of times a day, forever.

<Analogy>
  Training is like **going to culinary school**: years of expensive learning, done once. Inference is like **running
  the restaurant**: every night, every order, every table. The skills came from school, but the business lives or dies
  on how well the kitchen runs.
</Analogy>

Old-school machine learning models were small enough that inference was easy: put them on a regular computer and you
were done. Generative AI (chatbots, image generators, voice models) is different. The models are enormous, they need
GPUs, and doing it *well* is a whole engineering discipline. That discipline is **inference engineering**.

## Why anyone cares

The book makes a simple argument: a few years ago it looked like only a handful of labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google)
would ever run big models, and everyone else would just rent them through an API. That's not what happened.

There are now **millions of open models** (models whose weights anyone can download), and the best ones are close to
the best closed models. That means any company can run its own model, and once you run your own, you can make it better
in three ways that matter a lot:

| What you can improve | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Speed** (latency) | Voice agents and coding tools feel broken if they're slow. |
| **Uptime** (availability) | Your own deployment can stay up far more reliably than a shared public API. |
| **Cost** | Running open models yourself can be dramatically cheaper at scale. |

Getting those wins takes skill, which is why inference engineering is a young, fast-growing field.

## The three layers

The book organizes everything into three layers that stack on top of each other. Click through them:

<ThreeLayers />

A few things worth noticing:

- **Runtime** is about *one* machine. It's the most technical layer, full of GPU tricks. Chapters 2–6 are mostly about this.
- **Infrastructure** is about *many* machines. No matter how fast one server is, enough traffic will swamp it, so you
  need more copies of the model and a smart way to spread the work. As you grow, the problem changes: first it's
  "add servers fast enough", then it's "find enough GPUs at all", and eventually it's "treat GPUs in many clouds as one
  big pool".
- **Tooling** is about *people*. The best systems give engineers enough control to fix things without drowning them in
  details.

## The map of this course

Here's how the chapters fit together, in plain words:

- **Ch 1 · Before you optimize**: figure out what your app actually needs (speed? cost? quality?) and pick a model.
- **Ch 2 · How models work**: what's happening inside the model, and *where it gets slow*. (The most important chapter.)
- **Ch 3 · Hardware**: what's inside a GPU and how to choose one.
- **Ch 4 · Software**: the layers of software that run the model, from CUDA up to inference engines like vLLM.
- **Ch 5 · Techniques**: the big speed-up tricks, in depth.
- **Ch 6 · Beyond text**: how images, speech, and video models differ.
- **Ch 7 · Production**: running all of this for real users, reliably.

<KeyPoints>
- **Training** = learning (once, expensive). **Inference** = using the model to answer (constantly, forever).
- Inference engineering makes models **faster, cheaper, and more reliable** without making them dumber.
- Three layers: **runtime** (one machine), **infrastructure** (many machines), **tooling** (the people using it).
- Six recurring tricks: batching, caching, quantization, speculation, parallelism, disaggregation.
</KeyPoints>
