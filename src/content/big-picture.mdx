<TLDR>
  A trained AI model is just a big file of numbers. **Inference** is everything it takes to turn that file into a fast,
  cheap, reliable service that answers millions of people. Doing that well takes three layers of work (**runtime**,
  **infrastructure**, and **tooling**) and a toolbox of six recurring techniques. That’s what this whole course is about.
</TLDR>

## Two stages in every model’s life

Every generative AI model goes through two stages:

1. **Training**: the process of *learning the model’s weights from data*. You show the model a mountain of examples and
   it slowly adjusts billions of internal numbers (called
   <Term def="The numbers inside a model that were learned during training. A ‘70B’ model has 70 billion of them.">weights</Term>)
   until it gets good at its task. This happens once (or a few times) and costs a fortune.
2. **Inference**: *serving the model in production*. Someone sends a request, and the model uses its weights to produce
   an answer. This happens every time anyone uses the model: millions of times a day, for as long as the model is in
   use.

<Analogy>
  Training is like **going to culinary school**: years of expensive learning, done once. Inference is like **running
  the restaurant**: every night, every order, every table. The skills came from school, but the business lives or dies
  on how well the kitchen runs.
</Analogy>

### Why generative AI made inference hard

During the machine learning boom of the 2010s, hundreds of thousands of data scientists and ML engineers got used to
the full lifecycle, training *and* inference, for **classic ML models**. For those models, inference was relatively
simple. The book recalls that in the early days of Baseten (the author’s company), they ran inference for models built
with tools like **XGBoost** on lightweight **CPUs** with a simple software stack.

<Note kind="beyond">
  XGBoost is a popular library for *gradient-boosted decision trees*, a classic ML technique for predictions like
  “will this customer churn?” or “is this transaction fraud?” These models are typically small and answer quickly on an
  ordinary CPU. A modern LLM can be hundreds of gigabytes and needs GPUs, which is the whole reason a new discipline
  was needed.
</Note>

Generative AI (chatbots, image generators, voice models) is a different world. The book is blunt about it: **you can’t
simply take model weights, get some GPUs, and expect inference to be fast and reliable enough for large-scale
production use.** The models are enormous, they need specialized hardware and software, and small inefficiencies
multiply across millions of requests. Doing it *well* is its own engineering discipline: **inference engineering**.

## Why inference matters so much right now

The book opens (in its preface) with a strong claim: **inference is the most valuable category in the AI industry**,
yet **inference engineering is still in its infancy**. Inference engineers work *across the whole stack*, from
<Term def="NVIDIA’s platform for writing programs that run on its GPUs. Covered in Chapter 4.">CUDA</Term> (code
running on the GPU) all the way up to
<Term def="An open-source system for running and managing containers across a cluster of machines. Covered in Chapter 7.">Kubernetes</Term>
(managing fleets of machines), chasing one goal: faster, less expensive, more reliable serving of generative AI models
in production.

### The world in 2022 vs. today

On **November 30, 2022**, the day ChatGPT launched, there were perhaps **a few hundred** inference engineers in the
world, though nobody called them that. They mostly worked at **frontier labs** (OpenAI, Midjourney, Anthropic) or at
big tech companies (Google, NVIDIA).

Back then, one future looked plausible: training generative models might be *so* hard and *so* expensive that only a
handful of companies would ever build them. Everyone else would just be customers, **“renting intelligence a token at
a time”** through APIs, and only those few labs would ever need inference engineers.

That’s not what happened. Three years later, training turned out to be hard and expensive, **but not so hard or so
expensive that only a handful of players could do it**. Instead there’s been a “Cambrian explosion” of **open
models**:

- There are now **more than two million** open models on Hugging Face (the largest repository of open models). The
  preface’s first figure shows this: about **25 times more models than five years earlier**.
- Research labs around the world regularly release open models of every modality: **OpenAI** and **NVIDIA Nemotron**
  in America; **Mistral AI** and **Black Forest Labs** in Europe; **Alibaba Qwen**, **DeepSeek AI**, **Z AI**, and
  **Moonshot AI** in China.

### Open vs. closed models

Models differ in whether their weights are available to you:

| | Closed model | Open model |
|---|---|---|
| **Weights** | Proprietary, not available to the public | Publicly downloadable |
| **Examples** | GPT-5, Claude Sonnet | Llama, DeepSeek |
| **How you use it** | Only through the owner’s API | Run it anywhere you like, including on your own GPUs |
| **License** | Owner’s terms of service | Usually MIT or a similar permissive license, but **some restrict commercial use, so always check** |

### The quality gap closed

Until **December 2024**, there was a meaningful intelligence gap between the best closed and the best open models.
When **DeepSeek V3** and **R1** were released, that gap disappeared. Today, new closed models are typically matched by
open models **within months, if not weeks**, and occasionally an open model (the book cites **Kimi K2 Thinking**)
even beats the closed models for a brief window.

### Why that matters: capability thresholds

The preface’s second figure shows both closed and open models improving rapidly over time, and it frames progress in
terms of **capability thresholds**: levels of intelligence at which a new kind of product becomes possible. Even though
open models are usually *chasing* closed ones on benchmarks, crossing a threshold is what changes things for product
builders.

Here’s the sequence the book describes, step by step:

1. **In 2022**, it was impossible to build the kinds of AI-native products that define the industry today.
2. **Closed models got smarter first** and crossed thresholds that made new categories possible, like **customer
   service voice agents** and **AI-powered code editors (IDEs)**. These early models were slow, expensive, and
   unreliable, but the capability was there, so engineers started building companies around them.
3. **Open models then crossed the same thresholds**, and product builders began swapping them in to **replace closed
   models**.
4. Many teams went further and **fine-tuned open models**, which let them cross thresholds *faster* and even **exceed
   closed-model quality for their specific product and domain**.

The preface’s third figure sums up the payoff: customizing open models unlocks new capabilities **while keeping
control over latency, reliability, and economics.**

### Three dimensions you can improve

Once you run open models yourself, inference engineering can make your product better in three ways that closed APIs
don’t let you control:

| Dimension | The book’s point |
|---|---|
| **Latency** | Closed-model APIs are built for *throughput* (serving huge numbers of customers efficiently). Open models can be optimized for *real-time* applications. |
| **Availability** | APIs for GPT and Claude are “stuck at **two nines** of uptime.” With dedicated deployments of open models, **four nines or better** is achievable. |
| **Cost** | Open models are often **at least 80% less expensive** at scale. |

<Worked title="What “two nines” vs. “four nines” actually means">
  Uptime is quoted in “nines”: the number of 9s in the percentage of time a service is working.

  1. A month has about 30.4 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes ≈ **43,800 minutes**.
  2. **Two nines** = 99% uptime, so 1% downtime: 43,800 × 0.01 ≈ **438 minutes ≈ 7.3 hours of downtime per month**.
  3. **Four nines** = 99.99% uptime, so 0.01% downtime: 43,800 × 0.0001 ≈ **4.4 minutes of downtime per month**.

  That’s a **100× difference**. For a product in someone’s revenue path (a support agent, a coding tool), seven hours a
  month of outages versus four minutes is the difference between an annoyance and a dealbreaker.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The arithmetic above is standard reliability math, not from the book. Also note what “80% less expensive” means:
  paying $20 for work that used to cost $100. That’s a 5× reduction in cost, not a 20% saving.
</Note>

### Who is doing this

The book’s point is that where inference engineering once looked like a niche field, **every company that wants truly
differentiated, competitive AI products now needs an inference strategy**. It lists examples across the market:

- **AI-native startups** like **Cursor**, **Clay**, **Gamma**, and **Mercor**, growing extremely fast on products that
  rely on open and in-house models.
- **Digital-native companies** like **Notion** and **Superhuman**, deeply integrating AI into products hundreds of
  millions of people already use.
- **Blended research-and-engineering teams** like **World Labs**, **Writer**, and **Mirage**, training and productizing
  their own foundation models.
- **Enterprise and regulated industries**, historically slow adopters: companies like **OpenEvidence**, **Abridge**,
  and **Ambience** are making generative AI ubiquitous in **healthcare**, and at the world’s largest companies AI
  projects are moving past the pilot stage into mass adoption.

The author writes from four years at Baseten, an inference provider serving the companies listed above. The book is
based on interviews with dozens of experts on that engineering team, conference talks (NVIDIA GTC, PyTorch Conference,
AWS re:Invent, AI Engineer World’s Fair), and many conversations with customers and builders.

<Note kind="tip">
  The preface’s message to readers is encouraging: **you are early.** Relatively few people work on inference, the
  space is young, and **newcomers can become experts quickly**. There are novel, deeply technical problems at every
  level of the stack.
</Note>

## The three layers of inference

Doing inference well requires **three layers** that must work together to handle mission-critical inference at scale.

### Layer 1: Runtime (one machine)

**Definition:** optimizing the performance of **a single model on a single GPU-backed instance**. (An
<Term def="A rented cloud machine: one or more GPUs plus CPU, RAM, storage, and networking. Covered in Chapter 3.">instance</Term>
may contain several GPUs; runtime still means getting the most out of that one box.)

The runtime layer depends on a sophisticated **software stack**, from bottom to top:

- **CUDA**: NVIDIA’s platform for programming GPUs.
- **PyTorch**: the standard framework for building and running neural networks.
- **Inference engines** like **vLLM**, **SGLang**, and **TensorRT-LLM**: specialized servers that run models fast.

Low-level optimization matters a lot here. The book singles out
<Term def="Small programs that run on the GPU. FlashAttention is a family of highly optimized kernels for computing attention (Chapter 2).">kernels</Term>
like **FlashAttention**, which deliver significant performance gains on their own.

### The six runtime techniques

The runtime layer relies on a set of **model performance techniques** that adapt new research to the unique
challenges of inference for generative models. You’ll spend most of Chapters 2–5 on them, so here are the book’s own
definitions, expanded:

| Technique | What it does | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Batching** | Runs incoming requests in parallel, **weaving them together on a token-by-token basis** | One pass over the model’s weights serves many users, so **throughput** rises |
| **Caching** | **Reuses the KV cache** (the saved results of the attention calculation) between requests that **share the same prefix** | Work already done for an identical start of a prompt isn’t repeated |
| **Quantization** | **Lowers the precision** of *select pieces* of the model | Smaller numbers let you **access more compute** and **reduce the memory burden** |
| **Speculation** | **Generates and then validates draft tokens** | Produces **more than one token per forward pass** during decode, cutting latency |
| **Parallelism** | **Efficiently uses more than one GPU** for large models | Speeds up big models **without introducing new bottlenecks** |
| **Disaggregation** | **Separates the two phases** of LLM inference, *prefill* and *decode*, onto **independently scaling workers** | Each phase gets hardware and scaling suited to its very different needs |

Don’t worry if terms like *KV cache*, *forward pass*, *prefill*, and *decode* are new. Chapter 2 builds each of them from
scratch. For now, get familiar with the names:

<SixTricks />

<Note kind="beyond">
  A useful way to hold all six in your head is by **what each one attacks**:
  - **Batching** and **speculation** both get more useful work out of each pass through the model’s weights. Batching
    shares one pass across many users (more throughput); speculation squeezes several tokens out of one pass for a
    single user (lower latency).
  - **Caching** avoids repeating work entirely.
  - **Quantization** shrinks the numbers, so there’s less data to move and more math the GPU can do per second.
  - **Parallelism** adds more GPUs to one model when one isn’t big or fast enough.
  - **Disaggregation** recognizes that the two phases of an LLM request stress the GPU in opposite ways (one is limited
    by math, the other by memory), so it gives each phase its own workers.

  Chapter 2’s bottlenecks lesson explains the “math vs. memory” distinction that makes this grouping click.
</Note>

These techniques aren’t just for chatbots. They apply to **models of every modality**: vision language models,
embedding models, automatic speech recognition, speech synthesis, image generation, and video generation. Each of those
modalities also needs its own specific optimizations, which is what Chapter 6 covers.

### Layer 2: Infrastructure (many machines)

Runtime optimizations **are not enough**. No matter how performant a single instance of a model server is, **it will
eventually receive more traffic than it can handle.** The book stresses that this isn’t a CUDA problem or a PyTorch
problem. It’s a **systems problem**, solved at the infrastructure layer.

<Worked title="Why one instance eventually isn’t enough">
  Imagine one well-optimized model server that can stream answers to **50 users at the same time** without slowing
  anyone down.

  1. Your app has **2,000 users active at the daily peak**. You need at least 2,000 ÷ 50 = **40 copies** (replicas) of
     the server at that moment. No amount of kernel tuning turns 50 into 2,000.
  2. At **3 a.m.**, only 150 users are active: 150 ÷ 50 = **3 replicas** would do. Keeping 40 running all night means
     paying for 37 idle servers.
  3. So you need a system that **adds replicas as traffic rises and removes them as it falls**, and does it fast enough
     that users don’t wait while new servers boot. That’s autoscaling: the first infrastructure problem.
  4. Now grow tenfold: 400 replicas. Suddenly the question isn’t *when* to add servers but *whether the cloud has any
     GPUs left to give you*. That’s the capacity problem, the second stage.
</Worked>

<Note kind="beyond">
  The numbers in this worked example are illustrative, not from the book. Chapter 7 covers how real autoscalers decide
  when to add and remove replicas, and why booting a new replica (a “cold start”) can take minutes.
</Note>

The crucial insight is that **the nature of infrastructure problems changes with each level of scale**:

1. **First, autoscaling.** At small scale, the questions are *when* to add and remove
   <Term def="One running copy of the model server. More replicas means more capacity.">replicas</Term>, and *how to do
   it quickly*.
2. **Then, capacity.** Past a certain scale, **generally a few hundred GPUs**, the problem becomes simply *getting
   access to enough GPUs*. Inference engineers start spreading workloads across **multiple regions** and **multiple
   cloud providers**.
3. **Which creates silos.** Spreading out quickly leads to fragmentation: models in one cluster can be **starved for
   resources** while other clusters sit on **unused capacity**.
4. **Finally, a unified global system.** The last level of scale is infrastructure that treats **all available
   resources as a single unified pool of compute**, placing work wherever capacity exists.

Chapter 0’s other figures illustrate both halves: one shows a runtime optimization (speculation) improving latency, and
another shows serving at scale by **unifying capacity across multiple cloud service providers**.

Thoughtful multi-cloud infrastructure brings two more benefits beyond raw capacity:

- **Reliability**: spreading across regions and providers protects you against downtime in any single region or cloud.
- **Lower end-to-end latency**: for global applications, running inference **near your users** cuts the time requests
  spend traveling over the network.

<Analogy title="Think of it like a restaurant chain">
  Autoscaling is adding more cooks during the dinner rush. Capacity is discovering you can’t hire enough cooks in one
  city and opening kitchens in other cities. Silos are when one kitchen has a two-hour wait while another across town
  is empty. The unified system is a central dispatcher that sends every order to whichever kitchen can cook it fastest.
</Analogy>

### Layer 3: Tooling (the people using it)

Once runtime and infrastructure capabilities exist, they have to be presented to engineers at the **right level of
abstraction**. This applies both to **inference providers** (like Baseten) and to **internal teams** building
inference inside a company: both must decide what tooling and **developer experience** to offer.

The book admits developer experience is subjective, and frames it as a spectrum:

- **One extreme: the black box.** Give the platform model weights, get back an API. Easy, but you can’t fix or tune
  anything when it matters.
- **The other extreme: raw building blocks.** Provide only basic constructs for compute, network, disk, and so on. Full
  control, but engineers drown in details and get little done.
- **The right answer is in the middle**: enough **control** to run mission-critical inference confidently, with enough
  **abstraction** to work productively.

<KeyPoints>
- **Training** learns the weights (once, expensive). **Inference** serves the model in production (constantly).
- Classic ML inference was simple (small models, CPUs). Generative AI inference needs specialized hardware, software,
  and systems work.
- Open models (2M+ on Hugging Face) caught up with closed ones after DeepSeek V3/R1 (Dec 2024). Running them yourself
  lets you control **latency**, **availability** (four nines vs. two), and **cost** (often 80%+ cheaper).
- Three layers: **runtime** (one model, one instance), **infrastructure** (autoscaling → capacity → silos → one global
  pool), **tooling** (the right level of abstraction, between black box and raw building blocks).
- Six runtime techniques: **batching, caching, quantization, speculation, parallelism, disaggregation**. They apply to
  every modality.
</KeyPoints>
