<TLDR>
  A neural network is a huge stack of very simple math steps: *multiply, add, squish*, repeated billions of times. You
  don't need the math. You need the intuition that it's **mostly multiplying big grids of numbers**, because that's
  what GPUs are good at.
</TLDR>

## Why an inference engineer needs this

Your job is to make models faster *without making them worse*. You can't do either if the model is a total black box.
The good news is you only need a working picture, not a PhD.

A tiny bit of history for context: neural networks date back to the 1950s, got much deeper in the 2000s and 2010s, and
then in **2017** a paper called *"Attention Is All You Need"* introduced the **transformer**. Transformers power nearly
every generative AI model today: chatbots, image generators, voice models, all of it.

Transformers generate things in one of two styles:

- **One piece at a time** (<Term def="Generating output one step at a time, where each new piece depends on all the pieces before it.">autoregressive</Term>):
  predict the next token, add it, repeat. This is how **LLMs** write text.
- **Refine the whole thing** (<Term def="Starting from random noise and gradually cleaning it up into an image or video.">denoising / diffusion</Term>):
  start with static and clean it up step by step. This is how most **image and video** models work.

## The smallest building block: a neuron

A **neuron** (also called a *node*) is a tiny calculation. It takes a number in, multiplies it by a **weight**, adds a
**bias**, and passes the result on. The weight and bias are the numbers the model learned during training.

<Neuron />

That's genuinely it. A single neuron is trivial. The power comes from having *billions* of them, organized in layers.

## Layers

Neurons are grouped into **layers**, and each layer passes its output to the next:

- **Input layer**: takes in the raw input (e.g. your words, converted to numbers).
- **Hidden layers**: the middle layers that gradually transform the input. Their in-between outputs are called
  <Term def="The intermediate output of a hidden layer: a list of numbers representing what the model ‘thinks’ at that point.">hidden states</Term>.
- **Output layer**: produces the final prediction.

LLMs have dozens to hundreds of layers.

<Analogy title="Think of it like an assembly line">
  Each station (layer) takes what the previous station made, does one small transformation, and passes it along. No
  single station builds the car. The car emerges from the whole line.
</Analogy>

## The one operation that matters: matmul

Doing each neuron one at a time would be slow. Instead, a whole layer is done at once as a **matrix multiplication**,
or **matmul**: multiply a list of numbers (a *vector*) by a grid of numbers (a *matrix*, which holds the weights), then
add the biases.

```
output = input × Weights + bias
```

This is the heartbeat of every AI model. When people say GPUs are good at AI, what they really mean is that **GPUs are
extremely good at matmul**. Keep this in your head: most of inference is just matmul, over and over.

## Why we need a "squish" step (activation functions)

There's a catch. If you multiply by one matrix and then another, that's mathematically the same as multiplying by a
single combined matrix. So a stack of 100 plain matmul layers would collapse into **one** layer. All that depth would
be wasted.

The fix is to put an **activation function** between layers: a small non-linear "squish" that breaks the collapse. The
classic one is **ReLU**: *if the number is negative, make it zero; otherwise keep it.* (Try the checkbox in the neuron
demo above.) Modern models use fancier cousins with names like SiLU, Swish, and SwiGLU, but they all do roughly the
same thing.

## Encoders and decoders

Two more words you'll see everywhere:

- An **encoder** reads an input (text, an image, audio) and turns it into a rich internal representation.
- A **decoder** uses a representation to *generate* an output (text, an image).

Networks can be snapped together like Lego. **Modern LLMs are decoder-only.** Some other models use both: for example,
**Whisper** (a popular speech-to-text model) uses an encoder to listen to the audio and a decoder to write out the text.

<KeyPoints>
- A neuron = **multiply by a weight, add a bias**. Layers of neurons = a neural network.
- A whole layer runs as one **matmul** (matrix multiplication), and matmul is most of what a GPU does during inference.
- **Activation functions** (like ReLU) sit between layers so that depth actually adds power.
- **Encoders** understand inputs; **decoders** generate outputs. Today's LLMs are decoder-only.
</KeyPoints>
