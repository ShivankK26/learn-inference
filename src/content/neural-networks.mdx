<TLDR>
  A neural network is a huge stack of very simple math steps: *multiply, add, squish*, repeated billions of times. The
  operation that dominates all of it is **matrix multiplication**, which is exactly what GPUs are built to do. Understand
  that, and most of inference engineering starts to make sense.
</TLDR>

## Why an inference engineer needs to know how models work

The book defines inference engineering as making generative AI models **faster, less expensive, and more reliable,
without sacrificing the quality that makes them valuable**. Both halves of that job depend on knowing what’s inside the
model:

- To make a model **faster**, you need to know which parts do the most work and what they’re waiting on.
- To avoid making it **worse**, you need to know which parts are fragile and which can be simplified safely.

Every generative AI model, whether it writes text, draws pictures, or speaks, is built by composing big, complex
**neural networks**. This lesson builds up the vocabulary for them from nothing. You don’t need a PhD, but you do need
a working mental model, and that’s what we’ll build here.

## A short history, and why it matters

The ideas are older than most people think:

| When | What happened | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1950s | The first **perceptrons**, built in hardware, did simple yes/no classification. | The basic unit: weighted inputs, one output. |
| Later decades | Perceptrons were abandoned, then reinvented as **multi-layer perceptrons** trained with **back-propagation**. | Back-propagation introduced *hidden states* between layers and a procedure that repeatedly nudges the weights. |
| 2000s | Research into **deep** networks with dozens of layers. | More layers meant more capacity. |
| 2012 | **AlexNet** became the first deep network to show strong real-world results, and showed that **GPUs** were the right hardware for deep learning. | This led to word-embedding models for text and GANs (generative adversarial networks) for images. |
| 2017 | Vaswani and colleagues published *“Attention Is All You Need,”* introducing the **transformer**. | The foundation of all modern generative AI. |

A **transformer** is a neural network with an **attention mechanism** that can learn relationships between different
parts of a sequence (for example, which earlier word a pronoun refers to). Transformers aren’t just for chatbots: they
power every modality, from embedding models to voice to image and video generation.

### The two styles of generative model

Across all modalities, transformer-based models come in two important styles:

- **Autoregressive token generation**: start from a tokenized sequence and predict the most likely **next** token, add
  it, and repeat. This is how **LLMs** work, and it’s covered in the next two lessons.
- **Iterative denoising**: start from **random noise** and refine it, step by step, toward the most likely output. This
  process is called **diffusion**, and it’s how most **image and video** models work (covered in the image lesson).

These two styles have very different performance characteristics, so it’s worth keeping them separate in your head
from the start.

## The smallest building block: a node

The fundamental unit of a neural network is a **node** (also called a **neuron**). The book describes it as a short
program that:

1. takes an input,
2. multiplies it by some **weights**,
3. adds a **bias**,
4. and returns the result.

The weights and bias are numbers that were **learned during training**. At inference time they’re fixed; the node just
applies them. Try one:

## Layers and hidden states

Nodes are grouped into **layers**. Two facts about layers matter a lot for performance:

- **Nodes within a layer are independent.** Each does its own calculation without waiting for its neighbors.
- **The connections run between layers.** Every node in a layer receives the output of the previous layer.

Because the nodes inside a layer don’t depend on each other, they can all be computed **at the same time**. That’s the
first hint of why massively parallel hardware like GPUs fits neural networks so well.

The networks behind LLMs have **dozens to hundreds of layers**, of three types:

- **Input layer**: the first layer, which accepts and processes the input.
- **Hidden layers**: every layer between the first and the last. They transform the input step by step toward an
  answer.
- **Output layer**: the last layer, which returns the network’s prediction.

The book’s Figure 2.1 shows this shape: one input layer on the left, a stack of hidden layers in the middle, and one
output layer on the right, with lines connecting every node in one layer to every node in the next.

Each layer’s output becomes the next layer’s input. For the hidden layers, these outputs are called **hidden states**.

<Analogy title="Think of it like an assembly line">
  Each station (layer) takes what the previous station produced, does one transformation, and passes it on. Every worker
  at a station works in parallel on their own part. No single station builds the car; the car emerges from the whole
  line.
</Analogy>

### Internal representations and dimensionality

Hidden states are one kind of **internal representation**: the model’s own numerical way of describing its input. A
key property of any representation is its **dimensionality**, meaning how many numbers are in each vector.

Here’s a detail that surprises people: text and images go in **opposite** directions.

- **Text gets bigger.** A chunk of text (a token) is expanded into a vector of **hundreds or thousands** of numbers, so
  there’s room to capture its meaning and its relationships with other words.
- **Images get smaller.** A picture with **millions** of pixels is compressed down to a manageable size, because
  working on every pixel would be far too expensive.

<Note kind="beyond">
  For a sense of scale: Llama 3 8B represents every token as a vector of 4,096 numbers. A 1024 × 1024 color image has
  1024 × 1024 × 3 ≈ 3.1 million values, which image models compress into a much smaller latent grid (the image lesson
  goes into this).
</Note>

### Encoders and decoders

Some networks **create** internal representations and some **use** them:

- An **encoder** takes an input like text or an image and produces an internal representation enriched with extra
  information and semantic meaning.
- A **decoder** uses an internal representation to **generate** an output like text or an image.

Neural networks are **composable**: you can combine several into one model, or run them one after another as a
**pipeline**. Three patterns show up in practice:

| Pattern | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Decoder-only | Modern LLMs (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek…) | The standard design for text generation today. |
| Encoder-only | BERT-family text embedding models | Somewhat rare today; the best-known example is older embedding models. |
| Encoder-decoder | Whisper (speech-to-text) | An encoder processes the audio; a decoder writes the text tokens. Common in non-text modalities. |

## The one operation that matters: matrix multiplication

Doing nodes one at a time would be hopelessly slow. Instead, a whole layer is computed at once as a **matrix
multiplication**, or **matmul**. The book calls it the most essential operation in a neural network.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **Vectors and matrices, quickly.** A *vector* is a list of numbers, like `[1, 2]`. A *matrix* is a grid of numbers
  with rows and columns. Multiplying a vector by a matrix produces a new vector: each output number is the input list
  multiplied, position by position, against one column of the matrix, with the products added up. If the input has
  *d_in* numbers and the matrix has *d_in* rows and *d_out* columns, the output has *d_out* numbers.
</Note>

The simplest version of this inside a network is a **linear layer**. Given an input vector, it applies a **weight
matrix** and adds a **bias vector**. This is the book’s Figure 2.2:

```
y = x·W + b

x : input vector
W : weight matrix (learned in training)
b : bias vector   (learned in training)
y : output vector
```

<Worked title="A linear layer by hand">
  Take a 2-number input and a layer with 3 outputs:

  ```
  x = [1, 2]

  W = [ 3   0   1 ]      b = [0.5, 0, 1]
      [ 1   2  -1 ]
  ```

  Each output is the input multiplied against one column of W, plus that output’s bias:

  1. First output: 1×3 + 2×1 + 0.5 = **5.5**
  2. Second output: 1×0 + 2×2 + 0 = **4**
  3. Third output: 1×1 + 2×(−1) + 1 = **0**

  So `y = [5.5, 4, 0]`. That took 6 multiplications (2 inputs × 3 outputs). Every single weight was used exactly once.
</Worked>

That last observation is worth holding on to. In a real model, a linear layer might have 4,096 inputs and 14,336
outputs, which is 4,096 × 14,336 ≈ **58.7 million weights**, and **every one of them is used for every token**. The
weights of any single linear layer are still only a small part of the model’s total; a large model has hundreds of
these matrices. All of their values were set during training.

<Note kind="beyond">
  A common rule of thumb follows directly from this: each weight contributes one multiply and one add per token, so a
  model does roughly **2 floating-point operations per parameter per token**. An 8-billion-parameter model does about
  16 billion operations to process one token. You’ll use this idea in the bottlenecks lesson.
</Note>

## Why we need a “squish” step: activation functions

Matrix multiplication has a mathematical property that causes a problem: it’s **composable**. Multiplying a vector by
two matrices in a row gives exactly the same answer as multiplying it by the single matrix you get from multiplying
those two matrices together. The book’s Figure 2.3 shows two layer equations collapsing into one:

```
layer 1:  h = x·W₁
layer 2:  y = h·W₂

substitute:  y = (x·W₁)·W₂ = x·(W₁·W₂) = x·W
```

The same thing happens with biases included: `(x·W₁ + b₁)·W₂ + b₂` rearranges to `x·(W₁·W₂) + (b₁·W₂ + b₂)`, which is
again one weight matrix plus one bias. So a stack of 100 plain linear layers would **collapse into a single layer**,
and all that depth would buy you nothing.

Depth is the whole point. The book notes that deep networks are useful because **more layers use more parameters
effectively and encode more meaning in hidden states**.

The fix is to put an **activation function** between layers. To do its job, an activation function must be:

- **Non-linear**, so that consecutive matmuls can’t merge into one.
- **Differentiable, or mostly differentiable**, so that back-propagation can still train the network.

The classic example is **ReLU** (Rectified Linear Unit): *if the input is greater than zero, return it; otherwise
return zero.* The book’s Figure 2.4 shows its shape: flat at zero for negative inputs, then a straight 45° line for
positive inputs. Tick the ReLU checkbox in the neuron demo above to see it in action.

<Worked title="Why ReLU breaks the collapse">
  A purely linear function (no bias) always treats negative inputs as mirror images of positive ones: whatever it does
  to 2, it does the negative of to −2.

  1. ReLU(2) = **2**
  2. ReLU(−2) = **0**, not −2

  That lopsidedness is the non-linearity. Once it sits between two matmuls, there is no single matrix that reproduces
  the combined result for every input, so the layers stay separate and depth adds real power.
</Worked>

There are dozens of activation functions. One is named **Swish** because its curve resembles the Nike logo. Most
follow the same general pattern as ReLU: map negative values to zero or near-zero, and keep positive values roughly
unchanged. The ones you’ll see in modern models are **ReLU, SiLU, Swish, and SwiGLU**, and they share three useful
properties:

1. **Fast to run**: a few simple operations per number.
2. **Easy to train with**: they’re mostly differentiable, so they have a gradient for most input values.
3. **They break linearity**, which is what makes multi-layer networks work.

<Note kind="beyond">
  **What “differentiable” buys you.** Training works by measuring how wrong the model’s output was, then working out,
  for every weight, which direction to nudge it to be a little less wrong. That direction comes from the *gradient*
  (the slope) of each step in the network, and back-propagation computes it layer by layer from the output back to the
  input. A function needs a slope almost everywhere for this to work. ReLU has one everywhere except exactly at zero,
  which is good enough in practice.
</Note>

## Putting it together

Here’s the picture you should now have of any neural network:

1. The input is turned into a vector of numbers.
2. Each layer multiplies that vector by a weight matrix, adds a bias, and applies an activation function.
3. Because the nodes in a layer are independent, the whole layer is one big matmul that can be computed in parallel.
4. Dozens to hundreds of these layers are stacked, each producing a hidden state for the next.
5. The output layer turns the final hidden state into a prediction.

Almost all of the arithmetic is **matmul**. When people say GPUs are good at AI, what they mean is that GPUs can do an
enormous number of multiply-and-add operations in parallel. The rest of the course is largely about keeping that
hardware fed and busy.

<KeyPoints>
- Inference engineering = **faster, cheaper, more reliable** without losing quality, which requires knowing the model’s internals.
- The **transformer** (2017) underpins every modality. Two styles: **autoregressive** (LLMs) and **iterative denoising** (images, video).
- A **node** multiplies inputs by weights and adds a bias. Nodes in a layer are **independent**, so a layer runs as one parallel **matmul**: `y = x·W + b`.
- Hidden layers produce **hidden states**. Text representations **expand** to thousands of dimensions; image representations **compress** millions of pixels.
- **Encoders** build representations, **decoders** generate from them. LLMs are decoder-only; Whisper is encoder-decoder; BERT-style embedders are encoder-only.
- Stacked linear layers **collapse** into one. **Activation functions** (ReLU, SiLU, Swish, SwiGLU) prevent that and must be mostly differentiable for training.
</KeyPoints>
